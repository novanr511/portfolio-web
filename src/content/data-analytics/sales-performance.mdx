## The question

A retail manager does not need another chart of total revenue. The useful question is where margin leaks, and whether discounting is buying the volume it claims to buy. This demo treats a public retail transaction table as a decision brief, then states what the data cannot settle.

## Dataset and source

The [Sample Superstore dataset](https://github.com/yashbhanu/EDA--Exploratory-Data-Analysis--SampleSuperstore/blob/main/SampleSuperstore.csv) contains 9,994 order lines with sales, quantity, discount, profit, region, category, and segment. This mirror omits the `Order Date` and `Customer ID` columns present in the canonical Superstore file, so there is no time dimension and no customer identifier in the data used here. It is a public teaching dataset, not a private company file. Every figure below is computed by `scripts/build_data_analytics_demos.py` from that CSV.

## Findings

Total sales are **USD 2,297,200.86** and total profit is **USD 286,397.02**, a margin of **12.47%**. The Consumer segment contributes the most revenue at **USD 1,161,401.34**. Technology is the most profitable category, while Furniture contributes the least profit at **USD 18,451.27** despite being a major revenue line.

Grouping order lines by discount band shows aggregate margin falling from **29.51%** at no discount, to **12.75%** at 1% to 15%, **9.21%** at 16% to 30%, then turning negative at **-24.80%** for 31% to 50% and **-119.20%** above 50%. **18.72%** of order lines carry a negative profit. The 51%+ band is not a thin tail: it holds **856 order lines, all loss-making**, and the five largest losses account for **25.17%** of that band's loss.

The discount-band association is far stronger than the category or segment association. Measured by eta-squared, discount band accounts for **0.757** of the variation in row margin against **0.0084** for category and **0.0006** for segment. That is a descriptive comparison of grouping strength, not a causal estimate.

## What a manager would do with it

Treat discount bands as the first place to investigate margin leakage, then run an elasticity test before changing policy. A fuller analysis would need order identifiers and a time dimension to know how many discounted lines would have occurred at full price. Without that counterfactual, a discount ceiling is a hypothesis, not a recommendation from this table.

## Limitations

This is a public teaching dataset with no customer identifiers and no time dimension in the cited mirror. `Profit` is a supplied derived field, not a measured cost, and `Sales` is net of discount, so part of the **-0.86** correlation between discount and row margin is mechanical rather than behavioural. The margin by discount band is an aggregate ratio, sensitive to how lines are grouped, and it does not identify a causal effect. No seasonality, elasticity, or outcome after any decision is observed. Nothing here is evidence about a real company.
