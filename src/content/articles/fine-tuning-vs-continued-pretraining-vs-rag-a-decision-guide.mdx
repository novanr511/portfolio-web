Teams often ask which adaptation method is best, as if the three were competing products. They solve different problems: retrieval gives the model information, fine-tuning teaches it a behaviour or format, and continued pretraining teaches it a domain. Choosing the wrong one produces an expensive system that still fails.

**Direct answer:** Use RAG when the answer depends on information that can be retrieved and changes; use fine-tuning when the task is stable but the model's behaviour or output format needs teaching; use continued pretraining when the domain's language and concepts are unfamiliar. The RAG article, the LoRA article, and the domain-adaptation article cover the mechanisms. The boundary: many real systems need more than one — retrieval for facts, fine-tuning for behaviour — and the choice is about the dominant failure, not a single label.

## What each method changes

**RAG** changes what the model *reads* at answer time. It can use current, verifiable information and cite sources, but it does not change the model's behaviour or reasoning, as the retrieval article explains.

**Fine-tuning** changes what the model *does* — its output format, tone, and task behaviour. It does not add knowledge reliably, which the distillation article's caveats about transferred behaviour illustrate.

**Continued pretraining** changes what the model *knows* about a domain by exposing it to more of the domain's text. It is the most expensive and the least frequently justified, as [Continued Pretraining for Domain Adaptation](/articles/continued-pretraining-for-domain-adaptation) explains.

Three questions separate them:

- Is the problem missing information, wrong behaviour, or unfamiliar language?
- Does the information change often? Retrieval handles change better than weights.
- Can the desired behaviour be described by examples? Fine-tuning needs them.

## A worked decision

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A legal team wants an assistant that answers questions about their contracts and produces summaries in a specific internal format. Three failures are observed:

1. **Answers cite the wrong contract version.** This is an information problem — the correct document was not retrieved. RAG with better indexing and version control is the fix, not fine-tuning, as the knowledge-graphs article explains for structured sources.
2. **Summaries omit required sections.** This is a behaviour problem — the model does not follow the format consistently. A fine-tuned model with examples of good summaries can help, or a structured output schema, as the structured-output article describes.
3. **The model misreads domain terms.** If the terms are unfamiliar, continued pretraining on legal text may be justified — but only after retrieval and prompting have been tried, as [Continued Pretraining for Domain Adaptation](/articles/continued-pretraining-for-domain-adaptation) argues.

Three conclusions follow:

- **The three failures need three different responses.** A single method would fix one and leave the others.
- **RAG is first** because the most serious failure — wrong version — is an information problem, and retrieval is where provenance lives, as the data-governance article explains.
- **Fine-tuning is second** for the format, because that behaviour is stable and teachable by example.

## A decision sequence

1. **Classify the failure** — information, behaviour, or domain knowledge.
2. **Try prompting and retrieval first**, because they are cheapest and most reversible, as the prompt-engineering article argues.
3. **Add fine-tuning** when behaviour must be consistent across many cases and examples exist.
4. **Consider continued pretraining** only when language itself is the barrier and a substantial corpus exists.
5. **Evaluate the full system** after each addition, since methods interact, as the LLM evaluation article explains.
6. **Record why the method was chosen**, so a future team can revisit the decision, which the decision-memo article covers.

The counterargument — that one method should solve everything — is a preference for simplicity, not a description of the problem. A well-designed system often combines retrieval for facts with a small behavioural adaptation.

## Combining methods deliberately

Once the dominant failure is identified, combinations follow a pattern:

**Retrieval plus prompting** is the default starting point for most knowledge tasks. It is reversible, updatable, and makes provenance visible, as the retrieval article explains. Many teams never need more.

**Retrieval plus fine-tuning** is common when the answer depends on changing information and the output must follow a stable format. The retrieval supplies facts; the fine-tuning teaches the format, which the structured-output article covers.

**Continued pretraining plus retrieval** suits domains where the language itself is unfamiliar but facts still change. Pretraining teaches the vocabulary; retrieval supplies current detail, as [Continued Pretraining for Domain Adaptation](/articles/continued-pretraining-for-domain-adaptation) describes.

These combinations are not stacked at once. Each addition should follow evidence that the previous stage left a specific failure, which the evaluation article explains how to measure.

## What the guide cannot decide

**It cannot rank methods universally.** The right choice depends on your failure, data, and budget, which the cost-estimator article quantifies.

**It cannot replace evaluation.** Only a labelled set can show which failure dominates and whether a change helped, as the evaluation-rubric article explains.

**It is not static.** A system that starts with retrieval may need fine-tuning later when the task stabilises, which the monitoring article covers.

## A decision test

1. **Name the dominant failure** in observable terms.
2. **Check whether retrieval can fix it** with better data or indexing.
3. **Check whether examples can teach the behaviour** through fine-tuning.
4. **Check whether domain language is the barrier** before considering continued pretraining.
5. **Start with the cheapest reversible option.**
6. **Evaluate the combined system** after each step.
7. **Revisit when the failure pattern changes.**
