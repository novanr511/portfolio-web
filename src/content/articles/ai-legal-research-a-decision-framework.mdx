<Lead>
Legal research has two tasks that are often conflated: finding the relevant authority and deciding what it means for a client. AI is good at the first when the sources are bounded and citable, and unreliable at the second because interpretation depends on facts, jurisdiction, and judgement, as [AI in Legal Research: Retrieval, Summarization, Citation Checks](/articles/ai-in-legal-research-retrieval-summarization-citation-checks) explains for citation checks, as [AI for Contract Review: What to Automate and What to Escalate](/articles/ai-for-contract-review-what-to-automate-and-what-to-escalate) treats material clauses as a human decision. A framework should keep the two separate.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:**

<KeyPoint>
Use AI for retrieval, summarisation, and citation checking under a defined scope, with a lawyer responsible for interpretation and a record of the sources used.
</KeyPoint>

Anthropic's use-case guides cover summarisation patterns, and AAPOR's standards apply to any survey used in the research. The boundary: this is educational orientation, not legal advice — the law and the rules of professional conduct are jurisdiction-specific and change, as the copyright and AI Act articles treat current law as requiring a dated check.

## The framework

**Scope the sources.** Define the jurisdiction, the date range, and the types of authority the system may use, which the market-definition article analogises to defining the relevant set, as the data-governance article requires <Mark>provenance</Mark> for relied-upon records.

**Ground every claim.** Each summary point should cite a passage that supports it, which the legal-research article treats as the core control, as the hallucination article warns about invented citations, as the reduce-hallucinations article treats citation checks as a discipline.

**Separate finding from interpreting.** A tool can locate a statute and summarise it; a person decides how it applies, which the contract-review article treats as the boundary, as the human-in-the-loop article argues for a meaningful review.

**Record the work.** Keep the queries, the sources retrieved, and the review, which the decision-memo article requires for a recorded judgement, as the data-governance article requires for provenance.

Three consequences follow. The system's value is <Mark>time saved on search</Mark>, not authority, as [AI in Legal Research: Retrieval, Summarization, Citation Checks](/articles/ai-in-legal-research-retrieval-summarization-citation-checks) explains.

## A worked check

The following is hypothetical and illustrative. A lawyer asks a tool to find authority on notice periods for a contract type in a jurisdiction.

Three checks decide whether the output is usable. First, citation existence: does the cited section exist, which the legal-research article requires? Second, support: does the passage actually say what the summary claims, as the reduce-hallucinations article treats as a separate check from existence? Third, completeness: was <Mark>the controlling authority</Mark> retrieved at all, which the reranking article treats as a recall problem, as [AI in Legal Research: Retrieval, Summarization, Citation Checks](/articles/ai-in-legal-research-retrieval-summarization-citation-checks) warns that a clean summary of the wrong source is worse than none?

Three conclusions follow. The lawyer verifies every citation, as the oversight article requires for a meaningful review. The tool's output is a draft, not an opinion, as [AI for Contract Review: What to Automate and What to Escalate](/articles/ai-for-contract-review-what-to-automate-and-what-to-escalate) argues for human judgement on material clauses. And the queries and sources should be recorded, as the decision-memo article requires.

## What the framework cannot do

It cannot give legal advice, which the legal context requires a qualified person, as the AI Act article treats role-specific obligations, as the governance checklist requires an accountable owner. ## A decision test

1. Define jurisdiction, dates, and source types, as the scope rule advises.
2. Require a citation for every claim, as [AI in Legal Research: Retrieval, Summarization, Citation Checks](/articles/ai-in-legal-research-retrieval-summarization-citation-checks) requires.
3. Check existence and support separately, as the hallucination article advises.
4. Keep interpretation with a qualified person, as [AI for Contract Review: What to Automate and What to Escalate](/articles/ai-for-contract-review-what-to-automate-and-what-to-escalate) argues.
5. Record queries, sources, and review, as the decision-memo article requires.
6. Check current professional rules with a dated source, as [AI in Legal Research: Retrieval, Summarization, Citation Checks](/articles/ai-in-legal-research-retrieval-summarization-citation-checks) advises.
7. Review the scope when the matter changes, as the monitoring article recommends.
