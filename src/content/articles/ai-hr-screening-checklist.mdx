## The checklist

**Job-derived rubric.** Define the criteria from the role's actual tasks, as O*NET descriptions support, which the hiring article treats as the substance of a fair process, as the value-proposition article argues for specific claims.

**Structured output.** Ask for a summary against each criterion with supporting evidence from the application, as the structured-output article explains for schema-enforced results, which the hallucination article treats as a control against invented detail.

**Human decision.** A person makes the decision and can explain it, which the AI HR screening article requires for accountability, as the human-in-the-loop article argues for <Mark>meaningful review</Mark>, not a rubber stamp.

**Bias testing.** Check outcomes across relevant groups at intervals and after changes, which the model-monitoring article treats as drift detection, as the responsible-AI article requires for fairness.

**Records and notice.** Keep the criteria, the tool version, and the decision, which the data-governance article requires for provenance, as the privacy article requires transparency about automated processing.

Three consequences follow. The rubric is the real control, which the structured-output article supports, as the evaluation-rubric article requires for concrete criteria.

And the tool should be reviewed after a job description change, as the monitoring article treats as a trigger.

## A worked review

The following is hypothetical and illustrative. A team uses a tool to summarise 500 applications against five job criteria.

Three checks decide whether the process is defensible. First, <Mark>rubric relevance</Mark>: does each criterion predict performance in the role, which O*NET's task descriptions help ground, as the jobs-to-be-done article advises for situations? Second, <Mark>grounding</Mark>: does each summary point to evidence in the application, as the structured-output and hallucination articles require, with no invented detail? Third, bias: are outcomes distributed differently across groups, which the monitoring article requires you to test, as the responsible-AI article treats as an obligation?

Three conclusions follow. A tool that produces fluent summaries without grounding is a liability, as the hallucination article warns. A reviewer who accepts every summary has no meaningful control, as the oversight article argues. And the result should be recorded with the criteria and the version, as the decision-memo article requires.

## What an AI screening checklist cannot do

It cannot decide who to hire, which the AI HR screening article treats as a human and legal responsibility, as the governance checklist requires an accountable owner. ## A decision test

1. Derive criteria from the role's tasks, as O*NET supports.
2. Require evidence for each summary field, as the structured-output article advises.
3. Keep the hiring decision with a person who can explain it.
4. Test outcomes across groups at intervals, as the monitoring article recommends.
5. Record criteria, version, and decision, as the data-governance article requires.
6. Check current legal obligations with qualified advice, as the legal article advises.
7. Review after a role or tool change, as the monitoring article requires.
