A customer describing their experience in their own words carries a credibility a brand cannot claim for itself. That is why advocacy and user-generated content are valuable — and why manufactured versions of them backfire. The distinction is whether the voice is genuinely independent.

**Direct answer:** Earn advocacy by delivering value worth talking about and make it easy for customers to share honest experience; do not script or buy the voice. Demand-generation principles explain the preference stage advocacy serves, and AAPOR's standards apply if you survey customers about their experience. The boundary: a testimonial that is selected only for praise is evidence of a selection process, not of the product, as the online-reviews article explains for reputation.

## What works

**Real stories with specifics.** A customer describing what they tried, what failed, and what changed is more persuasive than a polished quote, as the customer-journey article recommends for evidence.

**Easy sharing.** Give customers something worth passing on — a template, a result, a useful example — which the content-repurposing article treats as a value asset.

**Response.** When a customer posts about the product, a thoughtful company response is itself visible evidence, which the reputation-management article covers.

## A worked programme

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A B2B product wants more customer stories. The programme:

1. **Identify** customers who already talk about the product publicly, as the advocates article recommends looking for existing behaviour.
2. **Ask with AAPOR-style wording** — an open, non-leading request about their experience, with a defined population, as the survey standards require.
3. **Publish with context** — the customer's situation, what they did, and the outcome, with any numbers defined, as the data-governance article requires for relied-upon figures.

Three checks decide whether it works:

1. **Independence** — was the story solicited without conditions on what it says? A required positive quote is a brand claim in disguise, which the reputation article warns about.
2. **Specificity** — does the story contain verifiable detail, or only adjectives, as the evaluation-rubric article argues for concrete evidence?
3. **Downstream effect** — do prospects who see the story convert better, which the funnel article treats as a stage metric rather than a vanity measure?

Three conclusions follow:

- **The best advocates are found, not created**, as the advocacy principle suggests.
- **Honesty is the asset**, which the value-proposition article treats as the claim the business can support.
- **Measurement is per stage**, which the marketing-attribution article requires.

## Making stories useful rather than promotional

A customer story fails when it reads like an advertisement. Three edits keep it credible:

**Include the problem before the product.** A story that starts with the customer's situation gives the reader something to recognise, which the customer-journey article recommends for evidence tied to a stage.

**Keep the numbers defined.** If the story says time was saved, state how it was measured, as the data-governance article requires for any relied-upon figure.

**Let the customer's voice remain.** Heavy editing turns a credible account into brand copy, which the brand-voice article treats as a loss of authenticity.

These edits make the story useful to a buyer, which the content-marketing article argues is the purpose of content before any ask.

## What advocacy cannot do

**It cannot fix a product that disappoints.** A story that oversells creates a gap between expectation and experience, which the retention article treats as a churn cause.

**It cannot be bought without cost.** Paid endorsements without disclosure damage trust and may breach rules, which the influencer-measurement article treats as a measurement and ethics question.

**It is not a guarantee.** No specific story structure guarantees conversion, which the publication standard makes explicit for content claims.

The counterargument — that customers will not talk without incentives — is often true at the start and misses the point: the first step is delivering something worth talking about, which the value-proposition article argues is the underlying condition.

## A decision test

1. **Find existing advocates** from public behaviour, as the advocacy article recommends.
2. **Ask with AAPOR-style wording**, without leading, as the survey standards require.
3. **Publish with context and defined numbers**, as the data-governance article requires.
4. **Never require a positive quote**, which the reputation article treats as a credibility failure.
5. **Respond to stories publicly**, as the reputation-management article advises.
6. **Measure downstream conversion**, not just views, as the funnel article recommends.
7. **Check disclosure rules** for any incentive, as the influencer-measurement article covers.
