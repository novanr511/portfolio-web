<Lead>
For years, better performance meant a bigger model. A recent line of work shows that spending more compute *at inference* — searching, sampling, or refining before answering — can be more effective than adding parameters, at least on some tasks. That shifts <Mark>part of the cost from training to every request</Mark>.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Test-time compute uses additional inference-time work — longer chains of thought, parallel samples, or search over candidate answers — to improve a response. The paper *Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally* reports that on certain tasks this outperforms parameter scaling. The boundary: <Mark>the gain is task-dependent and the cost is per request</Mark>, so a method that is economical for a rare hard question may be wasteful for high-volume simple ones.

## What counts as test-time compute

Three forms are common:

**Longer reasoning traces.** The model generates more intermediate steps before answering, which the reasoning-model article covers.

**Parallel sampling.** Generate several candidate answers and select the best, using a verifier or a vote. This trades tokens for a better chance of a correct answer.

**Search.** Explore a tree of possible reasoning paths, scoring and backtracking. This is the most expensive and most structured form.

<KeyPoint>
They share a principle: use computation at answer time to compensate for a model that is not certain.
</KeyPoint>

## A worked budget comparison

The following uses published list prices with hypothetical volumes.

OpenAI lists GPT-6 Astra at $10 input and $50 output per million tokens. A hard question averages 2,000 input tokens.

- **Single answer:** 500 output tokens → 0.5 × $50/M = $0.025 output; total about **$0.045**.
- **Four parallel samples with verification:** 4 × 500 = 2,000 output tokens → $0.10 output, plus a verifier call. Total roughly **$0.13** — about three times the cost.
- **A larger model with one answer:** suppose it costs $20/$80 per million. Input $0.04 + output $0.04 = **$0.08**, with no extra inference work.

Three lessons follow:

1. **The comparison depends on the task.** If four samples raise accuracy from 70% to 90%, the tripled cost may be worth it. If the gain is two points, it is not.
2. **Cost moves from training to usage.** A smaller model with heavy test-time compute has a low fixed cost and a high marginal cost, which favours low-volume, high-value work.
3. **There is a crossover point.** At very high volume, a larger model with one pass may be cheaper than a smaller model with four, as the routing and cost-estimator articles explain.

## Where it pays

**Hard, infrequent questions** — complex analysis, mathematics, or multi-step planning where a wrong answer is expensive.

**Tasks with a verifiable answer.** If you can check correctness — a unit test, a calculated result, a schema — parallel sampling and selection work well.

**Applications with low latency requirements** where extra seconds are acceptable.

## Where it does not

**High-volume, simple requests.** Classification and extraction do not benefit from more thinking, as the task taxonomy explains.

<Trap>
**Tasks without a verifier.** If you cannot tell which of four answers is best, sampling adds cost without a selection method.
</Trap>

**Latency-critical interfaces.** A user waiting for a chat reply experiences the extra work as a slower product.

The method is available now, and the choice is whether it fits this workload and budget.

<PullQuote>
The counterargument — that models will get better and make test-time compute unnecessary — is plausible and misses the current decision.
</PullQuote>

## Measuring whether it works

The only reliable evidence is a comparison on your own workload. Three measurements matter:

**Accuracy per unit of compute.** Compare accuracy at a fixed budget — say, the cost of one answer — across strategies. A method that raises accuracy but costs five times as much may lose on this measure, as the cost-estimator article explains.

**Cost per correct answer.** Divide the total spend by <Mark>the number of correct answers, not the number of requests</Mark>. This metric makes the trade-off visible when error rates differ.

**Latency distribution.** Report the tail, not the average. A method that makes 95% of requests fast and 5% very slow may still break a user interface that waits for the slow ones, which the observability article covers.

Run each comparison on a labelled held-out set so the result cannot be an artefact of tuning, as the evaluation article recommends.

## What the research does not promise

**Not universal superiority.** The paper reports gains on specific tasks. A rank on those tasks is not a guarantee for yours, as the benchmarks article argues.

**Not free accuracy.** More compute raises cost and latency on every request, which changes the unit economics.

**Not a replacement for evaluation.** Whether the extra compute helps your system is an empirical question, answered by the harness described in the evaluation article.

## A decision test

1. **Classify the task** — hard and infrequent, or simple and high-volume?
2. **Check for a verifier** that can select among candidate answers.
3. **Price the single-answer baseline** at your real volume.
4. **Measure accuracy gain** from sampling or longer traces on a labelled set.
5. **Compute cost per correct answer**, not per request.
6. **Check latency** against product expectations.
7. **Route selectively** — extra compute only where it pays.
