The cost of GDPR is often reduced to the threat of a fine, which makes it easy to dismiss for a small company. The real cost is operational: changing how data is collected, documented, and deleted, and maintaining those changes as the business grows. A fine is a tail risk; the operating cost is certain.

**Direct answer:** GDPR compliance costs sit in data inventory, consent and notice design, rights fulfilment, vendor review, security controls, and documentation. GDPR Article 33 defines a 72-hour breach-notification duty, and NIST control guidance supports the security side. The boundary: this is educational orientation, not legal advice — the regulation's application depends on role, jurisdiction, and processing, and the authoritative text must be checked with a qualified adviser, as the AI Act article treats current law generally.

## Where the cost sits

**Data inventory.** You cannot protect or delete what you cannot locate, which the data-governance article argues for a record with an owner. Building the inventory is a one-off project; keeping it current is ongoing work, as the monitoring article treats any maintained record.

**Rights fulfilment.** Access, correction, and deletion requests require a process that can find a person's data across systems, as the data-privacy article explains for deletion as a system capability. A manual process does not scale, which the workflow-automation article treats as a candidate for bounded automation with a human owner.

**Consent and notice.** Clear wording and a defined population, which AAPOR's standards require for any survey-based claim and apply here by analogy to how consent is requested. A banner that collects everything by default is a design failure, as the data-ethics article warns about dilution of choice.

**Vendor review.** Processors need assessment and contracts, which the build-versus-buy article treats as diligence for any external dependency. The cost grows with the number of tools, as the martech discussion implies for a proliferating stack.

**Security and documentation.** Controls such as access management and logging, which NIST's catalogue structures, and records of processing, as the regulation requires. A breach-notification plan should be rehearsed, which the incident-response article recommends for any timed obligation.

## A worked estimate

The following is hypothetical and illustrative, in days of internal time rather than currency because rates and scope vary.

A small company doing initial work might spend twenty days on inventory, ten on consent and notice changes, fifteen building a rights-request process, five on vendor terms, ten on security controls, and five on a breach plan — about sixty-five days of one-off effort. Ongoing work might be two days a month for requests and reviews, plus a yearly reassessment, as the monitoring article recommends for a maintained control.

Three checks decide whether this is realistic. First, scope: a company processing only employee data has a different job from one processing customer behaviour at scale, which the data-strategy article treats as a question of what data exists. Second, existing maturity: an organisation with a data inventory and access controls already has much of the work, as the governance article argues for building on artefacts. Third, growth: each new system adds a surface to document, which the technical-debt article treats as complexity that accrues without decision.

## What the cost estimate cannot do

It cannot predict a fine, which depends on facts and enforcement, as the regulation's text and a qualified adviser must settle. It cannot be copied across jurisdictions, which the AI Act article treats as date- and place-dependent. And it does not create compliance — spending days on a project is not the same as meeting an obligation, as the evaluation-rubric article argues for measuring outcomes rather than activity.

The counterargument that compliance is too expensive for a small company misses the point that the obligations scale with processing, which the data-governance article treats as a question of what you actually do with data.

## A decision test

1. Identify what personal data you process and why, as the data-governance article requires.
2. Map where it lives with an owner, as the inventory guidance advises.
3. Build a rights-request process with a measured response time.
4. Review consent wording and vendor terms, as the privacy and build-versus-buy articles advise.
5. Rehearse the 72-hour notification path, as the incident-response article recommends.
6. Record decisions and dates, as the decision-memo article requires.
7. Check current authoritative text with qualified counsel, which the legal article treats as essential.
