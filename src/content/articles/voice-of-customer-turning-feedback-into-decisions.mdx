<Lead>
Organisations collect enormous amounts of customer feedback and often act on very little of it. The gap is not storage — it is the absence of a loop that turns a comment into a decision with an owner. Voice of customer is a discipline about that loop, not a dashboard of quotes.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Turn feedback into decisions by grouping it into themes, linking each theme to a decision and an owner, and checking whether the change affected the outcome. AAPOR's standards govern any survey used, and the demand-generation article explains which decision a piece of feedback relates to. The boundary: <Mark>a customer quote is evidence of one experience, not a directive</Mark>, and acting on the loudest voice without a pattern is a common failure, as the NPS article warns about over-reading a single score.

## The loop

**Collect.** Gather feedback from support tickets, reviews, surveys, and interviews. Each source has a bias — surveyed respondents self-select, as AAPOR's standards require you to state — so treat them as different signals, not one pool.

**Group.**

<KeyPoint>
Cluster comments into themes by the underlying problem, not by the words used.
</KeyPoint>

The clustering step is where interpretation enters, which the evaluation-rubric article argues for making explicit.

**Decide.** For each theme, name the decision it informs and who owns it. A theme with no decision is an observation, as [Decision Memos That Survive Review](/articles/decision-memos-that-survive-review) requires for a record that changes something.

**Verify.** After a change, check whether the theme diminished, which the monitoring article treats as a measured outcome rather than an assumption.

## A worked example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A product team reads 1,000 support tickets and 200 survey responses in a quarter. Three themes emerge: confusing setup, slow export, and missing integration.

Three decisions follow:

1. **Setup** has 300 mentions and blocks activation, which the customer-success article treats as the first value moment. It gets an owner and a 90-day target, as [Decision Memos That Survive Review](/articles/decision-memos-that-survive-review) recommends.
2. **Export** has 80 mentions and affects a high-value segment, which the customer-segmentation article argues for weighting by real difference. It gets a smaller fix.
3. **Integration** has 40 mentions from one large customer. It is logged but not prioritised, which the risk-assessment article treats as a decision with stated reasoning.

Verification after the quarter: setup mentions fall by 60%, export mentions by 20%, and activation improves for the relevant cohort, as the funnel article recommends for stage metrics.

Three conclusions follow:

- **Themes are prioritised by impact and segment**, <Mark>not by raw count</Mark>, which the NPS article warns about for averages.
- **Each theme has an owner**, which the governance checklist requires.
- **The result is checked**, which the monitoring article treats as closing the loop.

## Closing the loop with customers

A programme that collects feedback without telling anyone what changed loses participation. Three practices keep the loop alive:

**Acknowledge receipt.** A short note that feedback was received and read sets the expectation that it may lead somewhere, which the reputation article recommends for public responses.

**Report back.** When a theme leads to a change, tell the people who raised it, which the retention article treats as closing the loop on an issue.

**Show the record.** A visible list of what changed and what did not, with reasons, gives participants a reason to keep contributing, as [Decision Memos That Survive Review](/articles/decision-memos-that-survive-review) recommends for recorded decisions.

These practices are cheap and determine whether the next round of feedback is as useful as the last, which the survey standards treat as a matter of maintaining a relationship with respondents.

## What voice of customer cannot do

**It cannot decide strategy.**

<PullQuote>
Customers describe problems; they do not choose the market, which the strategy article argues is a leadership decision.
</PullQuote>

**It is self-selected.**

<Trap>
The people who respond are not the base, which AAPOR's standards require you to state, as the NPS article explains for scores.
</Trap>

**It is not the same as behaviour.** What people say they want differs from what they use, which the product-analytics article argues for observed metrics.

The counterargument — that customers know what they need — is partly true for problems and unreliable for solutions, which the startup-validation article distinguishes as a reason to test rather than ask.

## A decision test

1. **State the source and its bias** for every signal, as AAPOR's standards require.
2. **Group by underlying problem**, as the evaluation-rubric article recommends for themes.
3. **Link each theme to a decision and an owner**, as [Decision Memos That Survive Review](/articles/decision-memos-that-survive-review) requires.
4. **Prioritise by impact and segment**, not count, as the segmentation article advises.
5. **Check outcomes after a change**, as the monitoring article recommends.
6. **Pair stated feedback with behaviour**, as the product-analytics article argues.
7. **Review the loop quarterly** with a record, which the governance checklist requires.

For the survey standard, see [AAPOR](https://www.aapor.org/); for the decision record, see [Decision Memos](/articles/decision-memos-that-survive-review).
