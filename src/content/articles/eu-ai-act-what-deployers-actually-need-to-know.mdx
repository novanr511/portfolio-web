The EU AI Act is often described as a set of restrictions on AI, which obscures how it actually works. It assigns obligations to different roles — providers, deployers, importers — and scales them with the risk of the system. For most organisations, the relevant question is what a *deployer* must do, which is different from what a provider of a model must do.

**Direct answer:** The Act regulates AI systems by risk category and assigns specific duties to deployers, including use according to instructions, human oversight, monitoring, and record-keeping for higher-risk systems. The European Commission's AI Act page is the primary source for the framework and its staged application; the EU AI Act Explorer provides a readable structure of the text. The boundary: this is educational orientation, not legal advice — the Act's application depends on role, system type, and jurisdiction, and the definitive text is the regulation itself.

## Roles matter first

**Provider** — the entity that develops an AI system or has one developed and places it on the market under its name. Providers face the heaviest conformity and documentation duties.

**Deployer** — the entity that uses an AI system under its authority. Most organisations that adopt AI are deployers, not providers, though the line can shift — fine-tuning a system under your own name may change your role.

**Importer and distributor** — roles in the supply chain with their own obligations.

Three consequences follow:

- **Know your role before reading obligations.** A deployer's duties are not a provider's duties.
- **Role can change with what you do.** Rebranding or substantially modifying a system has implications, which the build-versus-buy article discusses for software generally.
- **Documentation from the provider matters** because deployers must follow the instructions that come with a system, as the model-card article explains for AI artefacts.

## Risk categories

The Act structures obligations around risk:

**Unacceptable risk** — practices that are prohibited. The consequence is not a control but a ban on the practice.

**High risk** — systems in defined domains such as employment, credit, education, or critical infrastructure. Deployers of high-risk systems face concrete duties: use according to instructions, assign competent human oversight, monitor operation, and keep logs.

**Limited and minimal risk** — lighter obligations, with transparency duties in some cases, such as informing people they are interacting with a chatbot.

**General-purpose AI** — a separate category with obligations that scale with capability and systemic impact, which mainly concern providers of large models.

## A worked deployer check

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A company deploys a tool that screens job applications. Employment is a defined high-risk domain, so the deployer assumes heightened duties.

Four checks follow:

1. **Confirm the role.** If the company uses a third-party tool unchanged, it is a deployer. If it rebrands the tool, its obligations may change, as the role discussion above explains.
2. **Check the provider's instructions.** The system must be used for its intended purpose with the stated conditions, which the model-card article argues for independently.
3. **Assign human oversight.** A named person with authority to override decisions and the competence to understand the system's limits — the human-in-the-loop design applies here, as the oversight article describes.
4. **Monitor and log.** Record inputs, outputs, and overrides so decisions can be reviewed, which the observability article covers operationally.

Three conclusions follow:

- **The deployment needs governance, not just a licence.** Oversight and logging are ongoing duties, which the governance checklist describes.
- **Worker communication and data protection are separate concerns** that interact with the Act but are not replaced by it, as the data-privacy article explains.
- **The compliance basis must be dated.** The Act's obligations phase in over time, so a statement that is true today should carry the date it was checked.

## What this orientation cannot do

**It is not legal advice.** Whether a specific system is high-risk depends on its intended purpose and context, which the definitive text and a qualified adviser must settle.

**It does not fix a system.** Compliance duties and safety are related but distinct. The risk-assessment article covers the technical side.

**It will not stay current.** The framework and guidance evolve. Check the Commission's page and the regulation text before relying on any summary, including this one.

The counterargument — that only lawyers need to read the Act — is fair for formal compliance and wrong for product teams. A team that does not know whether it is a provider or deployer cannot design the right documentation or oversight, which the AI-governance article explains.

## A decision test

1. **Identify your role** — provider, deployer, importer, or distributor.
2. **Classify the system's risk category** by its intended purpose.
3. **List the deployer duties** that apply to that category.
4. **Check the provider's documentation** and use the system within its stated conditions.
5. **Assign competent human oversight** where required.
6. **Set up monitoring and logs** with an owner.
7. **Verify against current text** and record the date of the check.
