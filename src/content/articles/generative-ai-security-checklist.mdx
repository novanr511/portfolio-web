**Direct answer:**

<KeyPoint>
A practical checklist covers six areas: untrusted inputs, tool permissions, data exposure, output handling, dependency and supply chain, and monitoring.
</KeyPoint>

OWASP's LLM Top 10 and NIST's adversarial-ML taxonomy provide the reference structure. The boundary: a checklist reduces known risks; it does not make a system secure against an adaptive attacker, which is why the controls must be enforced outside the model.

## Six areas

**1. Untrusted inputs.** Treat anything from users, retrieved documents, emails, and webpages as data, not instructions. The prompt-injection article explains why the boundary blurs inside a context window. Delimit untrusted content and assume the model may still be influenced by it.

**2. Tool permissions.** Each tool needs its own authorisation, limits, and logging. A refund tool should enforce an amount ceiling and a policy check regardless of what the model requests. Excessive agency is a separate OWASP risk from injection.

**3. Data exposure.** What does the model see, and where does that text go? System prompts, retrieved records, and conversation history may be sent to a provider. Sensitive data needs explicit handling, not a default assumption.

**4. Output handling.** Generated text that is executed, inserted into a database, or shown to a user without validation creates injection and misinformation risks. Validate structured output against a schema and review high-impact text.

**5. Dependencies and supply chain.** Models, embeddings, and prompt templates come from somewhere. Their provenance and update behaviour matter, as the build-versus-integrate article discusses.

**6. Monitoring.** Log inputs, tool calls, and decisions with enough detail to reconstruct an incident. <Mark>An alert with no owner is not a control.</Mark>

## A worked example of a tool boundary

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

An assistant can query an order database and issue refunds up to 200. A poisoned document in the knowledge base instructs it to refund 200 to every account mentioning a delayed shipment.

Trace the controls:

1. **Input trust.** The document came from a shared drive and is treated as untrusted. Its instructions are delimited as data. This reduces success but does not eliminate it — the model is flexible with instructions.
2. **Tool authorisation.** The refund tool requires a reason code that exists in the policy engine. The model cannot invent one. This is the control that stops the attack.
3. **Limits.** A per-transaction ceiling of 200 and a daily aggregate ceiling bound the damage if the authorisation check is bypassed.
4. **Logging.** Each refund records the source documents in the context. An incident can be traced to the poisoned file.
5. **Detection.** A sudden cluster of identical refunds triggers an alert to a human owner.

Three lessons follow:

- **The model is not the security boundary.** The tool is. Controls that depend on the model continuing to behave correctly are assumptions.
- **Damage must be bounded.** Limits matter more when prevention is imperfect.
- **Provenance enables a response.** Without source logging, the team cannot find the poisoned document after the fact.

## What to do before launch

**Map every input source** and label which are trusted, user-supplied, and externally retrieved.

**List every tool** with its permissions, limits, and the evidence it requires before acting.

**Test indirect injection explicitly** by planting instructions in documents the system retrieves.

**Define the stop condition** and the owner who can pause the system, as the governance checklist describes.

**Rehearse an incident** — a wrong action, a leaked passage, a poisoned source — and check whether the logs support a diagnosis.

## What the checklist cannot guarantee

**Attackers adapt.** A check that worked last quarter may be bypassed by a new phrasing. Re-test when the model or the integration changes.

**Filters are not controls.** Blocking known phrases does not address a model that can be influenced by paraphrase. OWASP's ranking treats prompt injection as a design problem, not a string problem.

**Compliance is not security.** <Mark>A system can pass a checklist and still be compromised.</Mark> The purpose of the checklist is to ensure the obvious boundaries exist, not to certify safety.

<PullQuote>
The counterargument — that a small team cannot implement all six areas — is fair.
</PullQuote>

The response is to prioritise the tool boundary and logging first, because those contain damage when the model is wrong. The other areas can be added incrementally.

## A decision test

1. **List input sources** and mark the untrusted ones.
2. **List tools** with per-tool authorisation and limits.
3. **Classify data exposure** — what leaves your boundary, to whom.
4. **Validate output** before it is executed or displayed.
5. **Check dependency provenance** and update behaviour.
6. **Log inputs, tool calls and decisions** with an owner for alerts.
7. **Test indirect injection** before launch and after changes.
