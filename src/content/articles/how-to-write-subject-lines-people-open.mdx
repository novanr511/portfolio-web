Gmail's sender guidelines govern whether the message is seen at all, and research standards from AAPOR remind us that a survey or test needs a defined population and question wording. The boundary: open rate is a noisy proxy — privacy features can register opens without a reader — so a subject-line test should be judged on downstream action, not on opens alone.

## What a subject line has to do

**Earn the open by describing value.** The reader decides in a fraction of a second, using the sender name and the subject together. A specific promise — "Your March invoice is ready" — beats a vague one — "Important update" — because the reader can predict the content.

**Match the message.** A subject that overpromises produces opens and then disappointment, which shows up as unsubscribes and complaints. Those damage reputation, as the sender guidelines make clear.

**Survive the preview.** Many clients show the first line of the message next to the subject. If the subject and preview contradict each other, the reader trusts neither.

**Work on a phone.** Most messages are read on small screens. Long subjects are truncated, so the important words belong at the front.

## A worked test design

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A newsletter sends to 50,000 subscribers and wants to test two subject lines for an issue about pricing changes.

- **Version A:** "Important pricing update"
- **Version B:** "What changes in your plan on 1 June"

A valid test has four elements:

1. **Random assignment.** Split the list randomly into two equal groups, not by geography or signup date, so the groups are comparable.
2. **One variable.** The message body, send time, and sender name stay identical. Only the subject changes.
3. **A primary metric with a window.** Define the outcome — clicks to the pricing page within 48 hours — before the test. Opens are a diagnostic, not the decision metric.
4. **A minimum sample.** With 25,000 per group, a small difference in click rate can be detected. A test on 200 subscribers cannot.

Three outcomes are possible:

- B wins on clicks and does not raise complaints → adopt the specific-subject pattern.
- The two are indistinguishable → the subject did not matter; keep the clearer one and test something else.
- B wins on opens but not clicks → <Mark>the subject generated curiosity without delivering value</Mark>, which is a warning about the content, not a subject-line success.

Three lessons follow:

- **Clicks are a better decision metric than opens**, because they require action.
- **Complaints are a guardrail.** A subject that wins opens but raises complaints is a loss.
- **Tests need a defined population and wording**, which is the research principle AAPOR publishes for surveys.

## What to stop doing

**Fake urgency.** "Last chance" repeated weekly trains readers to discount it and can attract spam complaints.

**Clickbait.** A subject that promises one thing and delivers another improves one metric and degrades the relationship. The email-metrics article explains why unsubscribes and complaints are leading indicators.

**Keyword stuffing.** Piling promotional words into a subject line reduces clarity and can trigger filtering. The sender guidelines reward wanted mail, not keyword density.

**Testing one variable at a time is not optional.**

<Trap>
If the subject and the send time change together, a result cannot be attributed to either.
</Trap>

The counterargument — that a rigorous test is too slow for a weekly newsletter — is fair.

<PullQuote>
The response is to accumulate tests rather than run them all at once: one clean test per send builds a body of evidence over a quarter.
</PullQuote>

## What a test cannot prove

**Context changes.** A subject that works for a pricing update may fail for a product launch. Treat results as pattern evidence, not universal rules.

**List composition matters.** A test on engaged subscribers may not generalise to inactive ones. State which population was tested.

**Opens are unreliable.** Privacy features preload images and register opens without a reader. A test that optimises opens may <Mark>optimise a measurement artefact</Mark>.

## A decision test

1. **Describe the message in one sentence** before writing the subject.
2. **Put the distinguishing words first** for small screens.
3. **Keep the subject and preview consistent.**
4. **Randomise the test** and change one variable.
5. **Choose a downstream metric** with a defined window.
6. **Watch complaints and unsubscribes** as guardrails.
7. **Keep results by message type**, not as a single rule.
