Two companies can report the same margin and be structurally different businesses. One owns its stores; the other rents them. One recognises revenue upfront; the other spreads it over a contract. A comparison that ignores these differences ranks the companies by their accounting choices rather than their economics.

**Direct answer:** Compare companies in four steps — align the periods and definitions, normalise accounting differences, compare ratios with their drivers, and check the cash conversion behind the reported profit. The financial-statement overview supplies the statement structure, and Investor.gov's guidance is a reminder that risk is present in all investments. The boundary: normalisation involves judgement, so document each adjustment rather than presenting the result as an exact figure.

## Four steps

**1. Align periods and definitions.** Use the same fiscal periods. Check what each company calls revenue — gross or net, before or after discounts — and whether the segments you are comparing are actually comparable.

**2. Normalise accounting differences.** Adjust for different depreciation policies, lease treatment, revenue recognition, and one-off items. An "adjusted" figure from one company is not automatically comparable with a reported figure from another.

**3. Compare ratios with their drivers.** A higher margin is meaningful only if you know why — pricing, mix, scale, or accounting. A lower return on capital may reflect a newer asset base rather than weaker performance.

**4. Check cash conversion.** Compare profit with operating cash flow for both companies. A persistent gap in one but not the other is a signal to investigate before trusting the ratio.

## A worked example

The following numbers are hypothetical and illustrative.

Two retailers report:

| Measure | Company A (owns stores) | Company B (rents stores) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue | 1,000m | 1,000m |
| Operating margin | 10% | 10% |
| Depreciation | 60m | 10m |
| Rent expense | 0 | 50m |
| Capital expenditure | 55m | 12m |

At first glance the margins are identical. Three adjustments change the picture:

1. **Capital intensity.** A spends 55m a year maintaining its asset base; B spends 12m but pays 50m in rent. The economic cost of the stores is similar, and it appears in different lines. Comparing operating margin alone hides this.
2. **Cash conversion.** If both report operating profit of 100m, A's free cash flow after maintenance capex is roughly 105m (100 + 60 − 55) and B's is roughly 140m (100 + 10 − 12), ignoring other working capital. B converts more profit to cash with less reinvestment, which makes its 10% margin more valuable.
3. **Balance-sheet risk.** A owns assets that may be financed with debt; B has lease obligations that are now largely on the balance sheet under current standards. The risk profile differs even where the income statement looks the same.

Three conclusions follow:

- **The identical margin was not an identical business.**
- **Cash conversion revealed the structural difference** that the income statement obscured.
- **The comparison needed a normalisation step** — treating rent and depreciation as alternative ways of paying for the same resource.

## Building a repeatable comparison

A one-off comparison depends on whoever performs it. A repeatable process makes the result auditable and lets a reader see what changed. Four habits help:

**Use a fixed template.** The same rows — revenue definition, margin, depreciation, leases, cash conversion, debt maturity — for every company in the set. A template prevents the comparison from silently dropping a row that is inconvenient for one company.

**Show the unadjusted figures.** Beside each normalised number, keep the reported one. Adjustments are judgements, and a reader should be able to see both the raw data and the interpretation.

**Record the source and period.** Every figure should carry the filing and the period it came from. Comparisons often go wrong because one company's trailing period and another's forward estimate were mixed.

**Write the conclusion as a hypothesis.** "Company B converts more cash after normalising lease and depreciation differences, which may reflect a less capital-intensive model" is a claim that can be tested. "B is better" is not.

## Where comparisons mislead

**Averages hide mix.** A company with a high-margin segment and a low-margin one may report the same blended margin as a company with a uniform middle margin. Compare segments if they are disclosed.

**Different cycles.** Two companies in the same industry can be at different points in an investment cycle. A company that has just built capacity will show high depreciation and low growth; another that invested years ago will look more efficient.

**One-off items.** Restructuring costs, acquisitions, and asset sales distort a single period. Read several years and identify which items recur.

The counterargument — that normalisation lets an analyst make any company look good — is fair. The discipline is to record every adjustment and show the unadjusted figures beside them, so a reader can judge the adjustment rather than accept it.

## A decision test

1. **Align the periods and definitions** before comparing any ratio.
2. **List the accounting differences** — depreciation, leases, revenue recognition, adjustments.
3. **Normalise with documented adjustments** and keep the original figures visible.
4. **Explain each ratio gap** with a driver, not just a comparison.
5. **Compare cash conversion** alongside profit.
6. **Read several periods** to separate structure from one-off events.
7. **Label the result as analysis**, with the uncertainties stated.
