<Lead>
A vague brief produces a vague result. "Make it look more premium" sends a designer into a guessing game, and the eventual review becomes a debate about taste rather than a check against decisions. A good brief <Mark>resolves the questions design cannot answer on its own</Mark>.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** A brief should state the audience, the decision the work must support, the constraints, the evidence available, the success criteria, the approval process, and what is explicitly out of scope. Brand-management principles treat this as part of maintaining a consistent identity, and marketing-communications research covers coordination across channels. The boundary: a brief defines the problem, not the solution — specifying the exact layout in the brief removes the designer's expertise.

## Seven sections

**1. Audience and context.** Who sees this, where, and under what circumstances? A logo used on an app icon and a billboard has different constraints.

**2. The decision it supports.** What should the reader think, feel, or do? "Recognise us as a credible alternative" is a decision; "look modern" is a style preference.

**3. Constraints.** Budget, timeline, formats, accessibility requirements, and existing elements that must remain. Constraints narrow the search and make the result evaluateable.

**4. Evidence.** Research, brand guidelines, customer language, and examples of work you want to avoid as well as emulate. Evidence replaces opinion with reference points.

**5. Success criteria.** How will the result be judged — recognition in a test, consistency with an existing system, or performance in a channel? State the metric before the work begins.

**6. Approval process.** Who reviews, in what order, and how many rounds.

<Trap>
An undefined process produces unlimited revisions.
</Trap>

**7. Out of scope.** What this project is not — a full rebrand, a new tone of voice, a website redesign. Stating exclusions prevents scope creep.

## A worked example of a brief

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A company asks a designer to create a set of social templates. The brief states:

- **Audience:** operations managers at mid-sized logistics firms, encountered in a crowded feed.
- **Decision:** recognise the brand across three post types and stop scrolling on the ones that address a problem they have.
- **Constraints:** must work with the existing logo and colour palette; square and 4:5 formats; accessible contrast; delivered in two weeks.
- **Evidence:** the current brand guideline, three posts that performed well, two that did not, and five customer quotes describing the problem in their own words.
- **Success:** a recognition test with ten target readers; consistent application across the three formats.
- **Approval:** one review with the marketing lead, one with the product lead, then sign-off.
- **Out of scope:** no new logo, no change to the voice guide, no paid campaign assets.

Three properties make this useful:

1. **The decision is stated before the style.** A designer can make choices that serve recognition without being told which font to use.
2. **The evidence includes failures.** Knowing what did not work is as useful as knowing what did.
3. **The approval process is finite.** Two named reviewers and a sign-off end the loop.

Three cautions apply: the recognition test needs a defined method, the constraints must be real, and the success criteria should be agreed before the first draft.

## Reviewing the result without guessing

A brief gives the review a standard, which changes how feedback should be given. Three practices help:

**Refer to the decision, not to taste.** "This does not make the reader recognise us in a feed" is feedback a designer can act on. "I don't like the blue" is a preference that may have no connection to the brief's goal.

**Separate requirements from suggestions.** If something is required by the constraints — accessibility, format, existing logo — say so clearly. If it is a preference, label it and let the designer propose alternatives. Mixing the two produces work that satisfies nobody.

**Test the outcome where possible.** A recognition check with a few target readers gives evidence about the decision the brief named. It is not a substitute for design judgement, but it turns "does this look right?" into a question with a method, which is the point of the success criteria section.

A review conducted this way usually needs fewer rounds, because the designer can see <Mark>which decision failed instead of guessing which element the reviewer disliked</Mark>.

## What a brief cannot do

**It cannot replace a brand strategy.** If nobody has decided what the brand stands for, the brief will inherit that ambiguity. The positioning work has to happen earlier.

**It cannot guarantee a good result.**

<KeyPoint>
A brief improves the quality of the decision; the designer still has to solve the visual problem.
</KeyPoint>

Specifying the solution in the brief transfers a task to the person least equipped for it.

**It stays valid only until the context changes.** A product launch or a new audience changes the decision the work supports. Revisit the brief rather than designing around a stale one.

The counterargument — that a long brief slows work down — is fair for small tasks. The test is whether <Mark>the questions in it would otherwise be asked during review</Mark>.

<PullQuote>
If the brief prevents three rounds of "I don't like it", it saved time.
</PullQuote>

## A decision test

1. **Name the audience and the context** of use.
2. **State the decision** the work must support.
3. **List real constraints** with formats and accessibility.
4. **Attach evidence**, including examples to avoid.
5. **Define success** with a method, before work starts.
6. **Fix the approval process** and the number of rounds.
7. **State what is out of scope.**
