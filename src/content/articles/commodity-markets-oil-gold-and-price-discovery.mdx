Commodities are the raw materials of the global economy.

**Direct answer:** Commodity markets trade homogeneous primary goods—energy (oil, natural gas), metals (gold, copper), agriculture (wheat, corn, coffee), and livestock. Prices are discovered through global supply and demand, often via futures exchanges. Commodity prices affect input costs for businesses and inflation for consumers. Managing commodity risk is essential for producers, consumers, and investors.

## What makes a commodity

A commodity is fungible: one barrel of crude oil or one ounce of gold is essentially interchangeable with another, regardless of producer. This fungibility allows trading on standardized exchanges with uniform contracts.

## Major commodity categories

- **Energy:** crude oil, natural gas, heating oil, gasoline.
- **Metals:** gold, silver, copper, platinum, aluminum.
- **Agriculture:** wheat, corn, soybeans, coffee, sugar, cotton.
- **Livestock:** live cattle, lean hogs.

## Price discovery: how prices form

Commodity prices are set in global markets through continuous trading:

- **Spot markets:** immediate delivery and payment.
- **Futures markets:** agreements to buy or sell at a set price on a future date. Futures allow producers and consumers to lock in prices and allow speculators to take positions.

Futures prices reflect expectations of future supply and demand, storage costs, interest rates, and risk premiums.

## What drives commodity prices

**Supply shocks:** weather (droughts, frosts), geopolitical conflict (war in oil-producing regions), labor strikes, and production decisions (OPEC+ output changes).

**Demand shocks:** economic growth (increases industrial metal and energy demand), dietary shifts (increases protein demand), and currency strength (a stronger dollar makes dollar-priced commodities more expensive for foreign buyers).

**Inventory levels:** low stocks amplify price volatility; high stocks buffer shocks.

## Volatility: commodities move sharply

Commodities are more volatile than stocks or bonds because:

- Supply is inelastic in the short run (wells and mines cannot be turned on/off quickly).
- Demand is inelastic (people still need energy and food).
- Storage is limited and costly.
- Geopolitical events can disrupt supply instantly.

## Hedging: managing commodity risk

- **Producers** (farmers, miners, drillers) sell futures to lock in prices and protect against price drops.
- **Consumers** (airlines buying jet fuel, food companies buying wheat) buy futures to lock in input costs.
- **Speculators** provide liquidity by taking the other side of hedgers' trades, hoping to profit from price movements.

## The contango and backwardation puzzle

- **Contango:** futures prices are higher than spot prices (market expects higher future prices or pays for storage).
- **Backwardation:** futures prices are lower than spot prices (market expects lower future prices or faces immediate scarcity).

These structures affect the returns of commodity investment products that roll futures contracts.
