<Lead>
The default assumption in many projects is to use the largest model available and only downgrade when cost forces it. That is backwards for tasks with a narrow scope. A small language model, fine-tuned or prompted for a specific job, can match a frontier model on that job while being faster, cheaper, and deployable closer to the data.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Choose a small model when the task is narrow, the output is verifiable, and latency or cost dominates. Large multimodal families such as Gemini illustrate the capability available at the top end; the edge-AI article covers the deployment techniques that make small models practical. The boundary: a small model has less general knowledge and weaker reasoning on unfamiliar tasks, so it is the right choice for a defined job, not as a universal replacement.

## What "small" changes

Three differences matter:

**Capability breadth.** A small model knows less and reasons less well on unfamiliar problems. <Mark>On a narrow task with a clear pattern, that gap can vanish.</Mark>

**Speed and cost.** Fewer parameters mean faster inference and lower memory, which the KV-cache article quantifies. At high volume the difference is decisive.

**Deployment flexibility.** A small model can run on a laptop, a phone, or an edge device, which matters for privacy and offline use, as the cloud-versus-device article explains.

## A worked selection example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A company processes 50,000 support tickets a month, classifying them into ten categories. A frontier model achieves 95% accuracy at a cost of roughly $500 a month. A small model, fine-tuned on 5,000 labelled tickets, achieves 93% at $30 a month and half the latency.

Three questions decide:

1. **What does the two-point gap cost?** If a misrouted ticket costs a few minutes of rework, the saving is clearly worth it. If it delays a safety escalation, it is not.
2. **Is the accuracy stable on new categories?** The small model was tuned on ten categories. When an eleventh appears, it may fail badly, as the monitoring article warns about drift.
3. **Is there a verifier?** Classification has a clear correct answer, so errors can be measured continuously and the model retrained when accuracy falls. The evaluation-rubric article covers the scoring method.

Three conclusions follow:

- **The small model wins on economics** if <Mark>the error cost is low and the category set is stable</Mark>.
- **The frontier model wins on robustness** to novel inputs.
- **A hybrid is possible** — small model for known categories, escalation to a larger one for low-confidence cases, which the routing article describes.

## Where small models fit

**Classification and extraction** with defined outputs and labelled examples.

**High-volume, low-stakes tasks** where cost per request dominates.

**On-device and offline applications** where data cannot leave the device.

**Drafting with review** where a first version is enough and a human checks it.

## Where they do not

**Open-ended reasoning** across unfamiliar domains, where broad knowledge matters.

**Tasks with little training data** for fine-tuning, where a general model's pretraining is the advantage.

**Applications that need multimodal depth** — high-resolution images, audio, or video — where the largest models currently lead, as the Gemini paper illustrates.

The counterargument — that small models are always cheaper — needs a caveat.

<Trap>
A small model that fails and needs escalation can cost more than a frontier model that succeeds first time.
</Trap>

Measure the full pipeline, as the cost estimator explains.

## The evaluation that decides

<KeyPoint>
A small model should be chosen on a labelled comparison, not on a parameter count.
</KeyPoint>

Three parts of the evaluation matter:

**Task accuracy against a baseline.** <Mark>Score both models on the same held-out set from real work.</Mark> A two-point gap is meaningful only if the set is large enough to detect it, as the confidence-interval article explains.

**Behaviour on novel inputs.** Include cases outside the fine-tuning distribution — a new category, a different phrasing, a malformed request. A small model may fail sharply here while a general model degrades gracefully.

**Full-pipeline cost.** Count escalation, review, and retries alongside token cost. A small model that handles 80% of cases and escalates the rest may beat either extreme, which the routing article shows.

<PullQuote>
These three measurements turn a size debate into an engineering decision with stated trade-offs.
</PullQuote>

## A decision test

1. **Define the task narrowly** and list its outputs.
2. **Check for labelled examples** to fine-tune or evaluate.
3. **Measure accuracy against a frontier baseline** on your own data.
4. **Price the error cost** of the gap, not just the token cost.
5. **Test behaviour on novel inputs** and define an escalation path.
6. **Measure latency and memory** on the target hardware.
7. **Re-evaluate when the task or data changes.**
