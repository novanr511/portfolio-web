Embedding models are often chosen by leaderboard position. That is a reasonable shortlist, not a decision, because a benchmark averages across tasks and your application has one. The MTEB benchmark exists precisely because different models excel at different things — no single model wins everything.

**Direct answer:** Select an embedding model by measuring retrieval quality on your own queries and documents, with a defined metric and a fixed corpus. MTEB provides the broad comparison; the SBERT paper established the bi-encoder pattern most retrieval embeddings use. The boundary: a model that wins on a public benchmark may underperform on your domain, language, or document length — the only direct evidence is your own evaluation.

## What the benchmark tells you

MTEB evaluates embedding models across many tasks — retrieval, semantic similarity, classification, and others. Three lessons follow:

**Different tasks, different winners.** A model optimised for symmetric similarity may be poor at asymmetric retrieval, where a short query must match a long document.

**Averages hide domains.** Performance on general text says little about legal contracts, medical notes, or code.

**The benchmark is a snapshot.** New models appear, and a rank from an older evaluation may be outdated.

Use MTEB to build a shortlist of three to five candidates, then stop relying on it.

## A worked evaluation

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A company has 20,000 internal documents and 300 real user queries with known relevant documents. It compares three embedding models on the same corpus and the same queries.

**Metrics:**

- Recall@10 — is the relevant document in the top ten?
- MRR — how high does it rank?
- Latency — milliseconds to embed a query and search the index.
- Cost — per million tokens embedded.

Three results:

- Model A: Recall@10 92%, MRR 0.71, 40 ms, highest cost.
- Model B: Recall@10 88%, MRR 0.65, 15 ms, medium cost.
- Model C: Recall@10 84%, MRR 0.60, 12 ms, lowest cost.

Three decisions follow:

1. **If recall@10 is the priority**, Model A wins, and the cost may be justified by fewer retrieval misses — as the RAG article explains, a miss cannot be fixed by the generator.
2. **If latency dominates**, Model B offers a middle ground with a small recall loss.
3. **The gap between A and C is only eight points** on this set. If the set is small or the labels noisy, that difference may not be stable — repeat with more queries before deciding.

Three cautions apply: the 300 queries must represent real traffic, not convenient examples; the relevant-document labels must be agreed by someone who knows the domain; and the index configuration (distance metric, chunking) must be identical across models.

## What to hold constant

**Chunking strategy.** A different chunk size changes the corpus, which changes the result. Use the same chunks for all candidates.

**Distance metric.** Cosine similarity is common, but if a model expects a different metric, document that and apply it consistently.

**Query preprocessing.** If you expand or rewrite queries, do so identically for all models.

**The evaluation set.** Keep a held-out portion that is not used for any tuning. A model tuned on the full set will score well there by construction, as the evaluation article warns.

## Building the evaluation set

The quality of the decision depends on the quality of the queries and labels. Three practices make the set defensible:

**Sample from real traffic.** Take queries from logs or support tickets, with sensitive details removed. A set invented by the team reflects what the team thinks users ask, which is often wrong.

**Agree on relevance before scoring.** Two reviewers will disagree about whether a document answers a query. Define a rule — this document contains the answer, not merely a related topic — and resolve disagreements before comparing models.

**Keep a held-out portion.** Reserve a share of queries that is not used for any tuning or threshold choice. A model selected on the full set has been optimised on the test, which inflates the result — the same leakage problem the data-pipeline article warns about.

A set of 200 well-labelled queries is more useful than 2,000 careless ones. The effort spent on labels is repaid every time a model or index configuration changes.

## What evaluation cannot tell you

**It does not measure the full system.** A better embedding model with a poor reranker or prompt may produce worse answers than a worse model in a better pipeline. The reranking article covers the next stage.

**It does not guarantee transfer.** A model chosen for English queries may behave differently in another language, even if the benchmark includes multilingual tasks.

**It goes stale.** Documents and queries change. Re-run the evaluation when the corpus or the traffic changes materially.

The counterargument — that a public benchmark is good enough for most teams — is fair when the task is generic and the stakes are low. It stops being fair when retrieval quality determines whether the system can answer at all.

## A decision test

1. **Collect real queries** with known relevant documents.
2. **Build a shortlist** from MTEB, not a winner.
3. **Hold corpus, chunking and metrics constant** across candidates.
4. **Measure recall and ranking quality** plus latency and cost.
5. **Keep a held-out set** for final confirmation.
6. **Evaluate the full pipeline**, not only retrieval.
7. **Re-run on change** in corpus, language, or traffic.
