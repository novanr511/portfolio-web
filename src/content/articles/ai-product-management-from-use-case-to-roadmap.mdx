Many AI roadmaps are lists of capabilities the team wants to try — a chatbot, an agent, a summariser. That is a technology backlog, not a product roadmap. A useful roadmap starts with a decision that is currently slow or poor and asks whether AI changes it measurably.

**Direct answer:** Select AI use cases by decision value, data readiness, error cost, and reversibility, then move each through stages — problem, prototype, pilot, production — with a defined metric at each. Anthropic's use-case guides provide concrete patterns, and O*NET's task descriptions help identify the work a feature changes. The boundary: a capability that does not change a decision is a demo, and a use case with high error cost and poor data is a blocked project, not a priority.

## Selection criteria

**Decision value.** What decision becomes better, faster, or cheaper? If nobody can name one, the use case is speculative.

**Data readiness.** Does the system have the information it needs, with known quality and access rights? The data-governance article covers the prerequisites.

**Error cost.** What happens when the AI is wrong? A recoverable suggestion is a better first project than an irreversible action, as the human-in-the-loop article explains.

**Reversibility.** Can the feature be rolled back? A reversible pilot teaches more than a large launch, which the deployment article describes.

## A worked prioritisation

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A product team has four candidate use cases. Each is scored on value, data readiness, error cost (lower is better), and reversibility:

| Use case | Value | Data | Error cost | Reversible |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Support reply drafts | High | Good | Low | Yes |
| Automated refunds | High | Good | High | No |
| Meeting summaries | Medium | Good | Low | Yes |
| Churn prediction | High | Poor | Medium | Yes |

Three decisions follow:

1. **Start with support drafts.** High value, good data, low error cost, reversible — the clearest first candidate, as the customer-support use-case article describes.
2. **Do not start with automated refunds.** The error cost is high and the action is irreversible. It belongs after a review stage has been measured, which the tool-calling article explains.
3. **Fix data before churn prediction.** High value is irrelevant if the data is poor, as the data-quality article argues. This is a blocked project, not a late one.

The point is that the roadmap follows from constraints, not from the excitement of the ideas.

## Stages and what changes

**Problem.** Define the decision, the baseline, and the metric. No model work yet, as the AI-integration blueprint explains.

**Prototype.** Test whether a capability can do the task on a small labelled set. The question is feasibility, not scale, which the evaluation article covers.

**Pilot.** Put it in front of real users with a bounded scope and a review step. Measure the combined system, as the workflow-automation article describes.

**Production.** Add monitoring, ownership, a stop condition, and a review trigger. The governance checklist covers the requirements.

Each stage should be able to fail cheaply. A roadmap that proceeds from prototype to production without a pilot has no mechanism for discovering integration problems.

## The metric at each stage

A roadmap without metrics is a sequence of activities. Four metrics keep it honest:

**Feasibility rate** at the prototype stage — how often a capability can do the task on a labelled set, which the evaluation article covers.

**Adoption and usefulness** at the pilot stage — whether users actually use the feature and whether it changes their decision, as the AI-adoption article distinguishes from mere access.

**Combined-system accuracy** at production — the error rate of the whole workflow, not the model alone, which the workflow-automation article explains.

**Business outcome** — the decision metric the use case was chosen for in the first place, such as time per task or cost per case, which the cost-estimator article quantifies.

A use case should be able to fail at any of these stages. Killing a weak pilot is a success if it prevents an expensive production build.

## What a roadmap cannot do

**It cannot predict model capability changes.** A model update can make a previously hard task easy or break a working one. The monitoring article explains why re-evaluation is tied to change.

**It cannot remove governance.** Each feature still needs an owner, a stop condition, and documentation, as the governance checklist requires.

**It cannot substitute for measurement.** A roadmap item is a hypothesis; the metric is the evidence, which the evaluation-rubric article explains.

The counterargument — that a fixed roadmap is unrealistic in a fast-moving field — is fair. The response is to keep the stages but allow the order to change when evidence changes, which the decision-memo article recommends for reversible decisions.

## A decision test

1. **Name the decision** each use case improves.
2. **Score value, data, error cost and reversibility.**
3. **Start with low-error, reversible, data-ready cases.**
4. **Move each through problem, prototype, pilot, production** with a metric per stage.
5. **Block cases with poor data** until the data is fixed.
6. **Keep a review step** before irreversible actions.
7. **Re-prioritise on evidence**, not on announcements.
