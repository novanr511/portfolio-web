AI Overviews covered 88% of the tracked commercial keywords by August 2026, turning a comparison page into a source an answer may summarize rather than a destination readers must visit. The decision that changes is what a team tracks alongside rank.

## How widely do AI Overviews now cover commercial searches?

Semrush tracked 400 keywords across five commercial modifiers and three industries from September 2025 to August 2026. Keyword-weighted coverage rose from 45.75% to 88%; volume-weighted coverage reached 97.08% in August 2026. The study, published 21 October 2026, reports coverage of a fixed US desktop panel. It measured whether an answer appeared, not how many people clicked through.

The two weighting schemes answer different questions. Keyword-weighted coverage counts each of the 400 terms equally, so it describes how widespread the feature is across the panel. Volume weighting gives each term its estimated monthly demand, so it describes how much search traffic sits behind the answers. Semrush reports that the 12% of keywords without an AI Overview accounted for under 3% of total search volume in August 2026.

That gap matters for prioritization. A page might sit in the minority of keywords where no answer appears and still face the summary on most of the demand. The reverse is also possible: a keyword with an answer and tiny volume is a small operational problem. Neither figure is a click rate. Coverage says an answer was present on the results page in the tracked month; it says nothing about whether a reader used it.

The panel is fixed and US-desktop, which is a scope statement rather than a caveat to wave away. Banking, software, and ecommerce keywords with those five modifiers are the population being described. Extending the percentage to every commercial query would be a new claim needing new data.

## Which commercial modifiers changed the most?

Reviews moved furthest, from 30.14% coverage in September 2025 to 90% by August 2026. "Best X for Y" finished highest at 96.12%, while brand-versus-brand rose 23.18 percentage points and alternatives gained 34 points. All figures are source-reported from Semrush's fixed panel on 21 October 2026. Coverage growth describes where answers appeared, not which brand they favoured.

At the start of the window, modifier coverage ranged from 30.14% for reviews to 63.41% for brand-versus-brand. By August 2026 the range had compressed to 82.05% through 96.12%. Reviews gained about 59 points, the largest jump, and "best X for Y" gained about 53 points to finish first. Brand-versus-brand began highest and grew least.

The shape of that change is more useful than the ranking of endpoints. A comparison page written two years ago may have been planned for a results page where an answer was the exception. The tracked modifiers now make it the rule. Pricing and cost queries were part of the same panel, and their ecommerce slice rose from 4.76% to 71.43%, so even the slowest corners moved a long way.

None of this measures a brand's position inside the generated passage. An answer can appear on 90% of review keywords and mention a given brand on a small fraction of them. The study reports coverage of the surface, and the interpretation should stay at that level.

## Do industries differ, or has coverage converged?

By August 2026, banking, ecommerce, and software all sat between 85% and 91% keyword-weighted coverage. Ecommerce climbed from 32.62% to 85.11%, the largest industry rise in the panel. Three of fifteen industry-modifier combinations reached 100% coverage: best X for Y in banking and software, and alternatives in software. Those are panel results, not a universal rate.

Twelve of the fifteen combinations reached at least 80% keyword-weighted coverage by August 2026. Three stayed below: banking alternatives at 65%, ecommerce price and cost at 71.43%, and software reviews at 75%. All fifteen finished higher than they started, so the gap is one of degree and timing. A year earlier the industries spanned 32.62% to 68%, more than 35 percentage points; by the end the spread was under six.

The 100% figures deserve a careful reading. They mean every keyword in that particular industry-modifier cell triggered an AI Overview in the tracked month. They do not mean every searcher saw one, and they do not mean the answer mentioned any particular brand. Volume weighting produced a different overall number for the same panel, which is a reminder that a coverage percentage inherits its denominator.

For planning, convergence is more actionable than the exceptions. Sector-specific playbooks built around "our vertical is unaffected" have a shrinking evidence base, while the three lower cells still justify a closer look at the exact modifiers that drive demand.

## Why is ranking different from being described correctly?

A rank is your page's position in a result list. An AI Overview is a generated passage assembled from several sources, and it can describe a product using your competitor's page or a third party's wording. Semrush's "mailchimp vs klaviyo" example cites Klaviyo's own guide for one option and Zapier for the other. Position and accurate description are separate measurements.

The source reports that a comparison answer can draw on YouTube videos, community threads, review sites, and competitor pages as well as the brand's own site. A page can hold position one and be paraphrased badly in the answer above it. A different page can rank lower and supply the phrase that shapes how a product is characterized. Those are different objects, and tracking only one of them hides the other.

"Described correctly" also needs its own test. A citation is a link, not an endorsement, and a mention by name can appear without a link. Checking accuracy means comparing what the answer says against the facts you can substantiate, then recording where the conflicting wording came from. That is a content audit, not a rank check.

For the limits of the signals available on this side of search, see [Measuring GEO: what you can actually count](/articles/measuring-geo-what-you-can-actually-count), and for how those queries get framed in the first place, see [Keyword research for AI search answers](/articles/keyword-research-for-ai-search-answers).

## What does a comparison page need to do differently?

Keep the page for ranking, and make its distinctions easy to extract: state plainly which option fits which buyer, keep pricing and specifications current, and check the third-party pages the answer cites for how they describe you. Semrush reported these as tactics on 21 October 2026. They are recommendations from the source, with no lift measured in that study.

The published list also includes tracking AI Overview visibility next to traditional rankings, mapping which sources get cited for your commercial queries, keeping comparison and pricing pages updated and structured for clean passages, working with YouTube creators whose reviews get cited, and requesting reviews on platforms where the guidelines allow it. These are source-reported suggestions. No controlled test in the study shows that any of them changed coverage or clicks.

The last two deserve a boundary. Requesting a review where a platform permits it is a tactic; it is not the same thing as an unprompted recommendation, and the distinction matters if the resulting count is later used as evidence of customer sentiment. The point of citing them here is to show the shape of the response the source recommends, not to adopt its claims as measured results. The decision a team can make is to add answer-level checks to the same review cycle that already tracks rank.

## What did our own curation decision count, and what did it leave open?

On 22 November 2026, a documented migration reduced this site's 1,004-article archive to roughly 34 curated pieces by filtering at publication, with excluded files kept in version control. It counted structure, delivery, and evidence per piece. No traffic outcome was measured before or after. The parallel is methodological: report what a number covers and say plainly what it leaves unmeasured.

The mechanism was an allowlist, which inverts the default. Nothing becomes live until it earns an entry, so a new generated piece cannot quietly reflate the archive. Derived surfaces such as the site's AI-facing text file were then generated from the curated set rather than from the full archive. That changed an input; it did not establish a gain in visibility. The files stayed in version control, so the decision was reversible, which is a separate property from being effective.

I bring it up because coverage numbers invite the same slippage. A percentage can be perfectly measured and still describe a narrow slice of reality. The curation record states what was counted and what was not, and that habit transfers directly to a study of commercial answers. The [curation case study](/projects/seo-content-curation) documents the decision and its limits.

## What can this study not tell you about your own keywords?

It is one fixed US desktop panel of 400 keywords in three industries over twelve months. It reports answer coverage, not clicks, conversions, or whether a description was accurate. A high rate means an answer appeared often in that panel; it says nothing about your brand's position inside it. Generalize to your own keywords as a hypothesis, then measure them.

The specific limits are worth listing. Coverage is a surface-level observation, so it carries no claim about traffic. The 100% cells are complete within a small fixed set, not across all searchers. Keyword-weighted and volume-weighted figures differ because they count different denominators, and neither is a measure of influence. No page edit was randomized, so the study cannot say what caused the growth it recorded.

The working decision is to treat 88% as a planning assumption, not a verdict about a single page. Build a fixed keyword set for your own commercial queries, record answer presence alongside rank, and audit how your product is described when it appears. For why a missing click is not the same as a missing effect, see [Zero-click content metrics that matter](/articles/zero-click-content-metrics-that-matter). Answer coverage tells you where to look; only your own measurements tell you what happened there.
