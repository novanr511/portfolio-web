Markets do not rise in a straight line.

**Direct answer:** Financial crises are episodes where asset prices fall sharply, credit becomes scarce, and financial institutions face widespread distress. They are driven by leverage, maturity mismatch, asset bubbles, and loss of confidence. Crises spread through contagion—interconnected institutions and markets transmit stress. History shows crises recur, but each has unique triggers; the lessons lie in preparation, regulation, and response.

## What causes a crisis

- **Leverage:** borrowing to amplify returns also amplifies losses. A small price decline can wipe out equity and force fire sales.
- **Maturity mismatch:** funding long-term illiquid assets with short-term debt. If short-term lenders refuse to roll over, the institution faces a liquidity crisis.
- **Asset bubbles:** prices rise far above fundamentals, driven by speculation and credit expansion. When the bubble bursts, losses cascade.
- **Loss of confidence:** depositors run on banks, counterparties stop lending, and markets freeze.

## Contagion: how crises spread

Crisis in one institution or market does not stay there:

- **Direct linkages:** Bank A's failure means Bank B (which lent to A) faces losses.
- **Indirect linkages:** falling asset prices force all holders to sell, depressing prices further (fire sales).
- **Confidence channels:** uncertainty makes all lenders wary, freezing credit even for healthy borrowers.
- **Global linkages:** capital flight from one country can pressure others through trade and investment ties.

Systemic risk is the risk that the entire financial system collapses, not just one firm.

## Historical patterns

Crises recur across centuries:

- **17th century:** tulip mania (speculative bubble).
- **19th century:** railroad and banking panics.
- **1929–1933:** Great Depression (stock crash, bank failures, deflation).
- **1997–1998:** Asian financial crisis (currency collapses, capital flight).
- **2007–2009:** global financial crisis (housing bubble, subprime mortgages, bank failures, Great Recession).

Common elements: excessive leverage, complex opaque instruments, inadequate regulation, and the belief that "this time is different."

## Policy responses

- **Lender of last resort:** central banks provide liquidity to solvent but illiquid institutions.
- **Deposit insurance:** prevents bank runs by guaranteeing depositor funds.
- **Fiscal stimulus:** government spending and tax cuts offset falling private demand.
- **Regulation:** capital requirements, stress tests, and resolution regimes aim to prevent and manage failure.

Policy responses involve moral hazard: rescuing institutions may encourage future risk-taking.

## Lessons for business

- **Leverage is a double-edged sword.** Debt magnates gains in good times and kills in bad times.
- **Liquidity matters.** Profitable firms fail when they cannot meet short-term obligations.
- **Counterparty risk is real.** Your counterparty's problems become your problems.
- **Cash is king in a crisis.** Firms with cash reserves survive; firms dependent on rolling over short-term debt may not.
