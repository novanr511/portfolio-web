A canonical tag states which URL should represent a page; it does not oblige a search engine to agree. Google's canonicalization guide calls the preference "a hint, not a rule" and says the engine may pick a different page. The distinction matters most where several signals describe the same address. In the curation dated 22 November 2026, 1,004 templated articles were reduced to a curated, restorable set behind an allowlist; sitemap.xml is generated from that same list; and a header-only request to www returned HTTP/2 308 to the apex after a certificate reissue. Each is a statement about representation, inventory or address, recorded in one snapshot. None instructs a crawler.

The external sources below are Google Search Central deep URLs read on 22 November 2026. Ranking and crawl outcomes for the local changes were not measured.

## What does a canonical tag actually assert?

A `rel="canonical"` link declares which URL is representative of a set of duplicate or near-identical pages. Google describes it as an annotation that suggests, rather than dictates, the canonical choice. The assertion is a claim about identity: this address stands for that content. A claim can be accepted, ignored, or contradicted by a stronger signal elsewhere on the site.

Google's guide describes the `rel="canonical"` element as indicating that another page is representative of the content on the page. It belongs in the `<head>`, which must be valid HTML, and Google recommends an absolute URL rather than a relative one. For non-HTML documents such as PDFs, the same relationship can travel in an HTTP `Link` response header. Annotations carrying `hreflang`, `lang`, `media` or `type` attributes are ignored for canonicalization. Every one of those rules is about how to phrase the claim. None of them turns the claim into an instruction that a receiving system is bound to execute.

## When does Google choose a different canonical?

Google states that it may choose a different canonical than the one a site declares, for various reasons. The declaration joins signals that include permanent redirects, sitemap inclusion, HTTPS preference and hreflang clusters. When those signals point elsewhere, the claim loses the argument. Search Console's selected-versus-declared fields are where the disagreement becomes visible.

The same guide says none of the canonicalization methods is required, because a site that omits them will likely do fine: Google identifies the version it considers objectively best. That sentence is the clearest statement of the signal model. A reported disagreement between declared and selected canonical is therefore a normal, reportable state rather than an impossibility. The www-to-apex case makes it concrete. On 22 November 2026 a header-only request returned HTTP/2 308 with `location: https://novandri.com/` after a certificate covering www had been issued. A canonical still naming the www address would have contradicted that hop; the tag would not have won. The capture is described in [Redirect Chains and What They Hide](/articles/redirect-chains-and-what-they-hide). Whether the change altered indexing was not measured.

## Why is a redirect a stronger signal than a tag?

Google lists the methods in influence order: permanent redirects, then `rel="canonical"` annotations, then sitemap inclusion. A redirect moves the client and removes the old address from the candidate set; a canonical leaves both URLs fetchable and asks for a choice. The www-to-apex 308 on 22 November 2026 was stronger than any tag, because it changed which address serves the page.

The mechanism differs, not just the weight attached to it. RFC 7538 defines 308 as a permanent redirect that preserves the request method and body, which is why it suits a hostname or scheme change. For a GET-only page the preservation detail is invisible, but the permanence is not. The same code appears in a different job on this site: slugs that leave the curated set answer with a 308 to the /articles hub, and the rule is generated from the allowlist. One hop, one authoritative destination, no intermediate step. That inventory-side use is set out in [Sitemap as an Inventory, Not a Growth Hack](/articles/sitemap-as-an-inventory-not-a-growth-hack). A canonical cannot produce either effect; it only nominates a representative among addresses that continue to exist.

## What does the sitemap contribute to the statement?

Google calls sitemap inclusion a weak canonical signal and says listed pages are suggested as canonicals while Google decides which are duplicates. This site generates sitemap.xml from the curated allowlist, so the inventory and the publication decision share one source. A declaration that drifts from what the site serves is harder to trust than one derived from a single list.

The weakness is deliberate. A sitemap entry asks a crawler to consider a URL; it does not assert that the URL has won a comparison. On 22 November 2026 the curation reduced 1,004 templated articles to a curated set, and the derived surfaces followed that set: sitemap.xml, the article index, the redirect rules and the AI-facing files all read the same allowlist. Nothing was deleted from disk, so restoring a slug restores every representation of it. An inventory that can be re-derived is different from a hand-maintained list of canonical URLs that silently falls behind. The format itself is covered in [XML Sitemaps at Scale](/articles/xml-sitemaps-at-scale); the point here is narrower. Four surfaces have to agree in practice — internal links, the sitemap `loc`, the canonical value and the redirect rules — and a single source of truth is what keeps them from disagreeing.

## Does adding more canonical signals help?

Google says the methods stack and that combining two or more raises the chance the preferred URL appears. Stacking helps only when every signal names the same address. A canonical to `/page`, a redirect to `/page?variant`, and a sitemap entry for a third form create a conflict that more annotations deepen rather than resolve. Coherence is the goal, not volume.

The signal order, read from Google's documentation on 22 November 2026, is short enough to keep in view:

| Signal | Strength in Google's list | What it asks for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Permanent redirect | Strong | Move the client to the named target |
| `rel="canonical"` | Strong | Treat the named URL as representative |
| Sitemap inclusion | Weak | Consider the listed URL a suggested canonical |

Because none of the three is required, adding them is a choice about clarity rather than a prerequisite for discovery. A site that emits a relative canonical on one host and an absolute one on another has created two claims for the same page; at a hostname migration, which is exactly when the tag matters, the relative form resolves against a different base. The hygiene that prevents such variants is described in [SEO URL Structure](/articles/seo-url-structure-normalize-hostnames-paths-parameters). It is upstream of the tag in the same way that the curation was upstream of the sitemap.

## How do you diagnose a Google-chosen canonical?

Open URL Inspection on the affected URL and compare the user-declared canonical with the Google-selected one. Then check redirects, internal links and sitemap entries for a competing address, and confirm the declared target returns 200. Each step eliminates one explanation. The disagreement is a symptom of conflicting signals, not evidence that the tag was ignored at random.

If the two pages are substantively different, the honest fix is to differentiate them and remove the tag rather than insist they are duplicates. If a redirect points away from the declared target, update the tag to the destination instead of adding another signal. A chain such as A → B → C wastes the hint; point at the terminal URL. A canonical naming an unrelated page is worse than no canonical, because it asserts a relationship that does not hold. None of this requires guessing at an internal weighting formula, and none of it requires an invented percentage for how often a tag is "overridden". The mechanics of colliding slugs and duplicate URLs are handled in [Canonical Tags and Duplicate URLs](/articles/canonical-tags-and-duplicate-urls-signals-not-commands); the decision sequence here is about a single address whose signals disagree.

## Can a canonical mark a page as withdrawn?

A canonical nominates a representative among existing URLs; it has no way to declare that publication ended. In the 22 November 2026 curation, slugs leaving the curated set were removed from the sitemap and answered with a 308 to the /articles hub. The address changed status, so the correct instrument was a redirect generated from the allowlist.

That distinction is easy to blur because both mechanisms talk about a preferred address. A redirect says the resource now lives elsewhere and the old URL should not be treated as a live representation. A canonical says several fetchable URLs describe one resource and asks that a representative be chosen. Withdrawal is a third state: the site intends to serve no page at that address. Google's best-practice list makes the same separation when it recommends a canonical over `noindex` for choosing among duplicates within one site — `noindex` removes the page from Search entirely, which is a different decision again. Reading the three apart prevents the common mistake of using a hint to announce a status change. The inventory consequences are recorded in the [curation case study](/projects/seo-content-curation).

## What should be recorded after a canonical change?

Record the declared value, the selected value, the redirect path and the date of each observation. The 22 November 2026 captures — the www-to-apex 308, the curation behind an allowlist — are structural facts a reader can re-check. Whether the change altered crawling or ranking was not measured, and a healthy header is not evidence of an index effect.

A served 200 at the intended URL, a single hop from each legacy host, and a canonical that matches the sitemap are checkable today. A visibility claim needs a baseline, an observation window and a stated comparison; the diagnostic side is described in [Google Search Console for Technical SEO](/articles/google-search-console-for-technical-seo-a-diagnostic-workflow). Keeping those two records separate is the whole discipline. The honest decision after a canonical change is to align the signals, verify the served bytes, and leave the outcome open until evidence exists. That is a narrower claim than a ranking story, and it is one that survives the next release.
