Red teaming is often reported by the number of findings, which rewards breadth over relevance. The NIST adversarial-ML taxonomy describes categories of attack, and the OWASP LLM list identifies risks for language-model applications. The trade-off is how much of each category a team can test well.

**Direct answer:** Choose depth in the categories that matter to the deployment, record the conditions of each finding, and retest after mitigation. The sources provide the categories; the choice is a synthesis.

**Delta:** This article adds a claim-status table and a worked exercise, separating documented taxonomies from local judgements about scope.

## The mechanism

The first trade-off is coverage versus depth. The NIST taxonomy provides categories, but a team with limited time cannot probe each one deeply. Choosing a few categories based on the system's actual use produces better evidence than a shallow pass over all of them.

The second trade-off is reproduction versus discovery. The OWASP list describes risks, and a finding that cannot be reproduced is hard to verify after a fix. Recording the exact conditions costs effort at discovery and saves it at retest.

The third trade-off is severity versus count. A single critical failure is more important than many cosmetic ones, but a count treats them alike. Severity should be defined before the exercise and applied consistently, which is a local decision.

The sequence is: choose categories, define severity, record conditions, then retest. The choices are local, so the conclusions are `inferred`; the taxonomies are `source-reported`.

## A worked exercise

*Illustrative.* A team has a week to test a support assistant that can call a refund tool.

Three checks decide. First, which categories matter most — untrusted input, unauthorised actions, or data leakage? Second, can each confirmed failure be reproduced from a recorded condition? Third, after a mitigation, was the same probe run again with the same result?

The outcome is a small set of verified findings. The scenario is hypothetical; the method is the contribution.

## Why a finding count is weak

A count depends on how hard the team looked and how broadly it defined a finding. Two exercises on the same system can produce different counts without any difference in safety, which is why severity and reproduction are more useful measures.

A further consideration is that the team running the exercise knows the system, which shapes what it tries. An outside perspective often finds different failures, and a program that never changes its testers will converge on the same findings. Rotating the people or the method is part of keeping the exercise useful.

The findings also age. A system that is updated after a test has changed, and a finding that was fixed may reappear in a new form. Treating red teaming as a recurring activity rather than a milestone is the practical response, with each round recorded against the version it tested.

None of this makes the system safe. It produces evidence about specific attempts under specific conditions, which is valuable precisely because it does not overclaim.

The exercise should be repeated after material changes, since a finding from an older version may no longer apply.

A finding that cannot be reproduced cannot be verified as fixed, which is why conditions belong in the record.

Depth in a relevant category is more useful than shallow coverage of many, given limited time.

The test set should reflect the system's actual use, not a generic list of attacks.

A clean exercise says more about the probes that were tried than about the system's overall safety.

The scope of an exercise should be stated alongside its findings.

## What it cannot do

It cannot prove the absence of failures. It does not replace monitoring. And it cannot remove the need for a decision about what risk is acceptable.

The counterargument that a broad exercise is better is fair for discovery and incomplete for the depth needed to verify a fix.

## A decision test

1. Choose categories from the system's use.
2. Define severity in advance.
3. Record probe conditions.
4. Confirm and rank failures.
5. Mitigate and retest.
6. Record what remains untested.
