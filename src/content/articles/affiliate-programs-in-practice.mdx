## The mechanism

<KeyPoint>
Affiliate marketing pays for a credited outcome, but credit and cause are different things. The programme can only be judged by what changes because it exists.
</KeyPoint>

Three points matter:

- **The channel is performance-based.** The affiliate entry describes paying for referred outcomes. That makes the credit rule central to the programme, because the rule decides what the partner is paid for.
- **Recommendations spread socially.** The word-of-mouth entry describes how people influence each other. An affiliate's value often sits in credibility rather than reach, which is why a small trusted audience can outperform a large indifferent one.
- <Mark>Credit is not cause.</Mark> A tracked sale can come from a customer who would have bought anyway. The comparison is the causal-inference correction that turns a report into a decision.

The sequence is: define the outcome, set terms, measure with a comparison, then review. The terms are local judgement, so they are `inferred`; the channel is `source-reported`. Neither source states that a tracked sale proves a new sale.

## A worked review

*Illustrative.* Assume a programme credits 100 sales in a month at a 10% commission. A matched comparison period with no affiliate activity records 60 of those customers buying anyway. The 40 remaining sales are the plausible incremental set, and the commission spend is judged against those 40, not against 100.

Three checks decide. First, does the tracking window credit purchases that would have happened without the referral? A 30-day window after a click will capture routine repeat purchases that were already scheduled. Second, is the commission consistent with the value of a genuinely new customer? A flat 10% on every order pays the same rate for a first purchase and a loyal customer's tenth. Third, is there a period or region without affiliate activity that can serve as a comparison? Without one, the programme has no counterfactual and the 100 sales cannot be split.

The outcome is a qualified assessment, not a verdict. The scenario is hypothetical; the method is the contribution. The numbers are illustrative and are not evidence about any real programme.

## Why terms shape behaviour

A commission that pays for every signup will attract partners who optimise for signups, including ones that never become customers. A commission tied to a retained customer changes the incentive, because now the partner has a reason to send people who stay. The terms are therefore a product decision, not an administrative detail, and they should be reviewed against the outcome the programme wants.

<Trap>
Paying on attributed revenue alone rewards partners for demand the brand already created. The commission report will look healthy while the incremental gain stays near zero.
</Trap>

## Why credibility beats reach

Two partners with the same audience size can produce very different results because their credibility differs. The mechanism is the same as word of mouth: a recommendation works because the source is trusted, not because the message reached many people. Selecting on reach alone misses this and tends to favour the loudest partner rather than the most persuasive one.

The practical consequence is that review should rank partners on the quality of referred customers, not just volume. A small, trusting audience can be worth more than a large indifferent one. The boundary is that credibility is hard to measure before the fact, so it has to be inferred from retained-customer rates and refund rates after a trial period.

## What the programme actually changes

A programme cannot create demand for a product that does not convert. It also cannot remove the need to check fraud, which grows with the value of the commission. What it can do is lower the cost of reaching a customer who was already inclined to buy, and that is a real benefit, just not the same as new demand.

The measurement should be reviewed on a schedule. A channel that was incremental at one point can become a source of cannibalised sales as the audience overlaps more with existing customers, and a comparison is the only way to see that change. The comparison period should match the affiliate's typical conversion window, or the result will be false in a predictable direction.

There is also a cost that does not appear in the commission report. An affiliate who misrepresents the product may produce sales that are later refunded, and the damage to the brand is real even when the tracked sale looked clean. That is why the programme needs rules about claims and a way to review them, which is a governance decision rather than a detail of tracking.

<PullQuote>
A tracked sale is evidence of credit, not evidence of cause.
</PullQuote>

## What it cannot do

It cannot guarantee incremental sales. It does not remove the need to check fraud. And it cannot replace a value proposition that converts the referred customer.

The counterargument that tracked sales are the whole result is fair as a record and incomplete as a cause.

## A decision test

1. Define the outcome the programme is meant to change.
2. Set fair terms tied to that outcome, not to raw volume.
3. Check the tracking window against the normal purchase cycle.
4. Use a comparison with no affiliate activity.
5. Review partner quality on retained customers and refunds.
6. Record the decision, the recheck date, and the rule that would reverse it.
