<Lead>
Product teams ask questions in language, not SQL: "Are new users activating faster this month?" An AI layer can translate that into a chart, which removes friction — until the definition of activation, the cohort, or the denominator is wrong and the chart misleads a roadmap decision. The value depends on definitions, not on the translation.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Use AI to clarify product questions, map them to defined metrics and events, and generate the query — then verify the result against a known baseline before treating it as insight. Anthropic's use-case guides cover analysis patterns, and geo-experiment guidance explains how to test whether a change caused an outcome.

<KeyPoint>
The boundary: a chart is a description, not a causal finding, and a fluent natural-language answer does not make a metric well-defined, which the data-governance article explains.
</KeyPoint>

## The four parts

**Question clarification.** Resolve terms like "active", "activation", and "this month" before generating anything. <Mark>A product metric's definition is a decision</Mark>, as the unit-economics article shows for CAC and LTV.

**Metric mapping.** Connect the question to the defined events and dimensions in your tracking plan. If the event does not exist, the answer cannot be computed, which the data-strategy article covers.

**Query generation.** Produce SQL or a dataframe operation that is readable and stored, not hidden behind a chat interface, as the reporting-automation article recommends.

**Verification.** <Mark>Compare with a published baseline</Mark> or a second method, and <Mark>show the definition with the number</Mark>, as [AI for Data Analysis: From Question to Verified Answer](/articles/ai-for-data-analysis-from-question-to-verified-answer) argues.

## A worked example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A PM asks: "Did the onboarding change improve activation?"

The AI layer should proceed in four steps:

1. **Clarify**: activation is defined as completing the first project within 7 days; compare the cohort after the change with the prior cohort; exclude invited collaborators.
2. **Map**: identify the events `project_created`, `signup_completed`, and the cohort field in the tracking plan.
3. **Generate**: SQL selecting both cohorts with the same 7-day window and exclusion rule.
4. **Verify**: compare the result with the analytics team's published activation figure for the prior period; check that the exclusion rule was applied to both cohorts.

Three checks decide whether the insight holds:

1. **Definition consistency** — if the prior cohort used a different activation definition, the comparison is invalid, which the revenue-recognition article explains for definitional changes in accounting.
2. **Tracking changes** — if an event was renamed during the change, the data may be incomplete, as the monitoring article covers for instrumentation drift.
3. **Causality** — even a clean difference in activation does not prove the onboarding change caused it, which the causal-inference article explains. A controlled experiment is needed for that claim.

Three conclusions follow:

- **The AI layer's job is to make the query inspectable**, not to declare the result.
- **A definition change is a red flag**, not a footnote.
- **Causal questions require a different method**, which the geo-experiment guidance supports.

## Instrumentation is the prerequisite

A natural-language layer cannot answer a question the tracking plan does not support. Three conditions make product analytics AI useful:

**Events are defined and stable.** Each event has a name, a definition, and an owner. When an event is renamed or its meaning changes, the change must be recorded, which the monitoring article covers as instrumentation drift.

**Metric definitions are central.** Activation, retention, and engagement should have one documented definition each, as the unit-economics article argues for financial metrics. A natural-language layer that invents its own definition per question produces charts that cannot be compared.

**The tracking plan is reachable.** The AI layer should consult the plan rather than guess which event corresponds to "signup", which the data-strategy article treats as a governance question.

These conditions are unglamorous and determine whether the tool helps or misleads, which the data-quality article argues more generally.

## What product-analytics AI cannot do

**It cannot define metrics.** Definitions belong to the product and data teams, as the governance article requires.

**It cannot fix broken tracking.**

<Trap>
A query over incomplete events produces a confident wrong chart, as the data-quality article warns.
</Trap>

**It cannot establish causation from a chart.** The causal-inference article covers what a genuine causal question requires, which the A/B testing article operationalises.

The counterargument — that self-serve analytics democratises data — is fair for descriptive questions and overstated for causal ones, which the funnel article distinguishes as different stages of decision-making.

## A decision test

1. **Resolve ambiguous terms** before generating a query.
2. **Map to defined events** and check they exist.
3. **Show the query and definition** with the result.
4. **Compare with a published baseline** using the same rules.
5. **Flag definitional and tracking changes** explicitly.
6. **Treat causal claims as a separate method** requiring an experiment.
7. **Keep the metric owner accountable**, as the governance checklist requires.
