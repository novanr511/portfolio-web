Finance is not accounting. Accounting records what happened; finance decides what to do next.

**Direct answer:** Corporate finance governs how companies raise capital, structure debt and equity, and allocate resources to investments. Core concepts include capital structure (the mix of debt and equity), weighted average cost of capital (WACC, the hurdle rate for investments), and capital budgeting (choosing which projects to fund). These decisions determine whether a company creates or destroys value over time.

## Capital structure: debt versus equity

Companies finance operations and growth through:

- **Debt:** loans and bonds that must be repaid with interest. Debt is cheaper than equity (interest is tax-deductible) but increases financial risk and fixed obligations.
- **Equity:** selling ownership shares to investors. Equity does not require repayment but dilutes ownership and demands a return.

The optimal capital structure balances the tax benefits of debt against the costs of financial distress. Too little debt may mean missed tax shields; too much debt may mean bankruptcy risk.

## WACC: the hurdle rate

The Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) is the average rate a company pays to finance its assets, weighted by the proportion of debt and equity:

WACC = (E/V × Re) + (D/V × Rd × (1 − Tc))

Where E is equity value, D is debt value, V is total value, Re is cost of equity, Rd is cost of debt, and Tc is the corporate tax rate.

WACC is the minimum return a project must earn to create value. If a project's expected return is below WACC, it destroys value; if above, it creates value.

## Capital budgeting: choosing investments

Companies have limited capital and infinite project ideas. Capital budgeting selects which projects to pursue:

- **Net Present Value (NPV):** the difference between the present value of cash inflows and outflows. Positive NPV means value creation.
- **Internal Rate of Return (IRR):** the discount rate that makes NPV zero. Compare IRR to WACC.
- **Payback Period:** how long to recover the initial investment. Simple but ignores time value of money and cash flows after payback.

NPV is generally the most reliable criterion because it directly measures value added.

## Risk and return

Higher expected returns require accepting higher risk. Corporate finance quantifies risk through:

- **Beta:** sensitivity to market movements.
- **Standard deviation:** volatility of returns.
- **Scenario analysis:** modeling best case, base case, and worst case.

## Common mistakes

- **Ignoring the cost of equity.** Equity is not free; investors expect returns.
- **Using the wrong hurdle rate.** Applying a company-wide WACC to a risky new venture understates its cost of capital.
- **Overlooking cash flow timing.** A project with fast payback but low total value may be preferred over a slower, more valuable project.
