## The essentials

**A will** states who receives your assets and who manages the process. Without one, the law's default distribution applies, which may not match your wishes, as the estate-planning overview explains for intestacy.

**A power of attorney** names someone to handle financial matters if you cannot. Without one, family members may need a court process to act, which is slow and public in many jurisdictions.

**A health-care directive** names someone to make medical decisions and states your preferences, which the estate-planning overview treats as part of planning for incapacity.

**Beneficiary designations** on pensions, insurance, and accounts often pass outside the will, which the retirement-planning article treats as a separate mechanism. An outdated designation can override the will, as the estate-planning overview warns about conflicting documents.

Three consequences follow. The documents should agree with each other, which the data-governance article argues for any set of relied-upon records. They should be reviewed after a major life change, as the monitoring article treats as a review trigger. And they should be <Mark>stored where the people involved can find them</Mark>, which the documentation article recommends for any record that must be usable under pressure.

The following is hypothetical and illustrative. A person with a modest estate, a spouse, and two children wants a basic plan.

Three steps follow. First, make a list of assets and who is named as beneficiary on each, as the data-governance article requires for a complete inventory. ## Why documents must agree

A will that names one beneficiary and an account that names another produces a dispute, because the designated account passes outside the will, as the estate-planning overview explains for non-probate transfers. A plan is therefore a system of records that should be reviewed together, which the data-governance article argues for any set of relied-upon documents.

<Trap>
The most common failure in a basic estate plan is not a missing document but a conflict between them.
</Trap>

The same logic applies to the people named. A person who is named to act but does not know until a crisis may be unable or unwilling to serve, which the communication article treats as a failure to make a decision legible. Telling the named people what is expected, and where the documents are, is part of completing the plan, not a courtesy. The worked example above includes that step for this reason.

Finally, the plan should be revisited after a major change, as the monitoring article advises for any maintained record. A new account, a move to a different jurisdiction, a birth, or a death changes what the right documents are, as the estate-planning overview implies for a plan tied to circumstances. The discipline is to <Mark>set a review trigger</Mark>, which the risk-assessment article recommends for known change events.

## What estate planning cannot do

It cannot avoid all tax, which depends on jurisdiction and value, as the local law must settle. It cannot replace a conversation with the people involved, which the family context makes consequential, as the communication article argues for decisions others can act on. And it is not permanent: a document that fit one stage of life may not fit the next, which the monitoring article treats as a change trigger.
