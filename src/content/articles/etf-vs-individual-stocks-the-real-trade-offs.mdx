<Lead>
The ETF-versus-stock debate is often framed as a choice between safety and excitement. That is a poor description. The real differences are diversification, control over what you own, cost structure, and the time required to manage the position.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** An ETF gives <Mark>broad diversification in one holding</Mark> with a published expense ratio; individual stocks give control over each position and <Mark>the potential for concentrated outcomes</Mark>, with more research and monitoring required. Investor.gov's guidance emphasises that all investments involve risk and that diversification reduces the risk of a single failure without guaranteeing a result. The boundary: neither structure is inherently better — the right choice depends on your time, knowledge, and willingness to hold through volatility.

## Four differences

**Diversification.** An ETF tracking a broad index holds hundreds of companies, so one failure has limited impact. A single stock can lose most of its value on one bad decision or report.

**Control.** An investor in individual stocks chooses exactly what to own and when to sell. An ETF holder delegates those decisions to the index or manager.

**Cost.** ETFs charge an expense ratio; individual stocks typically have trading costs and your own research time.

<Trap>
The lower headline cost of a stock portfolio can be misleading if the time cost is ignored.
</Trap>

**Time and temperament.** Managing individual positions requires monitoring and a willingness to make decisions during volatility. An index approach requires less of both, which is an advantage for many people.

## A worked comparison

The following numbers are hypothetical and illustrative.

An investor has 50,000 and two options:

**Option A — broad ETF.** Expense ratio 0.10%, no trading costs after the initial purchase. Over 20 years at a gross 7% return, the net value is roughly 192,000.

**Option B — ten individual stocks.** No expense ratio, but 100 a year in trading costs and, conservatively, 20 hours a year of research. Over the same period at the same gross return, the net value is roughly 189,000.

Three readings follow:

1. **The financial difference is small in this example.** The ETF's expense ratio and the stock portfolio's trading costs are similar in effect. The deciding factor is the 20 hours a year and whether the investor enjoys the work.
2. **The risk difference is large.** The ten-stock portfolio is far more exposed to a single company's failure. If one position halves, the portfolio is hit in a way the ETF is not.
3. **The upside difference is also large.** A concentrated portfolio can outperform dramatically if a few positions do well. That is the other side of the same concentration, and it is not a free option.

Three decisions follow:

- **Choose based on temperament and time**, not on a belief that one structure is universally superior.
- **Price your own time** when comparing costs.
- **Understand the concentration you are accepting** in a stock portfolio.

## What diversification does and does not do

Diversification reduces specific risk — the risk that one company or sector fails. It does not remove market risk, which is the risk that the whole market falls. A broad ETF still loses value in a downturn; it simply avoids the additional loss of a single catastrophic holding.

That work is a prerequisite for a concentrated portfolio, because without it the concentration is a bet rather than a decision.

## What the comparison cannot settle

**It cannot predict relative returns.** Over a given period, concentrated portfolios sometimes beat indices and sometimes trail badly. The outcome depends on decisions and luck.

**It cannot account for taxes.** The treatment of gains and distributions differs by structure and jurisdiction. The tax-efficient-investing article covers the mechanism.

**It cannot replace a plan.** The right allocation between broad and concentrated holdings depends on your goals, horizon, and tolerance for volatility — the asset-allocation question.

<PullQuote>
The counterargument — that index investing is boring and individual stocks are too risky — expresses a real preference but does not decide the question.
</PullQuote>

A small allocation to individual positions inside a mostly diversified portfolio is a common compromise, provided the investor accepts the time cost and the risk.

## A decision test

1. **State how much time** you will spend on research and monitoring.
2. **Price the costs** of each structure, including your time.
3. **Assess the concentration** you are willing to hold through a decline.
4. **Check the tax treatment** in your jurisdiction.
5. **Use broad exposure for the core** of the portfolio if time is limited.
6. **Keep any individual positions** sized so <Mark>one failure is survivable</Mark>.
7. **Review on change** in goals, time, or knowledge.
