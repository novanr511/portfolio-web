A cross-functional team brings different expertise to one outcome, which is useful and expensive: each function has its own priorities, vocabulary, and definition of done. The re:Work material treats teams as a practice to examine, as the negotiation resources treat interests as the thing to uncover, as the O*NET-style task descriptions help define roles, as the decision-memo article requires for a recorded judgement, as the small-team time article treats attention as a constraint.

**Direct answer:** Make collaboration work by defining a shared outcome, explicit owners, a handoff contract, and a decision record — not by adding meetings. Google re:Work provides the team context, and the negotiation resources provide the method. The boundary: this is educational orientation, not a guarantee — every team's context differs, as the publication standard forbids guaranteed outcomes, as the risk-assessment article requires residual risk.

## The practices

**A shared outcome.** One statement of what the group is trying to change, which the decision-memo article requires for a specific question, as the value-proposition article argues for a buyer evaluation, as the evaluation-rubric article requires for concrete criteria, as the dashboard article argues for metrics tied to decisions.

## What collaboration cannot do

It cannot create a shared goal that does not exist, which the value-proposition and positioning articles treat as prior, as the decision-memo article requires. It does not remove the need for individual focus, which the time-management and deep-work articles treat as a constraint, as the meeting-culture article advises. And it cannot guarantee a result, which the publication standard forbids claiming, as the risk article requires residual risk.

The counterargument that more meetings improve collaboration is fair for visibility and incomplete for decisions, which the meeting-culture and dashboard articles advise, as the worked example shows.
