Personal branding has a bad name because it is often taught as self-promotion: pick a niche, post consistently, perform expertise. That approach produces visibility without credibility. A more durable view is that reputation is a by-product of work other people can inspect and verify — the artifacts you leave behind and the way you explain them.

**Direct answer:** Build a professional reputation by producing verifiable work, explaining it clearly, and making it easy for the right people to find. GitHub's documentation treats the repository README as the first thing a visitor sees, which makes it a concrete starting artifact; O*NET provides structured descriptions of occupations and skills, useful for choosing which evidence matters. The boundary: reputation is not a substitute for competence, and no tactic can manufacture experience you do not have.

## Why evidence beats claims

Three mechanisms explain why proof-based branding is more durable.

**Claims are cheap.** Anyone can write "expert in machine learning". A reader discounts the claim until something corroborates it, so uncorroborated statements add noise rather than signal.

**Artifacts are checkable.** A repository, a written analysis, or a talk with a recording can be inspected. The reader forms their own judgement, which is more persuasive than an assertion.

**Explanation reveals depth.** A person who can explain why a design failed demonstrates more understanding than one who lists successful projects. The failure analysis is the signal.

This is not a rejection of visibility. Work that nobody can find does not build a reputation. The point is that visibility should amplify evidence, not replace it.

## The three-artifact portfolio

A useful portfolio is small and deliberate. Three artifacts cover most of the signal a reader needs:

1. **A working artifact** — a repository, a tool, a published analysis. The README should follow the discipline described in [Technical Writing](/articles/technical-writing-documentation-api-docs-and-developer-experience): a clear description, a runnable example, and stated constraints.
2. **An explanatory piece** — a short write-up of a decision, a failure, or a trade-off. This shows how the author thinks, not only what they built.
3. **A reference point** — a profile or page that states what the person does, which problems they work on, and where the evidence lives.

The third artifact should be consistent with the first two. A profile that claims a specialty the artifacts do not demonstrate creates doubt rather than interest.

O*NET's occupation records — with tasks, skills, and requirements across 900+ occupations — are a practical tool for choosing which artifacts matter. If the target role emphasises data analysis and stakeholder communication, a repository plus a clear write-up of a decision communicates more than a long list of tutorials completed.

## A worked example of an audit

The following is a hypothetical composite, not a real person's history.

A developer wants to be known for reliable data infrastructure. Their current public presence consists of:

- A profile that says "passionate about data and AI".
- Six repositories, four of which are forks with no README.
- A blog with two posts, both summaries of courses they took.

Audit against the three artifacts:

**Working artifact.** Of the six repositories, two are original and one has a runnable example. The forks are not evidence of skill and dilute the signal. Action: archive or clearly label the forks, and improve the one strong repository's README.

**Explanatory piece.** The two course summaries show that the author consumed material, not that they can apply it. Action: write one piece about a data pipeline that broke — what the symptom was, how it was diagnosed, and what changed. That single post carries more signal than ten summaries.

**Reference point.** "Passionate about data and AI" is a claim with no object. Action: replace it with a specific statement — the problems worked on, the kind of role sought, and a link to the two strong artifacts.

The rebuilt presence has fewer items and more signal. The general principle: a reader should be able to answer "what can this person do?" within two minutes, from evidence.

## Choosing what to publish

A common mistake is to publish a large volume of low-signal material in the hope that quantity produces reach. The opposite is usually true: a small body of strong work is easier for the right reader to evaluate. Three questions filter a potential artifact:

**Would a stranger learn something from it?** A repository that merely imports a library and prints a result demonstrates very little. One that solves a small real problem, with tests and a note on what broke, demonstrates judgement.

**Can you defend it in a conversation?** If a reviewer asked why you chose a particular design, would you have an answer? Artifacts you cannot explain are liabilities, because they invite a question you cannot answer.

**Does it match the reputation you want?** Publishing ten introductory tutorials while claiming specialist depth sends a mixed signal. The artifacts should be evidence *of the claim*, not adjacent to it.

The filter is deliberately strict. A professional reputation is built over years, and a handful of durable pieces will do more work than a continuous stream of material that nobody cites. There is also a maintenance cost: every published artifact is something you may be asked about in an interview, and something that may age badly if it depends on a specific tool version.

This is why the three-artifact portfolio is a floor rather than a ceiling. Once the core evidence exists, additional work should be added only when it strengthens the same claim.

## What branding cannot do

Three limitations deserve clear statement.

**It cannot create experience.** Inventing projects, outcomes, or credentials is fabrication, and it fails the moment someone asks a specific question. Everything published should be something the author can explain in detail.

**Platform dynamics are not merit.** Visibility depends on timing, audience, and algorithm changes. A post that performs well is not necessarily the best work, and a quiet portfolio is not necessarily a weak one. Optimising for metrics rather than evidence produces content that looks successful and persuades nobody.

**It can crowd out the work.** Time spent maintaining a personal brand is time not spent developing the skill the brand claims. For most people at most career stages, the balance should tilt heavily toward the work.

The counterargument — that in a competitive market, invisible competence is wasted — is legitimate. The response is not to abandon evidence but to make it findable: a clear reference point and a small number of strong artifacts are enough.

## A decision test

1. **State what you want to be known for** in terms of problems solved, not adjectives.
2. **Check that your artifacts support the claim** a reader would make after seeing them.
3. **Build one working artifact** with a README that makes it runnable.
4. **Write one explanatory piece** about a real failure or trade-off.
5. **Make the reference point consistent** with the evidence.
6. **Remove material that dilutes** the signal rather than adding to it.
7. **Spend more time on the work** than on promoting it.
