<Lead>
A funnel diagram is <Mark>easy to draw and easy to misuse</Mark>. The boxes — awareness, interest, consideration, conversion, retention — describe a customer's journey, but a diagram does not tell you which stage your business actually loses people at. Building one from scratch means defining the stages with measurable transitions before designing the content for them.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** A useful funnel defines each stage by an observable action and a metric with a denominator, then identifies the largest drop between stages. Demand generation builds preference before a buyer is ready; lead generation captures contact details. Geo experiments offer a way to test whether a stage intervention caused a change. The boundary: a funnel is <Mark>a model of a journey, not the journey</Mark> — customers skip stages, return, and arrive through channels the diagram does not show.

## Five stages with measurable actions

**Awareness** — the person can name you when a need arises. Measure with branded search and direct traffic, not reach alone.

**Interest** — a repeat visit, a subscription, or a meaningful engagement. Define the action that distinguishes interest from a single accidental visit.

**Consideration** — the person compares you with alternatives. Measure product-page depth, demo requests, or time spent with evaluation content.

**Conversion** — the defined outcome: a purchase, a signed contract, a completed signup. State the time window.

**Retention** — repeat purchase or continued use. This belongs in the funnel because acquisition without retention is <Mark>a leaking bucket</Mark>.

Each stage needs a transition metric: what fraction of people move from this stage to the next, over what window.

<KeyPoint>
Those fractions are what make a funnel diagnostic rather than decorative.
</KeyPoint>

## A worked example of finding the break

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A B2B product has the following monthly numbers:

- 20,000 people reach the site.
- 4,000 return or subscribe — a 20% interest rate.
- 800 request a demo — a 20% consideration rate from interested visitors.
- 120 close — a 15% conversion rate from demos.
- 100 remain active after 90 days — an 83% retention rate.

Three observations follow:

1. **The largest absolute loss is at the top.** 16,000 of 20,000 never show interest. If the product's market is broad, this may be normal. If the site traffic is highly targeted, it signals a mismatch between the promise that brought people in and the content they find.
2. **Demo-to-close is the highest-value stage to improve.** Each additional percentage point there yields 8 more customers a month. Because those visitors have already shown intent, a small improvement is worth more than a large lift in raw traffic.
3. **Retention is healthy but not the constraint.** At 83%, the bucket leaks, but not enough to explain slow growth. It still deserves monitoring because a decline there would change the conclusion.

Three decisions follow:

- **Improve the demo stage first** — sharper qualification, better follow-up, clearer proof.
- **Diagnose the top-of-funnel mismatch** rather than buying more traffic.
- **Keep retention as a guardrail** with a defined threshold.

The funnel reveals where to work; it does not explain why. Explanations come from interviews, session review, and experiments, which is why stage metrics should be paired with qualitative evidence.

## Building the measurement first

A funnel cannot be built before its measurement, because the stages are defined by what you can observe. Three prerequisites make the model usable:

**Event definitions.** Decide what counts as a return visit, a demo request, or a conversion, and record the rule. If the analytics tool changes its definition, the funnel's historical numbers stop being comparable. Write the definition next to the metric so a new team member can reproduce it.

**A time window for each transition.** "Subscribed then purchased" is meaningless without a window. Thirty days may be right for a low-consideration product and far too short for enterprise software. The window should reflect the decision cycle you actually observe, not a default in the tool.

**A cohort view.** Measure the transition rates for each signup month rather than one blended number. Blended rates hide changes in traffic quality — a campaign that brings more visitors who never convert will look like a funnel problem when it is an acquisition problem.

A team that builds these three things first can draw a funnel that answers questions. A team that draws the diagram first usually spends months arguing about which box a customer is in.

## Where funnels break

**Undefined stages.**

<Trap>
If "interest" means whatever the analytics tool calls an engagement, the metrics are not comparable across teams.
</Trap>

Write the definition down.

**Attribution confusion.** A single conversion may touch several channels and stages. Credit rules distribute the credit; they do not measure what would have happened without each touch. Use geo experiments or holdouts when the decision is material.

**Ignoring the return path.** Customers move backwards and skip stages. A funnel is a simplification, and a useful one only if you remember it is a model.

The counterargument — that modern buying journeys are too nonlinear for funnels — is partly true.

<PullQuote>
The response is not to abandon the model but to keep it coarse enough to be useful and to pair it with stage-level evidence rather than pretending the diagram is literal.
</PullQuote>

## A decision test

1. **Define each stage by an observable action** with a time window.
2. **Measure the transition rate** between adjacent stages with a denominator.
3. **Find the largest loss in absolute customer terms**, not just the lowest percentage.
4. **Prioritise stages closest to intent** when the impact per visitor is higher.
5. **Pair metrics with qualitative evidence** — interviews or session review.
6. **Use experiments** for material interventions, not attribution alone.
7. **Review the definitions** when the product or channel mix changes.
