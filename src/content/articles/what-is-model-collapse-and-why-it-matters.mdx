The internet is filling with text written by models. If future models are trained on that text, a natural question arises: does the process degrade? The model-collapse literature says it can, and the mechanism is more specific than "AI training on AI".

**Direct answer:** Model collapse is the degradation that occurs when a model is trained recursively on data generated by previous models. The paper *The Curse of Recursion* reports that this process causes models to progressively lose information, particularly in the tails of the distribution — the rare cases that make a dataset diverse. The boundary: the result is a controlled experimental finding about recursive training, not a claim that all current models are collapsing, and it does not make synthetic data useless.

## The mechanism

Training a model on a finite sample approximates the underlying distribution. When the model generates new data, its output reflects that approximation — with the rare events underrepresented, because rare events are poorly estimated from finite samples. Training the next model on this generated data compounds the loss.

Three consequences follow:

**Tails disappear first.** Common patterns survive because they appear often in both real and generated data. Rare patterns — unusual phrasings, minority viewpoints, edge cases — are precisely what a finite sample misses, so they vanish over generations.

**Variance shrinks.** Generated data tends toward the model's average. A dataset built from it becomes more uniform, and a model trained on uniform data loses sensitivity to variation.

**Errors compound.** A small bias in one generation becomes a larger bias in the next. This is the same compounding principle as error propagation in agent workflows.

The paper's result is a warning about a feedback loop, not a claim about a single training run. A model trained mostly on human-authored data behaves differently from one trained on successive generations of synthetic output.

## Why it matters for a content pipeline

Consider an organisation that uses a model to generate product descriptions, then feeds those descriptions into a future training set. Three risks emerge:

1. **Loss of specificity.** Distinctive product attributes are often rare in the text. If they disappear from generated descriptions, a future model cannot learn them.
2. **Homogenisation.** Descriptions converge on a house style. That may be desirable for branding and undesirable for recall of unusual features.
3. **Undetected drift.** Because the generated text looks fluent, the degradation is not visible in a spot check. Only a comparison against the original distribution reveals it.

The data-curation article covers deduplication and quality filtering as countermeasures. The key practice is provenance: know which parts of a training set are human-authored, which are generated, and in what generation.

## A worked example of tail loss

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A dataset of 100,000 customer reviews contains 2% — 2,000 reviews — mentioning a rare compatibility issue. A model trained on it generates a replacement corpus of equal size.

If the model underrepresents that 2% and produces it at 0.5%, the replacement corpus has 500 mentions. A second generation trained on the replacement may produce 0.1%, or 100 mentions. After three generations, the pattern is effectively gone.

Three controls change the outcome:

- **Preserve an anchor of original data.** Keep a fixed fraction of human-authored examples in every generation. The collapse result is strongest when generated data fully replaces real data.
- **Track the tail explicitly.** Measure the frequency of the rare pattern at each generation. An aggregate quality metric will not show its disappearance.
- **Use generation for specific purposes.** Synthetic data is valuable for balancing a class or creating edge cases deliberately, as the synthetic-data article discusses. The danger is uncontrolled replacement, not all synthetic data.

The lesson is that collapse is a distributional problem. You see it by measuring specific slices, not by asking whether the new text reads well.

## What the result does not say

**It does not mean all synthetic data is harmful.** Deliberate generation with verified labels can improve a dataset. The paper studies recursive replacement under controlled conditions.

**It does not prove current models are collapsing.** The experiment isolates a mechanism; whether it dominates in a particular training corpus is an empirical question with access to data most researchers do not have.

**It does not make human data automatically correct.** Human-authored data contains errors and biases. The point is about diversity and provenance, not purity.

The counterargument — that new data will always be generated from human sources — is partly true and complacent. As generated content grows, the proportion of human-anchored text in some domains falls, which is exactly when provenance and tail measurement matter.

## A decision test

1. **Label the provenance** of every training source — human, generated, and which generation.
2. **Keep a fixed anchor** of original data across retraining cycles.
3. **Measure tail slices** explicitly, not only aggregate quality.
4. **Check for homogenisation** by comparing diversity across generations.
5. **Use synthetic data deliberately** with verified labels and a defined purpose.
6. **Monitor drift** after each retraining, with a rollback path.
7. **Treat "it reads fine" as no evidence** about distributional health.
