Most newsletters stop growing because they are launched before anyone knows what they are for. The first thousand subscribers are not found through a growth hack; they come from a clear promise to a defined audience and a repeatable way of reaching that audience.

**Direct answer:** Start with a specific promise to a named reader, publish on a schedule you can keep, and recruit through channels where that reader already is. Gmail's sender guidelines govern deliverability as the list grows, and demand-generation principles explain why a newsletter serves a stage of a decision. The boundary: subscriber count is a vanity metric unless those subscribers read and act — engagement and retention matter more than size.

## The first-thousand sequence

**1. Define the reader and the promise.** "A weekly note for operations managers on reducing manual reporting" is usable. "A newsletter about business" is not. The promise should be something a reader would want to receive next week.

**2. Publish three issues before recruiting.** A signup page with no archive asks for trust without evidence. Three issues show what the reader gets.

**3. Recruit from a channel you already control.** Existing customers, colleagues, and a personal account are the first sources. The point is not scale; it is finding the first readers who match the promise.

**4. Ask for referrals at the moment of value.** A reader who just found an issue useful is the right person to ask. Make the ask specific: "forward this to one person who runs reporting".

**5. Protect deliverability from the start.** Use confirmed signups, make unsubscribing easy, and watch complaint rates as the list grows, as the sender guidelines advise.

## A worked example of the arithmetic

The following numbers are hypothetical and illustrative.

A newsletter aims for 1,000 subscribers in six months with a weekly issue.

- Referrals from readers: 40 per month on average, if 20% of readers forward and each forward yields a small number of signups.
- Content upgrades on three articles: 60 per month combined.
- Direct promotion in an existing community: 60 per month.

Total: 160 per month, reaching 960 in six months — close to the goal. Three factors decide whether this holds:

1. **Churn.** If 3% of the list unsubscribes each month, net growth falls. At 500 subscribers, that is 15 lost per month; by 1,000, it is 30. The recruiting rate must exceed churn, which rises with size.
2. **Engagement.** If only 25% of subscribers open an issue, the promise is not landing with most of the list. A smaller, engaged list is more valuable than a large silent one, as the email-metrics article explains.
3. **Referral decay.** As the most enthusiastic readers exhaust their networks, referrals slow. New sources are needed by month four, not after growth stops.

Three decisions follow:

- **Track net growth**, not signups — subtract unsubscribes every month.
- **Measure engagement by cohort**, so a decline in quality is visible early.
- **Add a new acquisition source before referrals decay**, not after.

## What to write about

The promise should be tied to a decision the reader makes repeatedly. Four reliable sources:

**A recurring problem** — a task the reader does every week and would like to do better.

**A change in the field** — regulations, tools, or practices that affect their work, explained with a point of view.

**Worked examples** — a real problem solved with numbers and a method, which are more useful than opinions.

**Curated evidence** — a small number of sources worth reading, with a reason why each matters.

A newsletter that publishes generic summaries gives readers no reason to return. Specificity is the moat.

## What the numbers cannot show

**A signup is not a reader.** The metric that matters is whether people open, click, and reply over months, not the day they subscribed.

**Referrals are not automatic.** They follow from a reader feeling that forwarding an issue helps someone they know. A generic "share this" ask does less than a specific one.

**Growth is not the goal.** The goal is a reader who trusts the newsletter enough to act on it. A list of 200 such readers can be more valuable than 10,000 who signed up for a template and forgot.

The counterargument — that a small list cannot sustain a business — is fair. The response is to grow from a base of engaged readers rather than buying a list, which damages deliverability and produces no audience.

## A decision test

1. **Write the promise** for a named reader in one sentence.
2. **Publish three issues** before asking for signups.
3. **Recruit from a channel you control.**
4. **Track net growth** with unsubscribes subtracted.
5. **Measure engagement by cohort**, not just list size.
6. **Ask for referrals at the moment of value.**
7. **Protect deliverability** with confirmed signups and easy unsubscribes.
