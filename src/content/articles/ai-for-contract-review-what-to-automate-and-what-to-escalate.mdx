Contract review is an attractive target for AI: the documents are long, the language is repetitive, and a first pass takes experienced time. The useful division of labour is clear — let the model find and compare clauses, and let a lawyer decide what the clauses mean for this deal. Blurring that line produces confident summaries that miss the point.

**Direct answer:** Automate clause identification, comparison against a template, and extraction of key terms; escalate deviations, unusual language, and anything with material legal consequence to a qualified reviewer. Anthropic's use-case guides cover legal summarisation, and the document-processing article covers the extraction mechanics. The boundary: a model can flag that a clause differs from the standard, but it cannot judge whether that difference is acceptable given the commercial context, which is a legal and business decision.

## What to automate

**Clause identification.** Locate the termination, liability, indemnity, and governing-law clauses in a document. This is a retrieval-and-extraction task, as the document-processing article describes.

**Template comparison.** Flag where a clause deviates from the approved standard. The comparison is mechanical — does the text match the expected pattern — and the model can do it at scale.

**Key-term extraction.** Pull parties, dates, values, and renewal terms into a structured field, using a schema as the structured-output article explains.

**First-pass summarisation.** Produce a short abstract for a reviewer to orient themselves, with each claim linked to the source clause, as the reduce-hallucinations article recommends for grounding.

## What to escalate

**Any material deviation.** A clause that differs from the template may be a negotiable point or a deal-breaker; that judgement needs context.

**Unusual or novel language.** If the model has not seen a clause form before, its confidence is not evidence of meaning. The benchmark discussion of coverage applies here, as the benchmarks article argues.

**Cross-clause interactions.** A liability cap and an indemnity clause may interact in a way that is only visible when read together, which the document-processing article notes as a systemic limitation.

**High-value contracts.** The cost of an error scales with the deal, so the review threshold should scale too, as the risk-assessment article explains.

## A worked triage

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A team reviews 500 vendor contracts a quarter. The AI pipeline:

1. Extracts key terms and identifies the standard clauses.
2. Compares each against the template and flags deviations with the source text.
3. Produces a one-page summary for the reviewer.

Outcomes on a sample quarter:

- 350 contracts have no material deviations and go through a light review.
- 120 have one or two flagged clauses and go to a lawyer with the clauses highlighted.
- 30 have unusual language or high value and receive full legal review.

Three checks decide whether the triage is safe:

1. **False-negative audit.** Have a lawyer review a random sample of the 350 "clean" contracts. If a material deviation was missed, the comparison threshold is too loose, which the evaluation-rubric article explains for false negatives.
2. **Citation accuracy.** Check that each flagged clause is quoted correctly, which the reduce-hallucinations article treats as a core check.
3. **Review time.** Measure whether the 120 flagged contracts actually take less time with highlights, or whether reviewers re-read everything, which the workflow-automation article covers.

Three conclusions follow:

- **The value is in triage**, not in replacing review.
- **False negatives are the dangerous error**, so audit the clean bucket.
- **Legal retains the decision** on every material clause, as the human-in-the-loop article argues.

## What contract AI cannot do

**It cannot give legal advice.** A flag is a prompt to look, not an opinion, which the AI-governance article addresses for high-stakes domains.

**It cannot read commercial intent.** Whether a liability cap is acceptable depends on the deal's value and alternatives, which are not in the document.

**It stays current only if the template does.** A comparison against an outdated template produces misleading flags, which the monitoring article covers as a change trigger.

The counterargument — that experienced lawyers will not use such a tool — is an adoption problem, not a capability problem. A tool that reduces the time to find clauses while keeping the decision with the lawyer is often welcomed, as the contract-review use-case guide suggests.

## A decision test

1. **Automate identification and comparison**, not judgement.
2. **Cite the source clause** for every extraction and flag.
3. **Escalate material deviations** and unusual language.
4. **Audit the clean bucket** for false negatives.
5. **Scale review thresholds with deal value.**
6. **Keep the template current** with an owner.
7. **Measure review time and missed-clause rate**, as the evaluation article recommends.
