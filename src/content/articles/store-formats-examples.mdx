## Examples of formats

**Convenience.** Small footprint, limited assortment, high convenience, and higher prices, as the retail-channel economics article explains for how rent and service shape cost. The customer job is speed, which the jobs-to-be-done article frames as a situation.

**Supermarket.** Broad assortment, self-service, and moderate margins, which the assortment article treats as a trade between variety and inventory cost. The format depends on density and footfall, as the location question in the retail-channel article explains.

**Warehouse club.** Large footprint, limited SKUs, bulk packs, and membership, which the e-commerce-versus-brick article explains for how a format trades service for price. The customer job is stocking up, which the inventory article treats as a cash and space decision.

**Specialty.** Deep assortment in one category with high service, which the product-assortment article argues can matter more than breadth, as the private-label article treats brand and margin choices.

Three consequences follow. Each format has a different cost structure, which the unit-economics article requires for margin analysis. Each serves a different job, as the jobs-to-be-done article advises. And each fails when the signals are mismatched — <Mark>a convenience store in a low-footfall location or a warehouse club where customers lack storage</Mark>, as [E-commerce vs Brick and Mortar: The Economics of Retail Channels](/articles/e-commerce-vs-brick-and-mortar-the-economics-of-retail-channels) explains for operating context.

## Why formats evolve

A store format is not fixed once and kept forever. Customer habits shift, competitors open new formats, and the cost of space changes, which the retail-channel article treats as the economics that shape a format, as the real-estate article explains for how property cycles affect occupancy cost. <Mark>A format that was right a decade ago can be expensive and misaligned now</Mark>, which the monitoring article treats as a change trigger for any design tied to conditions.

The evolution is usually incremental. A supermarket adds prepared food, a convenience store adds seating, a specialty retailer adds a smaller satellite format, each a response to a job the current store does not serve, as the jobs-to-be-done article advises for framing demand.
