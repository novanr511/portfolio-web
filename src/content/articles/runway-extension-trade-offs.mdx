<Lead>
When a company extends its runway, it is buying time to learn. The question is what that time costs and what must be true by the end of it. <Mark>A runway extension without a learning goal is a delay</Mark>, and delay has a price in dilution, interest, or lost focus, as [Burn Rate and Runway: The Founder's Daily Number](/articles/burn-rate-and-runway-the-founder-s-daily-number) treats runway as a deadline rather than a comfort.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Compare extensions by cost, conditions, and what they allow the company to prove, using the burn-rate calculation as the baseline. Investor education is explicit that returns are not guaranteed, and Damodaran's valuation work treats funding as a conditional decision. The boundary: an extension cannot create product-market fit, which the startup-validation article treats as the prior question, and it cannot remove a structural cash problem, which the working-capital article addresses as a business-model issue.

## The options

**Raise equity.** Dilution but no repayment obligation, with a valuation that depends on evidence, as the startup-valuation article explains for conditional estimates.

**Venture debt.** Less dilution but a fixed obligation and covenants, which the venture-debt article treats as the central risk, as the debt-restructuring article explains for breaches.

**Cut burn.** Extends runway without external capital but may remove the capacity to learn, which the operating-model article treats as a cost of reducing scope.

**Increase collections or price.** Improves net burn from the revenue side, as the working-capital article explains for cash conversion, without the conditions of external funding.

Three consequences follow. <Mark>Each option changes what the company can prove</Mark>, which the 13-week cash-flow article treats as an operating question. Each has a time limit, which the burn-rate article makes explicit. And the choice should be recorded with the assumption that justifies it, as the decision-memo article requires.

## A worked comparison

The following is hypothetical and illustrative. A company has 3 million in cash, a net burn of 500,000 a month, and six months of runway. <Mark>It needs fifteen months to reach a milestone</Mark> that would support a larger round.

Three options are considered. Cut burn to 300,000 by delaying two hires, which gives ten months but may slow the milestone, as the product-management article warns about capacity for the wrong problem. Raise 4 million of equity, which gives fourteen months at current burn with dilution set by the current valuation, as the cap-table article models. Raise 2 million of venture debt, which gives ten months with interest and a covenant to raise within eighteen months, as [Venture Debt Best Practices](/articles/venture-debt-best-practices) describes. Three checks decide. First, which option preserves the ability to reach the milestone, which the cash-flow article treats as the use of funds? Second, what is the cost in dilution, interest, and lost capacity, as the unit-economics article requires for a comparison? Third, what if the milestone slips, which the scenario-analysis article treats as a downside case?

Three conclusions follow.

<KeyPoint>
The cheapest option in cash may be the most expensive in lost time, which the burn-rate article implies for a longer runway.
</KeyPoint>

The equity option preserves flexibility at a dilution cost, as the cap-table article explains. And the decision should be recorded with the milestone assumption, as the decision-memo article requires.

## What a runway comparison cannot do

It cannot guarantee the milestone, which the startup-validation article treats as a hypothesis. It cannot remove market risk, which investor education states explicitly.

And it does not decide strategy: buying time for the wrong goal is still the wrong goal, as the strategy article argues for positioning before execution.

## A decision test

1. State the milestone the extra time must reach, as the startup-validation article advises.
2. Compute runway and forward burn, as [Burn Rate and Runway: The Founder's Daily Number](/articles/burn-rate-and-runway-the-founder-s-daily-number) requires.
3. Compare cost in dilution, interest, and capacity, as the unit-economics article demands.
4. Model what happens if the milestone slips, as the scenario-analysis article recommends.
5. Check covenants if debt is involved, as [Venture Debt Best Practices](/articles/venture-debt-best-practices) advises.
6. Preserve the ability to learn, not just to wait, as the product-management article argues.
7. Record the decision and the assumption, as the decision-memo article requires.
