Personal branding advice sells a feeling: show up, be authentic, build a narrative. That is hard to decide with. A better question is what identity claim the evidence supports, and where it runs out. This site offers a checkable case: one canonical name, a separate wordmark, sameAs links to two real profiles, and public work. I have not run a survey, recall test, or any brand measurement. Every figure below is labelled ASSUMED or DERIVED; none is a reported outcome.

## What does "getting personal branding right" mean?

It means deciding what your name should reliably resolve to, then making the evidence agree. A personal brand is not a mood or a posting cadence; it is a claim about which entity a reader and a machine are dealing with. Make the claim narrow enough to check, state what it excludes, and stop when the evidence stops.

Tom Peters's 1997 essay popularised the idea that everyone has a brand. It is a frame, not a finding about outcomes. The decision here is narrower: can a stranger resolve a name to one person and one body of work? If they cannot, narrative will not fix the ambiguity. Where evidence is missing, build it or say so.

That is the publication's lens: make a more honest decision with the evidence available. Here the evidence is structural — config fields, markup, public artifacts — not a survey result. The boundary is the subject of [what personal branding actually measures](/articles/what-personal-branding-actually-measures).

## What first-hand evidence does this site provide?

Three identity artifacts are inspectable here. The shared site config defines one canonical author name, Rachmadwipa Novandri, and a short display wordmark (`Novandri.`). The home page emits a Person node with sameAs links to the author's GitHub and LinkedIn. An audit collapsed three conflicting names attached to one author identity. No brand metric was measured.

The fields live in `src/lib/site.ts`: a short wordmark with a trailing period and a canonical name used in bylines, metadata, and Person markup. The home page in `src/app/page.tsx` adds the sameAs links. An audit found three strings — `Novandri`, `Novandri R.`, `Rachmadwipa Novandri` — used for the author identity, and I unified them on the full name.

Each item is a decision a reader can check in a file, not an impression I caused. I measured no recognition, recall, or citation change, so no result is reported. ## Why is a wordmark not a canonical name?

A wordmark is a display string; a canonical name is an identity claim. Here the wordmark is short and stylised, while the canonical name is the full string used in bylines, metadata, and Person markup. Keeping them in separate fields lets each stay stable at its own layer. Merging them is how one person becomes three names.

Schema.org defines sameAs as a URL that unambiguously indicates an item's identity. The W3C JSON-LD specification defines `@id` as a node identifier: blocks sharing one `@id` assert they describe the same entity. A validator checks syntax, not whether names agree. The contradiction is semantic, which is why the audit mattered.

I pointed sameAs only at profiles that belong to the entity: GitHub and LinkedIn. The fix is a correctness repair, not a visibility tactic; I claim no ranking or citation change. ## What can brand-equity research tell you here?

Aaker and Keller built instruments for branded products with identifiable consumers. Aaker treats brand equity as assets and liabilities tied to a name; Keller defines customer-based equity as the differential effect of brand knowledge on response. Those frames explain what a claim would need to mean. They do not supply a score here, because I ran no such study.

Aaker's 1996 framework treats awareness, differentiation, and associations as dimensions to build and maintain. Keller's definition is sharper: a differential effect needs a comparison, a defined population, and a measured response. A personal site with no panel, survey, or comparison condition has none of those. Borrowing the vocabulary and quoting a number would be a category error.

**ASSUMED:** a hypothetical survey of ten readers who know the site. **DERIVED:** if six recognised the canonical name, that would be a rate of 60% in that imagined sample. That arithmetic shows what a measurement would require — a denominator, a question, a population, and a date. It is not an observation about this site.

## How do you make a promise a reader can check?

Keep the promise to something verifiable: here is who wrote this, here is the work, here are the profiles. The site's about page, public case studies with repositories, and linked profiles do that job. An unverifiable adjective is not a promise. The test is whether a stranger can confirm the claim in minutes without taking your word for it.

Checkability changes the claim worth making. "Experienced practitioner" asks for trust in an adjective; a case study with a repository lets a reader inspect what was built. The strongest personal brand in this sense is a set of artifacts that survive a skeptical visit. Where none exists, the claim should be smaller.

Consistency is part of checkability: one name across bylines and markup means a reader following a link lands on the same person. That is structural, not evidence of an impression. I have no clients, awards, or credentials to add, and I do not invent them. See [getting started with personal branding](/articles/getting-started-with-personal-branding).

## What does a narrow identity claim cost?

A narrow claim buys checkability and gives up reach. If the name resolves to one person with specific work, it cannot also stand for every future interest. That trade is real: a coherent identity excludes opportunities as well as confusion. I made it by choosing four desks and standing exclusions in the editorial map, not by measuring demand.

Read as positioning, the map picks a dimension — a standard of thinking across technology, business, and ideas — instead of a topic list, and refuses verticals the author cannot support. The cost is topics left uncovered. A different author with different access would choose differently: the decision depends on the evidence actually held.

I did not survey readers, so I cannot claim the narrow position was demanded. The case rests on what I could support, not measured preference. ## How do you check an identity claim without a survey?

Use checks that a second person can repeat: does one canonical name appear everywhere the identity is shown, do the sameAs links resolve to profiles that belong to the entity, and does the public work exist? These are correctness checks, not brand measurement. They lower the chance of confusion; they say nothing about whether anyone remembered the name.

A repeatable check has four steps. Inspect the config for the canonical string and wordmark. Search the rendered markup for the Person node and confirm one `@id` and one name. Open each sameAs link and confirm ownership. List the public artifacts a reader could inspect, and date the check so drift is visible.

A passing check is a precondition, not an outcome. "No contradiction found" is different from "recognised by readers." Keeping those two statements apart is the whole discipline here. ## Where does this evidence run out?

It runs out at the reader. I have no recall figure, no survey, no applicant data, no media measurement, and no before-and-after citation rate. A consistent name and working links describe files and profiles, not impressions. Calling that brand strength would turn an identity decision into a claim the evidence cannot carry.

This scopes the work rather than dismissing it. Readers and answer engines both benefit from knowing which entity a page describes, and one name makes that easier. But entity clarity is structural, not a switch that forces a citation, and no measurement here establishes a visibility effect. See [AI search and technical SEO](/articles/ai-search-and-technical-seo-what-website-owners-should-do) and [keyword research for AI search answers](/articles/keyword-research-for-ai-search-answers).

The useful question is not "is my brand strong?" but "which identity claim can I defend with the evidence I have?" The first invites a number I do not have; the second produces a decision I can show. The counterargument — that unmeasured consistency is tidiness — is fair. Keep the layers labelled rather than announce a result.

## How do you decide what to claim?

List the identity artifacts you can actually point to, choose one canonical string for the entity, keep the display name separate, point outward links only at profiles you own, and publish work a stranger can inspect. Then write down which questions you did not measure. Claim the structure; leave the impression unclaimed. That is the honest decision.

The decision test, in order:

1. Name the entity and write down the one canonical string it will use everywhere.
2. Keep the display wordmark in its own field with its own rule.
3. Point sameAs only at profiles that genuinely belong to that entity.
4. Publish artifacts a stranger can inspect, and keep the promise to what they show.
5. State the exclusion — what the identity does not claim to be.
6. Run the repeatable checks, record the date, and report what they found.
7. List the unmeasured questions — recall, perception, reach — and leave them unanswered.

A coherent identity is worth building. It is not a measured brand; conflating the two turns a checkable decision into a story the evidence cannot support. The identity system is described in [visual identity systems](/articles/visual-identity-systems-more-than-a-logo).
