<Lead>
Support is where AI assistance is most visible and most easily measured. Three points matter: routing the ticket to the right queue, drafting a reply from the knowledge base, and deciding when a person must take over. Each has a different failure mode and a different metric, which is why treating them as one "AI support" feature produces confusing results.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Use AI to classify and route tickets, draft replies grounded in the knowledge base, and flag cases for human handoff — with a distinct metric for each stage. Anthropic's customer-support agent guide is the primary use-case reference, and the O*NET task descriptions help identify what the work actually involves. The boundary: a fluent reply is not a correct one, and a handoff that loses context is worse than no automation, as [How to Build an AI Chatbot That Does Not Fail](/articles/how-to-build-an-ai-chatbot-that-does-not-fail) explains.

## Three points

**Routing.** Classify the ticket into a queue with a <Mark>confidence score</Mark>. Errors are recoverable — the ticket can be rerouted — but they cost time, as the classification-pattern article explains.

**Drafting.** Produce a reply from the knowledge base with a citation, so an agent can check it quickly. The reduce-hallucinations article covers the grounding mechanism. A draft sent without review is a different risk category.

**Handoff.** Decide when a person must take over — low confidence, an angry customer, a money-related decision — and pass the <Mark>structured state</Mark>, not just the transcript, as [How to Build an AI Chatbot That Does Not Fail](/articles/how-to-build-an-ai-chatbot-that-does-not-fail) recommends.

## A worked workflow

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A support team handles 3,000 tickets a day. The AI workflow:

1. Routes tickets into four queues with a confidence score. Low-confidence cases go to a triage queue.
2. Drafts replies for the two highest-volume queues, with a cited source passage.
3. Escalates to a person when confidence is low, the customer asks for a human, or the topic involves refunds.

Measured over a month:

- 85% of tickets routed correctly on first pass; 5% needed rerouting.
- Drafts accepted with minor edits in 60% of eligible cases.
- Escalation rate of 25%, with context carried in the handoff.

Three checks decide the result:

1. **Rerouting time** — do the 5% misrouted tickets cost more than routing saves? The workflow-automation article explains the arithmetic.
2. **Edit distance** — are the accepted drafts genuinely close, or are agents rewriting them while marking them accepted? The evaluation-rubric article warns about proxy metrics.
3. **Handoff quality** — do escalated tickets arrive with structured context, so customers do not repeat themselves, as [Human in the Loop by Design: Overrides, Escalation, and Failure States](/articles/human-in-the-loop-by-design) requires.

Three conclusions follow:

- **Each stage needs its own metric**, and a single "AI satisfaction score" would hide a failing stage.
- **The handoff is a control**, not a fallback, which the security checklist explains for tool boundaries.
- **Low-confidence routing is a feature**, reducing wrong replies at the cost of more human work, as [How to Build an AI Chatbot That Does Not Fail](/articles/how-to-build-an-ai-chatbot-that-does-not-fail) argues.

## What to instrument

<KeyPoint>
A support AI system needs metrics at each stage, and the right ones differ from generic model scores.
</KeyPoint>

Four are worth tracking:

**Routing accuracy by queue.** Aggregate accuracy hides a queue that consistently receives the wrong tickets, which the funnel article warns about for blended rates.

**Draft acceptance with edit distance.**

<Trap>
A draft marked accepted after heavy rewriting is not a saving.
</Trap>

Measure how much text actually changed, as the evaluation-rubric article explains for proxy metrics.

**Handoff completeness.** Does the escalated ticket carry the structured state — issue, attempts, sentiment — or just the chat log? The chatbot article explains why context loss is the failure to watch.

**Customer effort.** Track <Mark>repeat contacts</Mark> on the same issue, which the retention article identifies as a leading indicator of a problem that was closed but not solved.

These metrics make the combined system visible, which the evaluation article argues is the only meaningful unit of analysis.

## What support AI cannot do

**It cannot replace accountability.** A person must own the reply and the decision, as the governance checklist requires.

**It cannot fix a weak knowledge base.** A draft grounded in outdated documentation gives confident wrong answers, which the data-governance article covers.

**It cannot handle every customer.** Some conversations need empathy and judgement that a system should not simulate, as the humanizer guidance on voice would caution in a different context — here the point is simply that escalation is legitimate.

The counterargument — that support AI reduces headcount — is a business framing that ignores the shift in work.

<PullQuote>
Agents move from writing routine replies to handling harder cases, which may require more skill and better tools, as the future-of-work article discusses.
</PullQuote>

## A decision test

1. **Separate routing, drafting, and handoff** with a metric for each.
2. **Ground drafts in a cited source** from the knowledge base.
3. **Define escalation triggers** explicitly, including low confidence and high-stakes topics.
4. **Carry structured context** across the handoff.
5. **Audit the rerouted and escalated cases**, not just the successful ones.
6. **Keep the knowledge base current** with an owner.
7. **Measure combined-system outcomes**, as the evaluation article recommends.
