<Lead>
Product management is often described as the role between engineering and business, which sounds like coordination and misses the substance. The job is to <Mark>decide what problems are worth solving, in what order, with what evidence</Mark> — and to make those decisions legible to the people who must execute them.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Effective product management owns the problem definition, the evidence for prioritisation, and the measurement of outcomes, not the backlog itself. The product-management overview provides the discipline's context, and O*NET's task descriptions help ground the work in real activities. The boundary: a process cannot create a good product idea or a clear strategy, which the value-proposition article argues is the prior decision, and frameworks do not replace evidence, as the AI-product-management article treats use cases as hypotheses.

## The decisions

**What problem, for whom.** A specific reader and a consequential question, which the publication standard requires for articles and applies here to products. The jobs-to-be-done article frames this as a situation the customer is in, not a demographic.

**Why now.** Prioritisation should reflect value, evidence, and cost, as the engineering-management article treats competing demands.

<Trap>
A roadmap ordered by whoever asked loudest is not a decision, as the decision-memo article argues for recorded reasoning.
</Trap>

**What would prove it worked.** An outcome metric with a baseline and a window, which the evaluation-rubric article requires for concrete evidence. Shipping a feature is an activity; <Mark>changing a customer behaviour is an outcome</Mark>, as the funnel article distinguishes stages.

Three consequences follow.

<KeyPoint>
The product manager needs evidence before a roadmap, which the market-research article covers for customer input.
</KeyPoint>

The decisions must be communicated, as the internal-communications article argues for making trade-offs legible. And they must be revisited when evidence changes, which the monitoring article treats as a review trigger.

## A worked example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative. A team has three candidate projects: a billing fix, a new onboarding flow, and a reporting feature requested by a large customer.

The product manager defines the problem for each with evidence: billing errors cause support contacts, as [Voice of Customer: Turning Feedback into Decisions](/articles/voice-of-customer-turning-feedback-into-decisions) recommends for grouping feedback; onboarding stalls before first value, which the funnel article measures as activation; and the reporting request comes from one account, which the segmentation article treats as a question of representativeness. Three checks decide the order. First, what is the value and how concentrated, as the unit-economics article asks for margin and segment? Second, what evidence exists that the proposed solution helps, which the startup-validation article treats as a hypothesis to test? Third, what is the opportunity cost of each, as the engineering-management article frames capacity?

Three conclusions follow. The billing fix is justified by repeated contacts with a measurable cost, as [Voice of Customer: Turning Feedback into Decisions](/articles/voice-of-customer-turning-feedback-into-decisions) advises. The onboarding flow is tested before full build, as the trial-design article recommends for a bounded experiment. The single-account request is logged rather than built, with the reasoning recorded, as the decision-memo article requires.

## What product management cannot do

It cannot create demand by announcement, which the demand-generation article argues is preference built before capture. It cannot fix a weak product with a process, as the value-proposition article treats the offer as the buyer's real evaluation. And it is not the same as project management: <Mark>delivery without the right problem is efficient irrelevance</Mark>, which the strategy article warns about for operational effectiveness without position.

<PullQuote>
The counterargument that product management is just common sense is fair about the individual decisions and wrong about the discipline: doing them consistently, with evidence and recorded reasoning, is the work, as the decision-memo article argues for records that change behaviour.
</PullQuote>

## A decision test

1. Define the problem and the reader before the solution.
2. Gather evidence from support, interviews, and behaviour, as [Voice of Customer: Turning Feedback into Decisions](/articles/voice-of-customer-turning-feedback-into-decisions) recommends.
3. Prioritise by value and evidence, not loudness, as the engineering-management article advises.
4. Set an outcome metric with a baseline, as the evaluation-rubric article requires.
5. Test the riskiest assumption before full build, as the startup-validation article recommends.
6. Record the decision and the trade-off, as the decision-memo article requires.
7. Revisit on new evidence, as the monitoring article advises.
