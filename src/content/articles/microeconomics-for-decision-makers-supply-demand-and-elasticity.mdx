Markets are not mysterious. They are the result of millions of individual choices.

**Direct answer:** Microeconomics studies how individuals and firms allocate scarce resources and how those choices determine prices and quantities in specific markets. The core tools—supply, demand, equilibrium, and elasticity—explain why prices move, who bears the cost of taxes, and how markets respond to change.

## Demand: what buyers want

The demand curve shows how much of a product buyers want at different prices. Generally, as price falls, quantity demanded rises. Demand shifts when:

- **Income changes:** more income usually means more demand for normal goods.
- **Preferences change:** trends, health concerns, or new information alter tastes.
- **Prices of related goods change:** substitutes (tea vs. coffee) and complements (coffee vs. sugar) affect each other.
- **Expectations:** fear of future price hikes can accelerate current buying.

## Supply: what sellers offer

The supply curve shows how much producers are willing to sell at different prices. Generally, as price rises, quantity supplied rises. Supply shifts when:

- **Input costs change:** cheaper raw materials increase supply at every price.
- **Technology improves:** better production methods lower costs.
- **Regulation changes:** taxes or subsidies shift supply.
- **Number of sellers changes:** more competitors increase market supply.

## Equilibrium: where they meet

Market equilibrium occurs where supply equals demand. At that price, there is no surplus (unsold goods) or shortage (unmet demand). Prices tend to move toward equilibrium:

- **Price above equilibrium:** surplus puts downward pressure on price.
- **Price below equilibrium:** shortage puts upward pressure on price.

Government price controls (floors and ceilings) disrupt this process, creating persistent surpluses or shortages.

## Elasticity: how responsive are buyers and sellers?

Elasticity measures how much quantity demanded or supplied responds to price changes.

- **Elastic demand:** a small price change causes a large quantity change (luxuries, goods with many substitutes).
- **Inelastic demand:** price changes have little effect on quantity (necessities, addictive goods, medicines).
- **Unit elastic:** percentage change in price equals percentage change in quantity.

Elasticity determines who bears the burden of a tax. If demand is inelastic, consumers bear most of a tax through higher prices. If supply is inelastic, producers bear more.

## Marginal thinking

Economists think at the margin: should you produce one more unit, hire one more worker, or spend one more dollar on advertising? The optimal decision compares marginal benefit to marginal cost. Produce until marginal benefit equals marginal cost.

## Limits of microeconomics

Microeconomics assumes rational actors and perfect information. Real people and firms are influenced by bias, incomplete information, and social factors. Use microeconomics as a framework, not as a perfect predictor of behavior.
