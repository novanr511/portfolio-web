AI in human resources ranges from harmless — drafting an onboarding email — to consequential — ranking job applicants. The difference is the impact on a person and the legal and ethical obligations that come with it. A useful principle: the more a system affects someone's opportunity, the more explicit its controls and oversight must be.

**Direct answer:** Use AI for bounded HR tasks such as summarising applications against a defined rubric, drafting onboarding material, and flagging inconsistencies — with human decision-making, bias testing, and documented criteria for anything affecting employment. Anthropic's use-case guides cover relevant patterns, and O*NET provides occupation and skill descriptions for defining what a role requires. The boundary: a model can rank or summarise, but the decision and its justification must remain with a person, as the AI-ethics and governance articles argue for high-impact domains.

## Three uses

**Screening support.** Summarise applications against a defined rubric — the requirements of the role, stated as observable criteria. The rubric must come from the job, not from the model's assumptions about a "good candidate", which the O*NET task descriptions help ground.

**Onboarding.** Draft material, answer routine questions, and track completion. This is low-risk, as the workflow-automation article explains, provided the content is accurate.

**Consistency checks.** Flag where two candidates were assessed against different criteria or where a required step was skipped, which the internal-audit logic of the data-governance article supports.

## A worked screening example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A team uses AI to summarise 500 applications against a five-point rubric derived from the job description. The workflow:

1. Define the rubric from the role's actual tasks, with each criterion observable in an application.
2. Produce a structured summary per candidate with the evidence for each criterion, as the structured-output article explains.
3. A recruiter reviews the summaries and makes the decision.

Three checks decide whether it is defensible:

1. **Rubric relevance.** Does each criterion predict performance in the role, rather than reflecting a proxy for something else? The future-of-work article argues for tying skills to real tasks, which O*NET descriptions support.
2. **Bias testing.** Are outcomes distributed differently across protected groups at the summary or ranking stage? The model-monitoring article covers detecting bias drift, which the responsible-AI article treats as a core obligation.
3. **Human decision quality.** Does the recruiter engage with the evidence, or defer to the summary? The human-in-the-loop article explains why a rubber-stamp review is not a control.

Three conclusions follow:

- **The rubric is the control**, not the model — a bad criterion produces biased summaries consistently.
- **Bias testing is ongoing**, because inputs and models change, which the monitoring article covers.
- **The decision stays with a person**, who can be accountable for it, as the governance checklist requires.

## Making bias testing real

A commitment to fairness is not a control; a measurement is. Three practices make bias testing operational:

**Choose the outcome to test.** Decide whether you are checking summary scores, ranking positions, or final decisions, as the model-monitoring article distinguishes the layers. Testing only the final decision can miss a biased upstream signal that a person then amplifies.

**Test at intervals and after changes.** Distributions shift as applicant pools and models change. The monitoring article recommends scheduled checks with defined triggers, which the governance checklist reinforces for high-impact systems.

**Act on findings.** A test that shows a disparity needs an owner, a remediation, and a retest, which the incident-response article describes for other failure modes. A finding with no action is a report, not a control.

These practices are demanding, which is why high-impact uses should start small — low-stakes onboarding before consequential screening, as the risk-assessment article recommends.

## What HR AI cannot do

**It cannot define job relevance.** That is a decision about the work, grounded in task analysis, which O*NET's occupational data supports but does not make for you.

**It cannot remove bias.** It can encode or amplify it, which the AI-ethics article explains. Bias controls are active work, not a property of the tool.

**It cannot be accountable.** Responsibility for an employment decision belongs to a person and an organisation, as the responsible-AI and regulatory-compliance articles argue.

The counterargument — that humans are biased too — is true and misses the point. A system that scales a decision needs more scrutiny, not less, because its errors are systematic and repeatable, which the risk-assessment article explains.

## A decision test

1. **Define criteria from the role's tasks**, not assumptions.
2. **Keep the decision with a person** who can justify it.
3. **Test outcomes across relevant groups** on a schedule and after changes.
4. **Show the evidence** for each summary or ranking, as the explainability article recommends.
5. **Audit for rubric drift** when job descriptions change, which the monitoring article covers.
6. **Document the process** with an owner, as the governance checklist requires.
7. **Check current employment law** in your jurisdiction, which the blockchain-adjacent regulatory discussions treat as a date-sensitive question more generally.
