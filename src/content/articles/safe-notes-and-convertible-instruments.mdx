An early investor and a founder often cannot agree on a valuation, but both want to move. Convertible instruments — SAFEs and convertible notes — defer the valuation to a future priced round. That solves the immediate deadlock while creating a different set of questions about who converts at what price and with what rights.

**Direct answer:** A SAFE is an agreement that converts into equity at a future financing, typically using a valuation cap or a discount; a convertible note is debt that converts on similar terms. Investor education covers these as investment products with their own risks, and the term-sheet article treats the eventual priced round as the moment the terms become concrete. The boundary: deferring valuation does not remove it — it transfers the question to a later date with different information, and the cap-table article explains the resulting dilution.

## The mechanism

**A valuation cap** sets the maximum valuation at which the instrument converts. An investor with a 4 million cap who invests 400,000 converts at a price no higher than if the company were valued at 4 million, which protects the investor if the round values the company higher.

**A discount** gives the early investor a percentage reduction on the price paid in the next round, commonly expressed as 20%.

**A conversion trigger** is the priced round itself. Until then, the holder is not a shareholder in the same sense, which affects control and the cap table, as [Cap Table Mechanics: Equity, Options, and Dilution](/articles/cap-table-mechanics-equity-options-and-dilution) explains for recording instruments correctly.

Three consequences follow. The instrument is simpler and faster than a priced round, which is why it is common at the earliest stage. It is not free of negotiation — the cap and discount are the negotiation, as [Term Sheet Basics: What Founders Should Read Twice](/articles/term-sheet-basics-what-founders-should-read-twice) argues for reading terms, not labels. And it creates uncertainty about ownership until conversion, which the valuation article treats as a reason to model scenarios.

## A worked conversion

The following is hypothetical and illustrative. An investor puts 400,000 into a SAFE with a 4 million cap and a 20% discount. The next priced round values the company at 10 million.

Using the cap, the conversion price is based on 4 million, giving the investor 10% of the company. Using the discount, the price is based on 8 million, giving 5%. The cap is more favourable, so it applies, and the investor converts at 10%. The founder's ownership is diluted by that 10% plus the new round, which the cap-table article models for cumulative effects.

Three checks decide whether this was a good deal. First, was the cap realistic relative to the company's prospects, which the startup-valuation article treats as a judgement with explicit assumptions? A very low cap transfers too much ownership. Second, did the instrument include a "most favoured nation" or similar clause that changes terms if later investors get better ones, as [Term Sheet Basics: What Founders Should Read Twice](/articles/term-sheet-basics-what-founders-should-read-twice) warns about hidden terms? Third, did the conversion happen on the expected timeline, which the burn-rate article frames as a financing-dependency question?

Three conclusions follow. The cap, not the discount, decided this outcome, so the negotiation should have focused there. The delay cost the founder ownership relative to a higher-cap deal, which is the price of resolving the earlier disagreement. And the result must be recorded in the cap table with a date, as the data-governance article requires for relied-upon records.

## What convertibles cannot do

They cannot remove disagreement about value; they postpone it, as the valuation article explains for conditional estimates. They cannot replace legal advice on the specific terms and jurisdiction, which the copyright-and-AI article treats analogously for date-sensitive law. And they cannot fix a weak business: a convertible instrument finances a company, it does not make one viable, which the startup-validation article treats as the prior question.

The counterargument that convertibles are simpler and therefore always better ignores the cost of an unfavourable cap, which the worked example shows can be substantial.

## A decision test

1. State why valuation cannot be agreed now.
2. Compare the cap and discount against realistic scenarios, as the valuation article recommends.
3. Read the conversion trigger and any most-favoured clauses, as [Term Sheet Basics: What Founders Should Read Twice](/articles/term-sheet-basics-what-founders-should-read-twice) advises.
4. Model ownership after conversion with the cap table.
5. Check the effect on the next round, as [Cap Table Mechanics: Equity, Options, and Dilution](/articles/cap-table-mechanics-equity-options-and-dilution) explains.
6. Obtain qualified legal review of the jurisdiction-specific terms.
7. Record the instrument and its eventual conversion with dates.
