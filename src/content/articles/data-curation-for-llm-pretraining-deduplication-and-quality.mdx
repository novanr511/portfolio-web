Pretraining data is often treated as a volume problem — collect more text and train longer. The composition of that text matters as much as its size. A corpus full of near-duplicates trains a model to memorise common passages; one with systematic gaps produces blind spots no amount of compute fills.

**Direct answer:** Data curation for pretraining means removing duplicates, filtering low-quality content, and balancing the sources before training. The paper *Deduplicating Training Data Makes Language Models Better* reports that both exact and near-duplicate removal improve model quality and reduce memorisation. The boundary: curation choices are judgements about what counts as quality, and they encode values — a filtered corpus is not a neutral one, as the datasheets article explains.

## Why deduplication matters

Three effects follow from repeated content:

**Memorisation.** A passage seen many times in training is more likely to be reproduced verbatim at inference, which raises privacy and copyright concerns.

**Distorted frequencies.** If one source appears a hundred times, the model overweights its phrasing and facts. A corpus is a sample, and duplicates bias the sample.

**Wasted compute.** Tokens spent re-reading the same material are tokens not spent on new information, which matters at training scale.

The deduplication paper found improvements from both exact matching and near-duplicate detection, which suggests that paraphrases matter as well as identical copies.

## Beyond duplicates

**Quality filtering.** Removing content that is malformed, spam, or low-information. The risk is that a filter trained on one definition of quality discards legitimate material from other domains or dialects.

**Source balance.** A corpus dominated by a few large sources will reflect their perspective. Balancing is a deliberate choice with consequences for what the model knows.

**Toxicity and safety filtering.** Removing harmful content reduces some risks and can also remove material needed to recognise and refuse harmful requests. The trade-off belongs in the datasheet.

**Provenance.** Recording where each portion came from and what processing it received, so a later evaluation can interpret the model's behaviour, as [What Is Model Collapse and Why It Matters](/articles/what-is-model-collapse-and-why-it-matters) argues.

## A worked curation decision

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A team has 2 billion tokens of web text and a budget to curate before pretraining. Three options:

- **No curation.** Train on everything. Cheap, but duplicates and low-quality pages consume capacity.
- **Exact deduplication only.** Removes identical documents. Fast, but near-duplicates remain.
- **Exact plus near-deduplication and source balancing.** More expensive, but the deduplication paper suggests a quality gain and reduced memorisation.

Three checks decide:

1. **Measure duplicate prevalence.** If 20% of the corpus is duplicated, curation frees significant capacity. If it is 1%, the effort may not pay.
2. **Check what the filter removes.** Sample the discarded documents. If a near-duplicate filter is removing legitimate variation — different reviews of the same product, for example — the threshold is too aggressive.
3. **Evaluate on a held-out real corpus.** A model trained on curated data should be assessed on human-written text it did not see, not on a test set drawn from the same filtered distribution.

Three conclusions follow:

- **Curation has a measurable cost and a measurable benefit**; decide with both in view.
- **Filters encode values**, so record what was removed and why.
- **A held-out human corpus is the anchor**, since a filtered test set flatters the filtered training set.

## The operational side of curation

Curation is a pipeline, not a one-time cleanup. Three practices keep it from drifting:

**Version the corpus.** Record which documents were included, which filters were applied, and when. A model trained on version 2 should be comparable with one trained on version 3, which is impossible if the corpus changed silently.

**Audit the filters periodically.** A quality filter trained on one distribution will change behaviour as the web changes. Sample what it removes each quarter and check that the rule still matches the intent.

**Keep a small gold set.** A human-reviewed sample of the corpus, labelled as high or low quality, gives a stable reference for evaluating a filter change. Without it, a filter update is judged by the model's downstream behaviour, which is slow and confounded.

These habits are unglamorous and decisive. Most curation failures are not a wrong algorithm but a filter nobody re-examined after the data changed.

## What curation cannot do

**It cannot add knowledge that was never collected.** Filtering improves a distribution; it does not create missing domains.

**It cannot define quality objectively.** A filter is a model of quality with its own biases, as the datasheets paper argues.

**It cannot fix a bad objective.** A well-curated corpus trained with the wrong objective still produces a model that optimises the wrong thing.

The counterargument — that more data always helps — is true only under a fixed quality distribution. When composition changes, the relationship breaks, which is why model cards and datasheets exist as documentation of the data behind a system.

## A decision test

1. **Measure duplicate prevalence** before choosing a method.
2. **Sample what the filter removes** and check for legitimate variation.
3. **Balance sources deliberately** and record the reasoning.
4. **Document provenance** for every portion of the corpus.
5. **Evaluate on held-out human text**, not filtered data.
6. **Record the curation decisions** in a datasheet.
7. **Re-evaluate after a corpus change**, not only after a model change.

For the failure mode, see [Model Collapse](/articles/what-is-model-collapse-and-why-it-matters); for the documentation standard, see [Datasheets for Datasets](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09010).
