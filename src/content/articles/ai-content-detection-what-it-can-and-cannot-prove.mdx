A teacher submits a student essay to a detector and receives "98% AI-generated". The number looks like evidence. It is not a probability that the student used AI — it is the output of a classifier that can be wrong in both directions, and the research on reliability explains why.

**Direct answer:** Research on AI-text detection found that detectors are not reliably robust to attacks and can produce false positives, especially for non-native writing. Watermarking offers a different property: a statistical signal embedded at generation time, which can prove origin only if the provider applied it and the text was not substantially edited. The boundary: neither detection nor watermarking establishes authorship or quality, and neither should be the sole basis for a consequential decision.

## What detectors actually measure

A detector is a classifier trained to distinguish text from a reference distribution. It looks for statistical patterns — predictability, token choice, sentence structure — and outputs a score. Three limitations follow:

**The score is not a probability of guilt.** A "98%" output means the classifier placed the text far from its human-written reference. It does not mean there is a 2% chance of a human writing it.

**Distribution shift breaks it.** Text from a domain or author unlike the training reference can be flagged. Research on detection reliability documents false positives and the effect of adversarial paraphrasing.

**Attacks degrade it.** Simple edits — paraphrasing, adding errors, translation — can move text across the classifier's boundary. A detector that fails under trivial modification is a weak control.

The paper *Can AI-Generated Text be Reliably Detected?* is the clearest reference for these limits. It is not an argument that detection is impossible in every setting; it is an argument that reliability has been overstated.

## What watermarking offers

A watermark embeds a statistical pattern in generated text, as described in *A Watermark for Large Language Models*. Three properties distinguish it from detection:

**It is applied at generation.** The provider must cooperate, and the text must carry the signal from the start.

**It supports a statistical test.** A verifier can test whether the pattern is present, with a false-positive rate that can be stated. That is more defensible than a classifier's confidence.

**It is fragile to editing.** Rewriting, heavy paraphrasing, or mixing human and generated text can remove the signal. Absence of a watermark does not prove human authorship.

Watermarking is therefore useful for a narrow question — did this text come from a provider that watermarks? — and not for the broader question of who wrote it.

## A worked example of a policy decision

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A university considers using a detector to flag AI-assisted essays. It runs a trial on 1,000 known essays: 500 human-written, 500 generated.

- The detector flags 480 of the generated essays correctly — 96% true positives.
- It also flags 50 of the human essays — a 10% false-positive rate.

Three consequences follow:

1. **Base rates dominate.** If only 5% of submissions are actually AI-generated, the vast majority of flags are false positives. In a class of 1,000, that is roughly 50 true cases and 95 false ones.
2. **The cost is asymmetric.** A false accusation damages a student and consumes appeal time. A missed case is a policy violation that may never be detected. The threshold should reflect that asymmetry, which means being far more conservative than "flag when score > 0.5".
3. **A score alone is not a process.** Any flag must lead to a human review that considers evidence beyond the detector — drafts, writing history, an oral explanation. The detector is a prompt for investigation, not a verdict.

Three decisions follow:

- **Do not use a detector as automated evidence.** Use it to prioritise review, with a stated false-positive rate.
- **Publish the limitation** so students and staff know what the tool can establish.
- **Prefer process controls** — drafting history, in-class writing, clear policies — which are more reliable than detection.

## What neither approach proves

**Not quality.** AI-generated text can be accurate or wrong; human text can be brilliant or plagiarised. Detection says nothing about either.

**Not intent.** A student may have used a tool for spelling and not for ideas. A score cannot distinguish the two.

**Not authorship in a legal sense.** Ownership and responsibility are legal and institutional questions. A statistical test is not a determination.

The counterargument — that doing nothing is worse — is fair. The response is not to rely on a weak detector but to combine process controls, transparent policy, and human judgement, with the detector as one weak signal among several.

## A decision test

1. **State the question** you want the tool to answer — origin, quality, or intent.
2. **Check whether the tool actually answers it.** Detection answers neither quality nor intent.
3. **Measure false positives on your population**, not on the vendor's demo set.
4. **Account for the base rate** of the behaviour you are looking for.
5. **Set a conservative threshold** reflecting the cost of a false accusation.
6. **Require human review** with evidence beyond the score.
7. **Prefer process controls** where the stakes are high.
