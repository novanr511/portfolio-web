Conjoint analysis is often used to decide what to build, which overstates what a stated-choice survey can tell you. The Wikipedia entry describes measuring preferences across attributes, and AAPOR's standards cover wording and transparent reporting. The trade-offs concern the gap between a survey choice and a real decision.

**Direct answer:** Use conjoint analysis to understand relative preferences, then validate the result against real behaviour before committing to a product decision. The sources describe the method; the rule is a synthesis.

**Delta:** This article adds a claim-status table and a worked example, separating documented method from local judgements about design.

## The mechanism

The first point is the task. The conjoint entry describes presenting combinations of attributes and asking for choices, which produces relative preferences under the conditions of the survey. A respondent who chooses a cheaper option in a survey may choose differently when paying, and the gap is a property of stated methods.

The second point is design. AAPOR's standards require neutral wording and transparent reporting, which applies to the attributes and levels offered. A design that omits a realistic option forces a choice that would not exist in the market, and the result will reflect the design.

The third point is validation. The output is a model of preferences, not a prediction of sales. Checking it against an observed choice, a pilot, or a real purchase is the step that turns an interesting result into a usable one.

The sequence is: define the decision, choose realistic attributes, field the survey neutrally, then validate. The choices are local, so the conclusions are `inferred`; the method is `source-reported`.

## A worked example

*Illustrative.* A team uses conjoint analysis to choose the price of a new product.

Three checks decide. First, were the attributes and levels realistic, including the options customers actually have? Second, was the wording neutral, and was the sample reported? Third, does the result agree with a pilot or an observed purchase, or does it only describe a hypothetical choice?

The outcome is a preference estimate with a stated limit. The scenario is hypothetical; the method is the contribution.

## Why a survey choice is not a purchase

A respondent in a survey faces no budget consequence, which changes the weight of price. The result is useful for comparing attributes, and weak as a forecast of sales, which is the distinction that a product decision has to respect.

A further consideration is that preferences are not stable. A result collected before a competitor changes its offer describes the old market, and a record of the fielding date is part of interpreting it. Reviewing the design when the market changes is part of the practice.

The method also has a cost in attention. A conjoint survey with many attributes is tiring, and respondents may simplify their choices near the end, which introduces a pattern that belongs to the instrument. Keeping the task short is a design decision that protects the data.

The third limitation is that a preference model cannot create a product. It ranks combinations that were shown to respondents, which means an option nobody thought to include cannot emerge. Treating the result as the full space of possibilities is the error to avoid.

## A decision test

1. Define the decision the study supports.
2. Choose realistic attributes and levels.
3. Word the task neutrally.
4. Report the sample.
5. Validate against observed behaviour.
6. Record the result and recheck date.
