<Lead>
An AI code reviewer can spot a null check, a likely race condition, and a missing test in seconds. It cannot tell whether a change implements the right product decision. The useful way to deploy it is to assign it the pattern-matching work and leave the intent work to people.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Anthropic's use-case guides cover code-related assistance, and HELM's multi-metric approach is a reminder that performance must be measured on the specific task. The boundary: an AI reviewer's comments are suggestions, not verdicts — a false positive costs reviewer time, and a false negative is invisible until production.

**Delta:** This article adds a decision framing beyond the sources: it maps general AI use cases to a concrete logistics task decision, with claim status explicit.

<KeyPoint>
AI review helps on local, well-defined patterns — error handling, common vulnerabilities, style, test coverage — and misses issues that depend on business context, system-wide design, or trade-offs.
</KeyPoint>

## What it catches

**Local correctness patterns.** Missing null checks, unchecked returns, resource leaks, and off-by-one errors are often visible in a diff.

**Security smells.** Hard-coded secrets, unsanitised input, weak cryptography, and common injection patterns are <Mark>well-represented in training data</Mark>.

**Consistency.** Naming, style, and adherence to project conventions can be checked against examples, as the structured-output article explains for enforcing patterns.

**Test coverage gaps.** A new branch with no corresponding test is detectable from the diff, even if the test's quality needs a person.

## What it misses

**Intent.** Whether the change solves the problem the ticket described requires <Mark>context from the ticket, the product, and the users</Mark>.

**Systemic design.** A change may be locally correct and accumulate technical debt across the system, as the technical-debt article explains.

**Novel bugs.** A failure mode nobody has described in text is unlikely to be in the model's pattern library.

**Trade-offs.** Choosing a simpler implementation with higher latency, or a faster one with more complexity, <Mark>is a decision, not a pattern</Mark>.

## A worked review workflow

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A team adds AI review to its pull-request pipeline on 200 pull requests a month.

**Stage 1 — automated suggestions.** The AI reviewer comments on error handling, security patterns, and missing tests. Reviewers mark each comment as useful or noise.

**Stage 2 — measurement.** After 100 pull requests, 300 comments were made; 60% were marked useful. The 40% noise rate is high enough that reviewers are slowing down — a problem the review-fatigue discussion in the human-in-the-loop article warns about.

**Stage 3 — tuning.** The team restricts the reviewer to security patterns and test coverage, where usefulness was highest, and adds project-specific examples. False positives fall, and review time drops.

Three lessons follow:

1. **Precision matters more than coverage.**
2. **Scope should follow evidence.** The team kept the categories that worked rather than accepting all comments.
3. **Human review remains the decision point.** The AI flags; a person judges, as the oversight article explains.

<Trap>
A reviewer that produces noise is ignored, and an ignored reviewer adds no value.
</Trap>

## What to measure

**Useful-comment rate** — what fraction of suggestions reviewers accept or act on.

**Missed-issue rate** — measure periodically by having humans review a sample without AI comments, which the evaluation-rubric article recommends for catching false negatives.

**Review time** — the net effect on the team's cycle time, not the number of comments generated.

**Defect escape rate** — whether bugs reach production less often after the tool was introduced, which the observability article covers as an outcome metric.

## What AI review cannot do

**It cannot own a decision.** Responsibility for a merge stays with a person, which the governance article requires.

**It cannot replace tests.** A pattern check is not an execution, as the testing discipline in the MLOps article explains.

**It is not free.** Inference cost and reviewer time spent dismissing comments are real, as the cost estimator shows.

<PullQuote>
The counterargument — that AI reviewers will keep improving and replace human review — is plausible at the margin and wrong about responsibility.
</PullQuote>

Even a highly accurate suggestion system does not hold accountability for a merge.

## A decision test

1. **Choose the review categories** — start with security and missing tests.
2. **Label usefulness** of each comment over a sample period.
3. **Restrict scope** to categories that earn their keep.
4. **Measure human review time** and defect escape rate.
5. **Keep a human decision point** on every merge.
6. **Re-evaluate after a model change**, as HELM's cross-model comparisons imply.
7. **Document the tool's role** so reviewers know what it does not do.
