Segmentation decks often divide customers into neat groups that never appear in a sales conversation. The segments are defined by data the seller cannot see, or they do not imply a different action. A segment that survives contact with sales is one a seller can recognise from a few signals and use to change what they say or do.

**Direct answer:** Build segments from differences that predict a decision and are observable at the point of contact. AAPOR's standards apply when survey data defines the segments, and demand-generation principles explain which decision a segment changes. The boundary: a segment is a model of a group, not a fact about an individual, and treating it as a script for people is a failure, as the personas article warns.

## What makes a segment usable

**Observable signals.** A seller should be able to recognise the segment from information available before a conversation — a trigger, a behaviour, a stated need, as the lead-scoring article discusses for qualification signals.

**A different action.** If the same pitch follows for every segment, the segmentation has no operational value, which the value-proposition article treats as a failure to state what changes for the buyer.

**A decision basis.** The segment should inform a product, pricing, or messaging decision, as the pricing-strategy article argues for tying differences to what the business can change.

## A worked example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A B2B company segments by firmographics — industry and company size — and finds no difference in conversion. It rebuilds segments around a question asked at signup: "What are you trying to change in the next 90 days?"

Three segments emerge:

- **Compliance-driven** — must satisfy an audit, with a fixed date.
- **Efficiency-driven** — wants to reduce manual work, with no fixed date.
- **Exploring** — no immediate decision, building understanding.

Three checks decide whether this is usable:

1. **Recognition.** Can a seller place a prospect from the answer or a short call, as the lead-scoring article recommends for signals?
2. **Action difference.** Does the compliance segment get a dated implementation path, the efficiency segment a worked example, and the exploring segment an evaluation guide, as the funnel article recommends for stage-matched next steps?
3. **Outcome.** Do conversion and retention differ by segment, which the A/B testing article treats as measurable, or were the differences imagined?

Three conclusions follow:

- **The segments are tied to a decision**, which the value-proposition article requires.
- **They are recognised at contact**, which the sales article treats as a qualification condition.
- **They are checked against outcomes**, as the survey standards and the evaluation article require for any model.

## Reviewing segments

A useful segment degrades as the market changes. Three signs it needs a review:

**Conversion converges.** If the segments no longer predict different outcomes, as the A/B testing article measures, the underlying difference may have disappeared, which the monitoring article treats as a change trigger.

**Sellers stop using it.** If the categories do not appear in call notes, they are not recognisable at contact, which the qualification-signal article treats as a usability test.

**New needs appear.** A product change or a competitor can create a segment the model does not contain, which the jobs-to-be-done article frames as a new context.

These signs call for evidence, not a redesign on instinct, as the survey standards require for any claim about groups of customers.

## What segmentation cannot do

**It cannot describe individuals accurately.** A segment is a group model, which the personas article warns against treating as a script.

**It is self-selected if survey-based.** Who answered matters, which AAPOR's standards require you to state, as the NPS article explains for scores.

**It goes stale.** Needs change with the market, which the monitoring article treats as a change trigger.

The counterargument — that simple firmographics are good enough — is fair for some markets and misses the point when two companies of the same size and industry buy for entirely different reasons, which the jobs-to-be-done article explains as a difference in context.

## A decision test

1. **Start with a decision** the segment should change, as the value-proposition article requires.
2. **Use signals observable at contact**, as the lead-scoring article recommends.
3. **Define a different action** for each segment, which the funnel article supports for stage matching.
4. **Check AAPOR-style wording** if a survey defines the segments, with a defined population.
5. **Measure outcomes by segment**, as the A/B testing article recommends.
6. **Avoid treating segments as individuals**, which the personas article warns about.
7. **Review on market change**, as the monitoring article advises.
