Venture debt is a loan to a venture-backed company, often structured with warrants and tied to an equity round. It is pitched as a way to extend runway without dilution, which is true at signing and incomplete as a description. Debt is an obligation with covenants and a maturity date, and those terms decide whether the extra runway is useful.

**Direct answer:** Use venture debt when a company has a clear use for the cash, a credible path to repayment or refinancing, and an understanding of the covenants. The venture-debt overview provides the instrument's context, and investor education treats debt as an obligation with risk. The boundary: debt cannot replace product-market fit, which the startup-validation article treats as the prior question, and a covenant breach can transfer control, as the debt-restructuring article explains.

## The mechanics

**The facility.** A loan amount, an interest rate, and a maturity date, often drawn in tranches tied to milestones, as the venture-debt overview describes. The terms are a negotiation, as the term-sheet article treats financing terms as the substance of a deal.

**Warrants.** Lenders may receive the right to buy shares, which adds a potential dilution component, as the cap-table article explains for instruments that affect ownership. A "non-dilutive" loan with warrants is non-dilutive only until the warrants are exercised.

**Covenants.** Conditions such as minimum cash or a requirement to raise a new round, which the debt-restructuring article treats as a test that can be breached. The definitions matter more than the thresholds, as the EBITDA article explains for how a measured figure is constructed.

Three consequences follow. Debt changes the risk profile without changing the business, which the risk-assessment article treats as a decision about upside and downside. It interacts with equity rounds, as the financing sequence determines whether the company can repay or refinance, which the startup-valuation article considers. And it has a cost even when it is not drawn, as commitment fees are a real expense, which the cost estimator treats as certain.

## A worked example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative. A company has 6 million in cash and a net burn of 500,000 a month, giving twelve months of runway, as the burn-rate article computes.

It raises 4 million of venture debt with a 30-month maturity, interest, and a covenant requiring a qualified equity round within 18 months. Three checks decide whether this is sensible. First, what is the use of funds, as the 13-week cash-flow article requires for a short-term view? If the cash funds a product with measurable milestones, the extension has a purpose. Second, can the company repay or refinance at maturity, which the debt-restructuring article treats as the central question? A round within 18 months is an assumption that should be evidenced, as the startup-valuation article advises. Third, do the covenants leave room for a slower round, as the covenant definitions in the debt article require?

Three conclusions follow. The runway extension is real but conditional, as the burn-rate article treats forward assumptions. The covenant is the binding constraint, which the worked example reveals. And the decision should be recorded with the repayment scenario, as the decision-memo article requires.

## What venture debt cannot do

It cannot fix a business that does not convert cash into value, which the unit-economics article treats as the underlying question. It cannot remove dilution entirely if warrants are involved, as the cap-table article explains. And it cannot guarantee an equity round, which the startup-valuation article treats as a conditional estimate, not a promise.

The counterargument that debt is cheaper than equity is partly true in cash terms and misses the covenant risk, which the debt-restructuring article treats as a transfer of control.

## A decision test

1. State the use of funds and the milestones, as the cash-flow article requires.
2. Check repayment and refinancing scenarios, as the debt article advises.
3. Read the covenants and their definitions, as the restructuring article requires.
4. Account for warrants in the ownership model, as the cap-table article explains.
5. Price interest and fees, as the cost estimator requires.
6. Compare with an equity round on dilution and control, as the term-sheet article advises.
7. Record the decision and the repayment assumption, as the decision-memo article requires.
