A pre-deployment review is often treated as a form to complete before launch. Done well, it is a short investigation that changes the launch plan — sometimes by reducing scope, sometimes by adding a control. NIST's risk-assessment guidance and the AI RMF provide the structure; the value comes from answering honestly.

**Direct answer:** An AI risk assessment identifies the plausible failure modes of a specific system, their likelihood and impact, and the controls that reduce them. NIST SP 800-30 describes risk assessment in terms of threats, vulnerabilities, likelihood and impact, while the AI RMF organises the work into govern, map, measure and manage. The boundary: an assessment covers failures you can anticipate — it is evidence about known risks, not a guarantee about novel ones.

## Five questions

**1. What can go wrong?** List concrete failure modes for this system: a wrong answer, a leaked document, an unauthorised action, a biased decision, an outage. Vague categories like "AI risk" are not actionable.

**2. Who is affected, and how badly?** Impact differs by stakeholder. A misrouted support ticket is a nuisance; a wrong benefits decision is a harm. The severity shapes the control.

**3. How likely is each failure?** Use evidence — evaluation results, historical error rates, red-team findings — rather than intuition, as the red-teaming article explains.

**4. What control reduces it?** Each risk needs a control: retrieval grounding, a tool limit, a human review, a monitoring alert. A risk with no control is either accepted explicitly or blocks launch.

**5. What would change the assessment?** A model update, a new data source, or a new user group can invalidate the analysis. Name the trigger for revisiting it, which the governance checklist covers.

## A worked assessment

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A team deploys an assistant that summarises internal contracts for lawyers. The assessment lists four risks:

| Risk | Impact | Likelihood evidence | Control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wrong clause summarised | High | 8% error on labelled set | Cite source; lawyer review |
| Confidential clause shown to wrong user | High | Access-control test found a gap | Fix permissions; log access |
| Stale contract version used | Medium | Index refresh is weekly | Add version check; daily refresh |
| Output too long for review | Low | Feedback from pilot | Length limit in prompt |

Three conclusions follow:

1. **The access-control gap blocks launch** until fixed, regardless of low likelihood — impact is high and the control is concrete, as the security checklist requires.
2. **The 8% error rate is accepted with review**, because the control (lawyer review) catches it and the workflow has time for it, as the human-in-the-loop article explains.
3. **The version risk has an owner and a refresh trigger**, which turns a vague concern into an operational task, as the data-governance article recommends.

## Making the assessment usable

A risk assessment that lives in a document nobody opens has no effect. Three practices make it a working artefact:

**Keep it short and specific.** One page with four or five named risks, their controls, and their owners is more useful than a long catalogue of hypotheticals. Each row should be something a reviewer can check, as the decision-memo article argues for concrete claims.

**Attach it to the change process.** When a model, prompt, data source, or tool changes, the assessment should be revisited. The trigger belongs in the deployment checklist, which the governance article describes, not in a calendar reminder.

**Record accepted risks with an owner.** Some risks will be accepted rather than controlled. That is a legitimate decision, but it needs a name and a review date, otherwise it becomes an unexamined assumption that grows with the system, as the model-monitoring article warns.

These practices turn the assessment from a launch gate into a living record of what the team knows about its system.

## What an assessment cannot cover

**Novel attacks.** An adversary will find failure modes the assessment did not list, which is why red teaming and monitoring are ongoing, as the observability article explains.

**Interaction effects.** Two individually safe components can combine into an unsafe system. The combined-system error rate is what matters, as the workflow-automation article argues.

**Value judgments.** Deciding which risk is acceptable is a business and ethical choice, not a technical calculation, which the AI-governance article addresses.

The counterargument — that assessments slow deployment — is fair when they become paperwork. A short assessment that changes one decision is worth more than a long document that approves everything.

## A decision test

1. **List concrete failure modes** for this system.
2. **State impact and likelihood** with evidence.
3. **Assign a control** to each material risk.
4. **Block launch** on high-impact risks without controls.
5. **Accept residual risks explicitly**, with an owner.
6. **Name the change triggers** for re-assessment.
7. **Record the assessment** where the team can find it, as the decision-memo article recommends.
