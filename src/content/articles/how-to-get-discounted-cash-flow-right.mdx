Discounted cash flow is often judged by the precision of its output, which hides the assumptions that produced it. The Wikipedia entry describes the method, and Damodaran's framework insists that assumptions be explicit. The right way to build one is to make those assumptions challengeable.

**Direct answer:** Build a DCF from a clear forecast, a consistent discount rate, and an explicit statement of what would change the result. The sources describe the method; the discipline is a synthesis.

**Delta:** This article adds a claim-status table and a worked example, separating documented method from local judgements about growth and risk.

## The mechanism

The first input is cash flow. The DCF entry describes discounting future cash to the present. A forecast that begins with revenue and works down to free cash flow is more transparent than one that begins with a desired value and works backwards, because each step can be checked.

<Figure src="/images/articles/inline/how-to-get-discounted-cash-flow-right-1.png" alt="A four-step flow: forecast cash flows, choose a consistent discount rate, state the terminal value, then test sensitivities." caption="Conceptual: a DCF is a set of challengeable assumptions, not one precise figure." />

The second input is the discount rate. Damodaran's framework treats the rate as a reflection of risk, which should be consistent with the cash flows being discounted. A nominal cash flow discounted at a real rate, or a risky cash flow at a risk-free rate, produces a number that looks sound and is not.

The third input is the terminal value. A large share of many DCFs comes from the period beyond the explicit forecast, which makes the terminal assumptions the most consequential and often the least examined. Stating them separately is the control.

The sequence is: forecast cash flows, choose a consistent rate, state the terminal assumption, then test how much the result depends on each. The choices are local, so the conclusions are `inferred`; the method is `source-reported`.

## A worked example

*Illustrative.* An analyst values a company with five years of forecasts and a terminal value.

Three checks decide. First, does each forecast step follow from an explicit assumption about volume, price, and margin? Second, is the discount rate consistent with the currency and risk of the cash flows? Third, how much of the total value comes from the terminal assumption, and what happens if it changes?

The outcome is a value with stated sensitivities. The scenario is hypothetical; the method is the contribution.

## Why precision can mislead

A value calculated to several decimal places inherits the precision of its inputs, which are forecasts. Showing a range from alternative assumptions is more honest than a single precise figure, because it reflects what the analyst actually knows.

A further issue is that the discount rate and the cash flows must describe the same world. A rate derived from market data on similar companies assumes a risk profile, and the forecast should reflect that same level of uncertainty. Mixing a conservative forecast with a low rate produces an estimate that is confident for the wrong reasons.

The terminal value deserves its own review. Because it often dominates the total, a small change in the assumed long-term growth can move the result substantially, and that sensitivity should be shown rather than buried. A single figure without the sensitivity is hard to challenge.

None of this turns a DCF into a forecast. It produces a structured estimate whose assumptions can be argued with, which is the most that the method can honestly promise.

A result should be reported with its assumptions, since a figure without them cannot be challenged.

The sensitivity table is often more informative than the headline value, because it shows where the argument is weakest.

The forecast and the rate should describe the same risk, which is a consistency check before any arithmetic.

A precise result built on weak inputs is less useful than a range built on honest assumptions.

## What it cannot do

It cannot predict the future. It does not remove the need to understand the business. And it cannot make a bad assumption better.

The counterargument that a model is objective is fair for the arithmetic and incomplete for the inputs.

## A decision test

1. Forecast cash flows from explicit drivers.
2. Choose a consistent discount rate.
3. State the terminal assumption.
4. Compute the value.
5. Test sensitivity to key inputs.
6. Record the assumptions and recheck date.

For the pitfalls, see [DCF Pitfalls](/articles/dcf-pitfalls-the-assumptions-that-break-valuation).
