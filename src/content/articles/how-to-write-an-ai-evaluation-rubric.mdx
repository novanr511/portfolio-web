Most AI evaluation arguments fail because the participants mean different things by "good". One reviewer values completeness, another values brevity, and neither has written down the rule. A rubric is the instrument that makes the judgement explicit and repeatable.

**Direct answer:** A rubric defines the dimensions being scored, what each score level means, and the evidence required for each level. HELM's multi-metric approach — measuring accuracy, calibration, robustness and other properties separately — is the principle at scale; Anthropic's prompt guidance makes success criteria a prerequisite to any prompt work. The boundary: a rubric makes judgement consistent, not correct — a badly chosen dimension will be measured consistently and still mislead.

## Four dimensions to score separately

Combining everything into one number hides trade-offs. Four dimensions cover most generation tasks:

**Correctness.** Are the claims supported by the supplied evidence? For extraction, is the value right?

**Completeness.** Does the answer cover the parts of the question that matter? A correct but partial answer fails here.

**Groundedness.** Does the answer stay within the provided sources, and does it say when evidence is absent? This is distinct from correctness — a fluent answer can be wrong and ungrounded at once.

**Presentation.** Is the output in the required format, length, and tone? Important for integration, and the least important of the four for truth.

Scoring these separately lets a team see that a prompt improved presentation while worsening groundedness, which a single score would conceal.

## Writing the levels

Each dimension needs anchor descriptions for the score levels. A three-point scale is enough to start:

- **2 — meets the requirement.** Correctness: all claims are supported by the cited source.
- **1 — partially meets.** One claim is unsupported or ambiguous.
- **0 — fails.** A core claim is contradicted by the source, or no source is provided.

The anchors matter more than the number of levels. A five-point scale with vague anchors produces more noise, not more precision.

## A worked rubric for a support answer

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A support assistant answers questions from a policy handbook. A reviewer scores one answer per dimension:

| Dimension | Score | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Correctness | 2 | The refund window (14 days) matches Section 7. |
| Completeness | 1 | It omits that customs duties are excluded. |
| Groundedness | 2 | Section 7 is cited and supports the claim. |
| Presentation | 2 | Two sentences, required format, no speculation. |

Total: 7/8. The decision is not "ship it" — completeness failed on a detail that matters to customers. The rubric shows *which* dimension to fix, which is the point.

Three lessons follow:

1. **A total can hide a failure.** Set a minimum per dimension, not only a total threshold.
2. **The evidence column is part of the rubric.** A score without the reason cannot be audited or discussed.
3. **Partial scores are informative.** "One missing detail" is a different problem from "no citation", and each points to a different fix.

## Keeping judges consistent

Two reviewers using the same rubric will still diverge until they calibrate. Three practices help:

**Score a sample together.** Have both reviewers score ten answers, then compare. Most disagreements come from different interpretations of an anchor, which a shared session fixes quickly.

**Keep the rubric with the test set.** Anchors that live in a document nobody opens will drift. Put them next to the examples they score.

**Distinguish the judge from the system.** If an LLM scores answers, validate it against human scores on a sample. A judge that rewards length will inflate presentation and penalise concise correct answers. HELM's point about measuring beyond accuracy applies to judges too.

## What a rubric cannot do

**It cannot define what matters.** Choosing dimensions is a product decision. A rubric that omits groundedness will happily reward fluent fabrication.

**It cannot remove subjectivity.** Anchors reduce disagreement; they do not eliminate judgement. A human should still review the cases near the threshold.

**It stays valid only for one task.** A rubric for support answers is wrong for code generation. Reuse the structure, not the dimensions.

The counterargument — that a rubric is bureaucratic overhead — is fair for a single experiment. It earns its cost when a team evaluates after every change, because reproducibility is otherwise impossible.

## A decision test

1. **Choose dimensions that reflect the failure modes** you have observed.
2. **Write anchor descriptions** for each score level.
3. **Require an evidence note** for every score.
4. **Set a minimum per dimension**, not only a total.
5. **Calibrate reviewers** on a shared sample.
6. **Validate an automated judge** against human scores.
7. **Revise the rubric** when the task or the failure modes change.
