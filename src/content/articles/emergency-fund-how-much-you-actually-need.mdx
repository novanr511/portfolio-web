<Lead>
"Three to six months of expenses" is the standard answer to the emergency-fund question. It is <Mark>a starting point, not an estimate</Mark>. The right number depends on how stable your income is, how much of your spending is fixed, and how quickly you could reduce it if needed.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Size an emergency fund from your actual monthly essentials and <Mark>the length of time you could plausibly need them</Mark>, then adjust for income stability and household concentration. Investor.gov's investor education emphasises that all investments involve risk and that planning starts with understanding your finances. The boundary: a reserve that is too large has an opportunity cost, and one held in volatile assets is not a reserve — it is a bet.

## A sizing method

**1. Define essentials.** Rent or mortgage, utilities, food, transport, insurance, and minimum debt payments. Exclude discretionary spending, because the fund's job is to cover survival, not lifestyle.

**2. Estimate the duration.** Ask how long it would realistically take to replace income after a job loss, an illness, or a major repair. A stable public-sector role and a commission-based sales role are different cases.

**3. Adjust for concentration.** A single-income household with dependants needs more than a dual-income household with no dependants. A person with a supporting network may need less than one without.

**4. Add known one-off risks.** A older car, a home with an aging boiler, or a chronic health expense are predictable in kind if not in timing.

**5. Decide where to hold it.** The fund must be accessible and low-volatility. A cash account or a government money-market vehicle is typical; equities are not a reserve because their value falls precisely when jobs are scarce.

## A worked example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A household has monthly essentials of 3,000, including rent, food, transport, and minimum debt payments. One earner's income covers 70% of the total; the other is stable but insufficient alone.

- **Base estimate:** 3,000 × 6 = **18,000**.
- **Concentration adjustment:** the dominant earner's role is in a cyclical industry with an estimated six-month search. Add two months: **24,000**.
- **Known one-offs:** a car likely to need 2,000 of work within two years. Add it: **26,000**.

Three decisions follow:

1. **The number is derived, not conventional.** It came from actual expenses and a realistic search time, not from a rule.
2. **The one-off addition is explicit.** Adding it prevents the car repair from becoming an "emergency" that consumes the income-replacement fund.
3. **The fund has a review trigger.** A change in employment, a new dependant, or a refinancing changes the inputs, so the target should be recalculated.

Three cautions apply: the six-month search is an assumption that can be checked against local data; the car cost is an estimate that should be replaced with a quote when possible; and the household should decide whether the full 26,000 must be in cash or whether the one-off portion can be a shorter-term holding.

## Where to keep the money

The right location for a reserve is boring by design. Three properties matter more than the headline interest rate:

**Accessibility.** The money must be available within a day, not after a notice period or a sale. An account that restricts withdrawals fails at the moment it is needed.

**Low volatility.** A reserve held in assets that fall during a market decline may be worth less precisely when income stops.

<KeyPoint>
The correlation with the reason you need the fund is what disqualifies volatile assets, not their long-run expected return.
</KeyPoint>

**Separation from everyday spending.** A reserve kept in the same account as daily spending tends to be spent. A separate account, even at the same institution, creates a small friction that protects the balance.

Some savers split the fund into a fully liquid portion for immediate needs and a slightly less liquid portion for longer-term income replacement. That is reasonable provided the second portion is still low-risk and available within the stated timeline.

## What the fund cannot do

**It cannot cover a long-term income loss.** Six months is <Mark>a bridge, not a replacement</Mark>. Longer risks are an insurance question.

**It cannot be invested for growth.** The trade-off is deliberate: you accept a lower expected return for availability.

<Trap>
Holding the fund in equities defeats the purpose because the correlation with job loss is unhelpful.
</Trap>

**It cannot fix a cash-flow problem.** If monthly essentials exceed income, a larger reserve delays the problem rather than solving it. The spending side needs attention first.

The counterargument — that holding cash is wasteful when returns are high — ignores the asymmetry.

<PullQuote>
The fund's value is in being there when other assets or income are unavailable.
</PullQuote>

Its cost is the difference between cash returns and what the money could have earned, which is a price worth paying for a defined risk.

## A decision test

1. **List essentials** from actual spending, not estimates.
2. **Estimate the replacement time** for your specific situation.
3. **Adjust for concentration** and dependants.
4. **Add known one-off risks** by name and amount.
5. **Choose low-volatility, accessible holdings.**
6. **Review on change** — employment, household, or major assets.
7. **Keep a separate plan** for risks longer than the fund's horizon.
