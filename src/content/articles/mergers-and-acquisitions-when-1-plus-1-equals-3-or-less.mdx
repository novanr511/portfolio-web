Most mergers fail to create value. The ones that succeed follow discipline.

**Direct answer:** Mergers and acquisitions (M&A) combine two companies into one, with the goal of creating value greater than the sum of the parts (synergy). In practice, most M&A deals destroy value for the acquiring company's shareholders due to overpayment, integration failure, and culture clashes. Successful M&A requires a clear strategic rationale, realistic valuation, careful due diligence, and disciplined integration.

## Why companies pursue M&A

Strategic reasons include:

- **Synergy.** Cost savings (eliminating duplicate functions) and revenue growth (cross-selling, combined offerings).
- **Market share.** Acquiring a competitor to consolidate a market.
- **Capabilities.** Gaining technology, talent, or intellectual property faster than building it.
- **Entry.** Entering a new geography or customer segment quickly.
- **Defense.** Preventing a competitor from acquiring a strategic asset.

## Why most M&A fails

**Overpayment.** Auction dynamics and enthusiasm lead acquirers to pay more than the target is worth. The premium paid must be justified by synergies that are often overestimated.

**Integration failure.** Combining systems, processes, teams, and cultures is harder than signing the deal. Key employees leave, customers churn, and product momentum stalls.

**Culture clash.** Different decision-making styles, risk tolerance, and ways of working create friction that undermines collaboration.

**Integration underestimation.** Management assumes integration happens automatically. It does not. It requires dedicated resources, clear leadership, and time.

**Lack of strategic clarity.** Deals done for "scale" or "synergy" without a concrete plan rarely deliver.

## What makes M&A succeed

**Clear strategic rationale.** The deal answers a specific question: how does this acquisition make our strategy more likely to succeed?

**Conservative valuation.** Pay based on the target's standalone value plus realistic synergies, not on auction fever.

**Due diligence beyond finance.** Examine culture, customers, technology, talent, and integration risks—not just financial statements.

**Integration planning before close.** Integration should be planned before the deal is signed, with a named leader and dedicated team.

**Retain talent.** Key people from the target company often leave after acquisition. Retention plans matter.

**Measure outcomes.** Track synergy realization, customer retention, and employee retention post-deal.

## The integration phases

1. **Day one.** Announce the deal, communicate to employees and customers, appoint integration leadership.
2. **First 90 days.** Stabilize operations, retain key talent, communicate integration plans, begin systems integration.
3. **First year.** Complete systems and process integration, realize cost synergies, cross-sell to combined customers.
4. **Ongoing.** Measure results, adjust, and decide on further M&A.

## When not to acquire

M&A is not always the answer. Sometimes building internally, partnering, or licensing is faster, cheaper, and less risky. M&A should be a means to a strategic end, not an end in itself.
