<Lead>
An organisation can have thousands of AI licences and no change in how work gets done. <Mark>Counting seats is easy and nearly meaningless</Mark>. The NBER working paper on generative AI adoption is useful precisely because it separates measures — any use, use at work, weekly use, daily use — instead of reporting one number.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Measure adoption with four signals: access, reported use, frequency of use in specific workflows, and outcomes. Bick, Blandin and Deming's surveys found that "as of late 2024, nearly 40 percent of the U.S. population age 18-64 uses generative AI", with 23% of employed respondents having used it at work in the previous week and 9% every workday. The boundary: these are survey reports of use, not measurements of productivity — METR's randomized trial found early tools slowed experienced developers before later data reversed that result.

## Four signals

**1. Access.** Who has a licence and can use a tool. This is a ceiling, not adoption.

<Trap>
A high access number with low use is a procurement result.
</Trap>

**2. Reported use.** Ask whether someone used a tool in a defined window, with the same wording as a benchmark you trust. "Have you used generative AI for work in the last seven days?" is a measurable question; "do you use AI?" is not.

**3. Workflow frequency.** Which tasks is it used for, and how often? A person who uses a tool once a week to draft emails has a different relationship to it from one who uses it daily for code review. The task taxonomy article distinguishes the underlying types.

**4. Outcomes.** Time per task, error rate, quality, or cycle time — measured against a baseline. <Mark>This is the only signal that tells you whether adoption created value</Mark>.

<KeyPoint>
The signals are a funnel: access enables reported use, reported use may become workflow integration, and integration may produce outcomes.
</KeyPoint>

Each stage can fail independently.

## A worked example of a survey

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A company of 2,000 staff wants to measure AI adoption. It sends a survey to all staff and receives 1,200 responses.

- **Access:** 1,000 of 2,000 staff have a licence — 50%.
- **Reported use:** 600 of 1,200 respondents say they used a tool at work in the last seven days — 50%.
- **Workflow frequency:** 180 of those 600 say they used it daily; 420 used it once or twice in the week.
- **Outcomes:** a follow-up with 50 daily users finds a measured time saving on one task, but no change in cycle time at the team level.

Three cautions follow:

1. **Response bias.** With a 60% response rate, the respondents may be the enthusiasts. The 50% use figure is an upper bound if non-respondents are less engaged.
2. **Denominators must be stated.** "50% of respondents" and "30% of all staff" are different claims. The NBER paper is careful to specify its population — employed respondents, working-age population — and a company survey should do the same.
3. **Use is not outcome.** <Mark>Daily use with no cycle-time change is a real finding</Mark>. It may mean the tool helps individuals while the workflow's bottleneck sits elsewhere, which is a process problem rather than an adoption problem.

Three decisions follow:

- **Report all four signals separately**, not as a single adoption score.
- **State the denominator and response rate** alongside every figure.
- **Pair the survey with one process measurement** — a task's time or error rate — to test whether use changed anything.

## What adoption figures do not show

**They do not show value.** The NBER paper describes fast diffusion, not proven economic impact. The METR result is the clearest reminder that using a tool can be slower.

**They do not show depth.** A daily user may use the tool for one trivial task. Without the workflow question, the frequency number flatters the programme.

**They do not show distribution.** Adoption can be concentrated in one function while others are untouched. Aggregate figures hide that, and the teams left behind may be the ones with the most to gain.

<PullQuote>
The counterargument — that measuring adoption is itself overhead — is fair.
</PullQuote>

The response is to keep the survey short: four questions, one process metric, and a repeat every quarter. That is enough to detect direction without building a measurement programme.

## A decision test

1. **Separate access, use, frequency and outcomes.**
2. **Use the same question wording** each wave so results are comparable.
3. **State the population and response rate** with every figure.
4. **Ask which task** the tool is used for, not just whether it is used.
5. **Pair the survey with one process metric** from a baseline.
6. **Break results down by function** to find the gaps.
7. **Treat use without outcome as a question**, not a success.

For the broader evidence, see [The Rapid Adoption of Generative AI](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32966) and the [field study of AI at work](/articles/what-a-field-study-of-generative-ai-at-work-found); for the implementation sequence, see [AI Integration Into an Existing Business](/articles/ai-integration-blueprint-businesses).
