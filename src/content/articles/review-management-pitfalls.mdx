Review management is often treated as reputation engineering: ask for good reviews, respond to bad ones, and push the average up. The customer-review entry treats reviews as a public record, which changes the logic — a manipulated record loses the trust that made it valuable, as the marketing-communications entry treats a message as something received, as the online-reviews article treats reputation as earned, as the advocacy article argues for genuine voices.

**Direct answer:** Manage reviews by improving the underlying experience, inviting honest feedback from all customers, and responding to negative reviews with a resolution rather than a defence. The customer-review entry provides the structure, and marketing communications covers reception. The boundary: this is educational orientation, not legal advice — platform rules and consumer law differ by jurisdiction, as the legal and privacy articles require a dated check, as the publication standard forbids guaranteed outcomes.

## The pitfalls

**Asking only for positive reviews.** Selecting happy customers biases the record and can breach platform rules, which the referral article treats as an incentive design problem, as the online-reviews article warns about distorted evidence, as AAPOR's standards require for transparent sampling where a survey is used.

**Treating the average as the goal.** A higher average can hide a worse pattern, which the dashboard article warns about metrics without dimensions, as the evaluation-rubric article requires for concrete criteria, as the retention-analytics article advises for grouping feedback over time.

**Responding defensively.** A response is visible to future readers, which the customer-review entry and crisis article treat as a public record, as the value-proposition article argues for claims that hold, as the voice-of-customer article treats feedback as a signal for decisions.

**Ignoring the cause.** A response does not fix the problem that produced the review, which the root-cause article treats as distinct from the symptom, as the incident article requires for containment, as the decision-memo article requires for a recorded judgement.

Three consequences follow. The invitation should be neutral, as AAPOR's standards and the survey-design article advise. The responses should be recorded, as the data-governance article requires, as the decision-memo article requires. And the underlying cause should be addressed, as the root-cause article advises, as the monitoring article treats as a follow-up.

## What review management cannot do

It cannot manufacture trust, which the online-reviews and advocacy articles treat as earned, as the value-proposition article argues for truth.
