Interest rates are the price of time. They change how technology companies are valued, funded, and run.

**Direct answer:** Interest rates affect technology companies through the cost of capital, discount rates used in valuation, and the availability of growth funding. Higher rates reduce the present value of future cash flows, making growth-stage companies less valuable relative to profitable ones. Lower rates make future-oriented investment cheaper, encouraging risk-taking. Technology companies—especially startups and high-growth firms—are more sensitive to rate changes than mature businesses with current profits.

## Why rates matter for tech

Technology companies often operate with a specific financial profile:

- **Heavy upfront investment:** product development before revenue.
- **Long time to profitability:** growth prioritized over current earnings.
- **Intangible assets:** code, data, and users rather than factories and inventory.
- **External capital:** venture debt, convertible notes, equity rounds.

This profile makes tech companies sensitive to the cost of capital. When rates rise, future earnings are worth less today, and borrowing becomes more expensive.

## The valuation effect

Most tech valuations use discounted cash flow (DCF): future cash flows are discounted back to the present using a rate that includes the risk-free rate (often tied to government bond yields). When the risk-free rate rises:

- The discount rate rises.
- Future cash flows are worth less today.
- Valuations fall, especially for companies whose profits are far in the future.

This is why high-growth, loss-making tech companies often see steeper valuation declines than profitable companies when rates rise.

## Funding and capital costs

**Venture capital:** VC funds raise money from limited partners who compare tech returns against safe alternatives (bonds). When bond yields rise, tech must offer higher expected returns to attract capital. This makes fundraising harder and valuations more conservative.

**Venture debt:** startups often use debt to extend runway. Higher rates increase debt service costs, reducing cash available for growth.

**Public markets:** tech stocks often trade at high price-to-earnings multiples based on expected growth. Rate increases compress these multiples.

## The shift to profitability

When capital is cheap, growth at all costs is viable. Companies can lose money for years while scaling, funded by investors betting on future dominance. When rates rise, the calculus changes:

- Profitability becomes more important than growth.
- Unit economics must work sooner.
- Cash runway is scrutinized.
- "Efficient growth" replaces "growth at all costs."

Companies with strong unit economics and cash generation are more resilient to rate cycles.

## Real estate and operations

Tech companies often lease office space and data centers. Higher rates increase financing costs for real estate and equipment. Remote work (discussed in [Remote Work: The Economics and Culture of Distributed Teams](/articles/remote-work-the-economics-and-culture-of-distributed-teams)) can mitigate some of these costs by reducing physical footprint needs.

## M&A and exits

Higher rates reduce acquirers' willingness and ability to pay for targets. IPO markets slow when investors demand higher returns. This affects exit options for startups and investors, which in turn affects early-stage investment appetite.

## The rate cycle is not permanent

Interest rates move in cycles. Technology companies that build sustainable unit economics, maintain cash reserves, and avoid over-reliance on external capital survive rate changes. Companies that depend on cheap capital for survival do not.
