Most value propositions fail because they describe the company rather than the customer's decision. "We provide innovative solutions for modern teams" could appear on thousands of websites. A useful value proposition lets a specific buyer answer three questions: is this for me, what changes if I choose it, and why this rather than the alternative.

**Direct answer:** Write a value proposition that names the customer, the problem or outcome, and the differentiated reason to believe — then test whether the intended reader can repeat it back. Porter's strategy argument warns that operational effectiveness is not strategy, and the blue-ocean framing asks whether the comparison set itself should change. The boundary: a sentence is not a value proposition if the product cannot deliver it, and clever wording cannot substitute for a distinctive position.

## Three questions to answer first

**Who is it for?** A segment specific enough that a reader recognises themselves. "Businesses" is too broad; "finance teams preparing for an annual audit" is usable.

**What changes?** An outcome the customer values, ideally stated in terms they already use. "Faster" is weak unless the reader knows what becomes faster.

**Why you?** A reason grounded in something the business can defend — a mechanism, a documented result, or a structural advantage. "Better service" is a claim; a response-time guarantee with measured support is evidence.

These questions are a test of the underlying strategy. If the third has no good answer, the problem is not the wording.

## A worked rewrite

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

**Before:** "We offer a modern platform that helps teams work more efficiently with their data."

Problems: no named customer, no specific outcome, no reason to believe, and words that any competitor could use.

**After:** "For finance teams preparing an audit, our reconciliation tool cuts evidence-gathering from three days to four hours by matching transactions to source documents automatically. Unlike manual spreadsheet workflows, every figure keeps a traceable link to its source."

Three changes follow:

1. **The customer is named.** A finance team with an audit coming recognises the situation.
2. **The outcome is measured, with a mechanism.** "Three days to four hours" is a claim that needs a source, and "automatic matching" explains how.
3. **The comparison is explicit.** "Unlike manual spreadsheet workflows" names the alternative, so the buyer knows what is being replaced.

Three checks make it honest:

- The time figure must come from a real measurement, with a stated context.
- The automatic matching must actually exist and work on the buyer's data.
- The alternative must be the one the buyer really uses, not a straw man.

## From sentence to test

A value proposition is a hypothesis about what customers value. Three ways to test it:

**The repeat-back test.** Show the sentence to a target customer and ask what they think the product does. If their answer differs from yours, the proposition is unclear.

**The landing-page test.** Use the proposition as the headline and measure qualified conversion, as the landing-page article describes. A clear proposition should improve the rate at which the right visitors take the next step.

**The sales-call test.** Note whether prospects quote the proposition back when explaining why they bought. Their words are better evidence than internal approval.

Each test has a different failure mode: repeat-back catches ambiguity, landing-page tests catch irrelevance, and sales calls catch a gap between the promise and the delivered experience.

## What a value proposition cannot do

**It cannot fix an undifferentiated product.** If a competitor can make the same claim truthfully, the issue is positioning or capability, not copy. Porter's point about fit applies: a distinctive position is supported by a system of activities.

**It cannot replace evidence.** A measured claim needs a source and a context. A number without a denominator invites disbelief from a careful buyer.

**It cannot serve every segment.** A proposition broad enough to cover all buyers is too vague to persuade any of them. Separate propositions for distinct segments are better than one compromise.

The counterargument — that a value proposition is just marketing language — is fair when it is written without strategy. Done properly, it is a compact statement of the strategic choice, which is why it is hard to write and worth the effort.

## A decision test

1. **Name the segment** narrowly enough to be recognised.
2. **State the outcome** in the customer's language, with a source for any number.
3. **Name the alternative** the buyer actually considers.
4. **Give a defensible reason** grounded in mechanism or evidence.
5. **Run the repeat-back test** with target customers.
6. **Test on the landing page** with qualified conversion as the metric.
7. **Revise when the product or the market changes**, not on a writing schedule.
