Where a product sits on a shelf or a page determines whether it sells. Placement is a data decision, not an aesthetic one.

**Direct answer:** Retailers use point-of-sale, foot traffic, basket analysis, and online click data to decide product placement—what goes at eye level, near the checkout, or on the homepage—and then measure whether the placement changed behavior. Placement optimization is an experiment with a measurable outcome, not a permanent rule.

## What placement data captures

**Sales velocity by location.** The same product sells at different rates depending on where it is displayed. End-of-aisle displays, eye-level shelves, and checkout-adjacent positions typically outperform back-wall or bottom-shelf placement. Comparing the same SKU across locations isolates the placement effect from product appeal.

**Basket analysis.** Which products are bought together? Placing complementary items near each other—batteries near electronics, sunscreen near beachwear—increases attach rate. Data reveals these pairs; intuition often misses them.

**Traffic patterns.** In physical stores, cameras and Wi-Fi/Bluetooth signals show which aisles get traffic and which are skipped. Online, heatmaps and click paths show which homepage modules and category pages convert. Placement follows traffic: high-margin items go where people already look.

**Substitution and cannibalization.** Moving a product can steal sales from a neighbor rather than create new sales. Data distinguishes true lift from reshuffled demand.

## Online versus physical placement

Physical placement is constrained by shelf space, planograms, and store layout. Changes are slow and expensive to reverse. A/B testing is possible across stores but takes weeks.

Online placement is nearly free to change and easy to test. Homepage modules, search result order, and "customers also bought" carousels can be A/B tested daily. The feedback loop is tight: change the layout in the morning, see results by evening.

Both channels use the same underlying logic: put high-intent, high-margin products where attention is highest, and measure the result.

## Measuring placement impact

A placement change is an experiment. Before moving a product, define:

- **Baseline:** current sales, conversion, and attach rate for the affected products.
- **Hypothesis:** "Moving X to end-cap Y will increase X sales by Z % without reducing Y sales."
- **Control:** a comparable store or page segment that does not change.
- **Duration:** long enough to capture normal traffic patterns, not a single weekend.

After the change, compare treatment to control. If sales rose without cannibalizing neighbors, the placement worked. If not, revert. Placement decisions that cannot be reversed are bets; decisions that can be measured are decisions.

## The limits of placement data

Placement data shows behavior, not motivation. A product sells better at eye level because it is seen more, because it looks more important, or because shoppers associate eye level with quality. The data cannot separate these. It can tell you what works, not why.

Placement also interacts with assortment, pricing, and promotions. A well-placed product with a bad price still does not sell. Data must be read in context, not as a standalone answer.
