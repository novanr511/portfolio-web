<Lead>
EBITDA appears in almost every investment pitch, and it is routinely used to make a business look better than its cash generation suggests. The metric is not fraudulent; it answers a specific question about operating performance before financing and accounting choices. The mistake is treating that question as the only one that matters.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:**

<KeyPoint>
EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortisation — a measure of operating profit that excludes financing decisions, tax environments, and non-cash charges.
</KeyPoint>

The financial-statement overview provides the context for where these items sit; Damodaran's valuation work is a useful reminder that cash flow, not accounting profit, ultimately determines value. The boundary: <Mark>excluding depreciation hides the cost of maintaining the asset base</Mark>, so EBITDA is a poor proxy for cash generation in capital-intensive businesses.

## What it clarifies

Removing the four items serves three purposes:

**Comparability across financing.** Two companies with the same operations but different debt levels will report different net income. EBITDA removes interest so their operating performance can be compared.

**Comparability across tax environments.** Tax rates vary by jurisdiction and change over time. Excluding tax makes the operating comparison cleaner.

**A focus on operating cash generation before capital structure.** For a quick view of whether the core business produces earnings, EBITDA is useful, which is why it appears in lending covenants and transaction multiples.

Each of those is a legitimate analytical purpose.

<Trap>
The problem starts when the measure is used as if it were the whole answer.
</Trap>

## What it hides

**Depreciation is not free.** It allocates the cost of assets over their useful lives. Excluding it makes a capital-intensive business look more profitable than one that needs little equipment, even if the latter generates more cash.

**Working capital is invisible.** A company can report growing EBITDA while cash is absorbed by receivables and inventory. The working-capital article shows how growth creates that gap.

**Adjustments are negotiable.** "Adjusted EBITDA" adds back costs the company considers one-off — restructuring, share-based compensation, litigation. Each adjustment is a judgement, and <Mark>a generous set of adjustments can turn a loss into a profit on the slide</Mark>.

A company with high maintenance capital expenditure and a long list of adjustments may be precisely the one whose EBITDA is least informative.

## A worked example of the gap

The following numbers are hypothetical and illustrative.

A company reports:

- Operating profit: 8 million.
- Depreciation and amortisation: 6 million.
- EBITDA: **14 million**.
- Interest: 3 million, tax: 1.25 million — net income 3.75 million.

Now consider two versions of the same business:

**Asset-light version.** Maintenance capital expenditure is 2 million a year. Free cash flow before working capital is roughly 8 + 6 − 2 − 3 − 1.25 = 7.75 million. EBITDA of 14 overstates the cash available by a wide margin.

**Asset-heavy version.** Maintenance capital expenditure is 7 million a year. The same EBITDA of 14 becomes cash flow near 2.75 million. The asset-heavy business looks identical on an EBITDA multiple and is materially different as an investment.

Three lessons follow:

1. **The same EBITDA can represent very different economics.** The difference is <Mark>the capital required to sustain the business</Mark>.
2. **Depreciation is a proxy for that cost.** Excluding it without considering maintenance capex removes the signal.
3. **Net income is not the answer either.** It includes financing and tax choices. The right move is to look at several measures, not to choose one.

## How to use it honestly

**Report EBITDA alongside free cash flow.** The gap between them is where the capital intensity lives. The free-cash-flow article works through the calculation.

**List the adjustments.** If a figure is "adjusted", every add-back should be visible with a reason. A long list is a prompt to investigate, not a reason to accept the number.

**Compare within an industry.** A multiple is meaningful against peers with similar capital structures and asset bases. Across industries, the same multiple can mean opposite things.

The counterargument — that EBITDA is a rough measure everyone understands — is fair for a first pass.

<PullQuote>
The error is stopping there, especially in a business where assets need replacing.
</PullQuote>

## A decision test

1. **State the question** EBITDA is being used to answer.
2. **Report it next to free cash flow** and maintenance capex.
3. **List every adjustment** with its reason.
4. **Compare within the same industry** and asset profile.
5. **Check the working-capital trend** separately.
6. **Treat a high multiple on EBITDA as a question** about capital intensity.
7. **Do not use EBITDA as a substitute for cash generation.**
