Most internal documents described as decision memos are not about a decision. They describe a situation, list options, and end with "let's discuss". The reader leaves with context but no clear claim to accept, reject, or modify. A memo that survives review does the opposite: it makes a specific recommendation easy to challenge.

**Direct answer:** A decision memo is a short written document that states the decision, the evidence, the trade-offs, and the recommendation so a reviewer can evaluate the reasoning rather than reconstruct it. Wikipedia's memo entry describes the memo as a written instrument for internal communication, and decision-making literature separates the quality of a decision from the quality of its outcome. The boundary: a memo structures a decision, it does not guarantee a good one — and a document written to win an argument rather than test a claim will fail its purpose regardless of format.

## What the memo has to do

Four jobs matter:

**Make the decision concrete.** "Should we migrate the billing service to a new provider?" is a decision. "Explore billing options" is a project. A memo without a decided question cannot be reviewed.

**Separate facts from judgements.** The evidence — costs, dates, measured performance — should be distinguishable from the interpretation of it. A reviewer who disagrees with the recommendation should be able to say *which* input or inference they dispute.

**State the alternative.** A recommendation without a named alternative is not a choice. The comparison should include the option of doing nothing, with its cost.

**Make reversal possible.** Write down what would change the decision — a new datum, a changed constraint, a failed assumption. Without that, a decision is treated as permanent and revisited only after it fails.

## A structure that works

A reviewable memo has seven parts:

1. **Decision requested** — one or two sentences, with the person who will decide.
2. **Context and constraint** — why this now, and what bounds the choice.
3. **Options considered** — including the status quo, with the criteria used to compare.
4. **Evidence** — measured facts with sources and dates, separated from interpretation.
5. **Recommendation and reasoning** — the chosen option and why it wins on the stated criteria.
6. **Risks and reversal conditions** — what could go wrong and what would change the answer.
7. **Decision record** — the outcome, who made it, and the review date.

The recommendation comes after the evidence, not before. A memo written to justify a conclusion will arrange the facts to support it; one written to test a decision will let the facts lead.

## A worked example of a weak and strong memo

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

**Weak version:** "We are considering moving billing to a new provider. The current provider has been slow and support is frustrating. The new provider looks better. Let's discuss."

**Strong version, using the structure:**

- **Decision:** whether to migrate billing to Provider B within the next quarter. Owner: the CFO.
- **Constraint:** the migration must not interrupt invoice delivery; the team can absorb one engineer-quarter.
- **Options:** stay with A; migrate to B; renegotiate with A.
- **Evidence:** current provider's invoice delivery averaged 3.2 days late over the last six months; two outages exceeded four hours; B's published SLA is 99.9% with a 30-day termination clause; migration estimated at one engineer-quarter.
- **Recommendation:** renegotiate with A first, with migration as fallback. The SLA gap is real but the migration cost is large; a contractual remedy may capture most of the benefit.
- **Risks:** renegotiation may fail, costing a quarter before migration starts. If A misses two more SLAs during negotiation, migrate.
- **Record:** decision and review date filled in after the meeting.

Three lessons follow:

1. **The weak version hides the alternative.** Staying put is presented as a complaint, not an option with a cost.
2. **The strong version is falsifiable.** A reviewer can dispute the 3.2-day figure or the SLA claim, and there is a stated trigger for changing course.
3. **The recommendation changed.** Starting with "the new provider looks better" and doing the analysis produced a different answer — renegotiate first. A memo that lets the analysis run is a decision tool, not a sales document.

## Writing for the reviewer

A reviewer reads a memo under time pressure and with a specific job: decide whether to accept, reject, or change the recommendation. Three writing choices help:

**Put the decision in the first line.** A reviewer who has to read three paragraphs to discover what is being asked will form an opinion before seeing the evidence. State the question and the owner immediately.

**Use tables for comparisons.** Options and evidence are easier to check in a table than in prose, because a reader can scan a single column. Prose is for the reasoning that connects the comparison to the recommendation.

**Keep it short enough to finish.** A memo that nobody reads has no effect on the decision. Length should be a function of the decision's consequence, not of the author's desire to show thoroughness. An appendix can hold the detail a reviewer may want.

The opposite of this is a document written to demonstrate how much work went into the analysis. That document is a performance, and reviewers can tell.

## What the memo cannot do

**It cannot replace conversation.** A memo makes a decision reviewable; it does not build agreement. The discussion should start from the memo, not re-derive it.

**It cannot remove uncertainty.** Reversal conditions are a way of admitting what is unknown. A memo that claims certainty it does not have is less useful than one that states the uncertainty plainly.

**It is not a status report.** Progress updates belong elsewhere. A memo that mixes the two buries the decision under information nobody needs to make it.

The counterargument — that writing a memo is slow — is fair for small decisions. The format earns its cost when the decision is consequential, contested, or likely to be revisited. For a routine choice, a short note with the recommendation and the reversal condition is enough.

## A decision test

1. **Can you state the decision in one sentence?**
2. **Is the owner named?**
3. **Are the options, including doing nothing, explicit?**
4. **Can a reader separate the measured facts from your interpretation?**
5. **Is the recommendation supported by the stated criteria?**
6. **What would change your mind, and is that written down?**
7. **Will someone record the outcome and the review date?**
