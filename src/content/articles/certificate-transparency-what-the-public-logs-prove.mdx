Every publicly trusted TLS certificate is now, in practice, a public record. Certificate Transparency (CT) turned certificate issuance into something an outsider can watch: logs publish what they received, and browsers refuse certificates that arrive without proof they were logged. That sounds like a solved problem. It is not — it is a *discoverability* mechanism, and the gap between "logged" and "legitimate" is where operational incidents actually happen.

**Direct Answer:** CT proves that a specific certificate was submitted to at least one publicly monitored append-only log, not that the certificate is legitimate, that the domain owner wanted it, or that it is still safe to trust. The protocol guarantees *discoverability of misissuance*; everything after that — monitoring, response, revocation, reissuance — is operational work that the protocol deliberately leaves outside itself.

## What CT actually guarantees

RFC 6962 (June 2013, Experimental) defines the core model. Each log is a single, ever-growing, append-only Merkle tree of submitted certificates. When a log accepts a valid chain it must immediately return a **Signed Certificate Timestamp (SCT)** — and the RFC is explicit about what that promise is: the SCT is the log's promise to incorporate the certificate in the Merkle tree within a fixed time known as the **Maximum Merge Delay (MMD)**.

Two consequences follow directly from that design:

1. **Presentation.** RFC 6962 states that TLS servers must present an SCT from one or more logs to the client together with the certificate.
2. **Rejection.** The same RFC states that TLS clients must reject certificates that do not have a valid SCT for the end-entity certificate.

That is the entire guarantee. It is a *presence* guarantee, not a *validity* guarantee. The RFC is careful about this in its informal introduction: the logs "do not themselves prevent misissue, but they ensure that interested parties (particularly those named in certificates) can detect such misissuance."

Read that clause again — **"particularly those named in certificates."** Detection is only useful if the domain owner is actually watching. CT converts a silent misissuance into a *public* event; it does not convert it into a resolved one.

## The v2.0 revision and what changed

RFC 9162 (December 2021, Experimental) obsoletes RFC 6962 and revises the protocol after years of deployment feedback. The changes are mostly structural, but three are worth internalizing:

- **The TLS extension changed.** The `signed_certificate_timestamp` extension was replaced by a `transparency_info` extension, which carries CT artifacts more generally.
- **Log identity changed.** Logs are now identified by an OID rather than by the hash of their public key, and log artifact extensions live in IANA registries.
- **Proofs were added.** Clients can now request an inclusion proof (proving a specific entry is in the tree) and consistency proofs (proving two tree heads are consistent) rather than trusting the log's word. There are also defined **monitor** and **auditor** roles: a monitor watches logs for certificates of interest — for example, all certificates covering a domain you own.

The monitor role is the operational hook. If you run infrastructure for a domain, a monitor over CT logs is how you find out that someone obtained a certificate for your name.

## What browsers actually enforce

The protocol says clients must reject uncertified certificates. Chrome's published CT policy is the concrete implementation, and it is more specific than the RFC.

Under that policy, a publicly trusted TLS certificate must be **CT Compliant**, which requires a set of SCTs satisfying one of two paths:

| Delivery path | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| **Embedded SCTs** (in the certificate's `SignedCertificateTimestampList` X.509 extension) | At least one SCT from a log that was Qualified, Usable, or ReadOnly at the time of check; SCTs from at least **N** distinct logs (N = 2 for certificate lifetime ≤ 180 days, N = 3 for lifetime > 180 days); and among those, at least two SCTs from **distinct log operators** |
| **SCTs delivered via TLS** | At least two SCTs from logs that were Qualified, Usable, or ReadOnly at the time of check, from at least two distinct log operators |

Two details matter in practice. First, the log's *state* at the time of check is evaluated, not just at issuance — logs move through Pending, Qualified, Usable, ReadOnly, Retired, and Rejected states, and a retired log's SCTs stop counting for fresh certificates. Second, the policy notes that in Chrome versions prior to version 148, Chrome also accepted SCTs embedded in stapled OCSP responses; those no longer count toward the requirements above.

Chrome also documents the practical consequence for operators: most TLS servers do not support the TLS extension, so **CAs should be prepared to embed SCTs into issued certificates** to ensure successful validation.

## The current log landscape

Chrome's CT log list is the machine-readable source of truth, published as JSON at `https://www.gstatic.com/ct/log_list/v3/log_list.json`. When I checked it on 2026-11-13, the list was at version 97.0 with a `log_list_timestamp` of `2026-11-13T13:36:42Z`, containing **26 logs operated by 11 organizations** — Google, Cloudflare, DigiCert, Sectigo, Let's Encrypt, TrustAsia, Geomys, IPng Networks, Microsec, GoDaddy, and HARICA.

Individual entries carry their own parameters. One example, Google's `Argon2026h2`, listed an MMD of 86,400 seconds (24 hours) and, at that moment, a read-only tree size of 3,501,950,757 entries. MMD matters because it is the *worst-case* delay between an SCT being issued and the certificate actually appearing in the log — the window during which a monitor cannot yet see the entry.

## Checking your own certificates

You do not need to run a monitor to verify the basics. `openssl` will show you the SCTs on a live endpoint:

```bash
echo | openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com -showcerts 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -text \
  | grep -A2 "1.3.6.1.4.1.11129.2.4.2"
```

That OID is the `SignedCertificateTimestampList` extension. On 2026-11-14, running the same check against three large production hosts returned:

```text
www.cloudflare.com : issuer Let's Encrypt YE1, 4 certs in chain, SCT extension present
github.com         : issuer Sectigo DV E36,  3 certs in chain, SCT extension present
www.mozilla.org    : issuer Let's Encrypt YR1, 3 certs in chain, SCT extension present
```

None of those three connections returned a stapled OCSP response in my check — which, given the Chrome 148 change above, is consistent with SCTs arriving as embedded extensions rather than inside a stapled OCSP response.

For ongoing detection rather than a point check, the RFC 9162 monitor model is the mechanism: poll each log's signed tree head, fetch new entries, and filter for certificates covering domains you control. The same document defines the auditor role — an entity that verifies a log is behaving correctly, which matters because RFC 9162 notes the auditing mechanisms "can be circumvented by a misbehaving log that shows different, inconsistent views of itself to different clients," so logs have to be treated as trusted third parties.

## Where CT still fails you

Three failure modes are worth naming explicitly, because they are all operational rather than protocol-level:

- **Issuance from a compromised CA.** If a CA is breached, CT faithfully records the attacker's certificates — and every domain in the CA's scope becomes a monitoring target at once. CT makes the blast radius *visible*, not smaller.
- **SCTs for the wrong thing.** A certificate can be perfectly CT-compliant and still be the certificate you did not want issued. Compliance is a property of the certificate, not of the decision to issue it.
- **Silent expiry of trust signals.** Because log states and Chrome's policy both change over time, a certificate issued and validated years ago can stop being compliant without anything on your side changing. A renewal that assumes yesterday's policy is a renewal that can fail.

## Practical takeaways

1. **Treat CT as an alerting source, not a control.** Its output is a signal to investigate, not a verdict.
2. **Monitor your own names.** The protocol's value is concentrated in the "particularly those named in certificates" case, and that requires someone watching.
3. **Check SCT delivery, not just certificate validity.** A site with a perfectly valid chain and no usable SCTs fails in CT-enforcing browsers and passes in `curl`. That asymmetry is why certificate problems sometimes look like "the site works for me."
4. **Do not assume stapling covers you.** If your CT evidence arrives via stapled OCSP responses, it stopped counting in Chrome 148.
