Growth is not just about getting new customers. It is about keeping the ones you have.

**Direct answer:** Customer retention is the practice of keeping existing customers over time. It is cheaper than acquisition, increases lifetime value, and creates compounding growth. A business that loses customers as fast as it gains them is on a treadmill; a business that retains customers builds a moat. Retention is measured by churn rate, repeat purchase rate, and net revenue retention—and improved by product quality, customer experience, and consistent value delivery.

## Why retention beats acquisition

Acquiring a new customer costs money: marketing, sales, onboarding, discounts. Retaining an existing customer costs less because the relationship already exists. Moreover:

- **LTV increases with time.** The longer a customer stays, the more they pay and the more the acquisition cost is amortized.
- **Retained customers buy more.** Trust leads to upgrades, cross-sells, and referrals.
- **Referrals reduce acquisition cost.** Satisfied customers bring new customers at near-zero cost.
- **Feedback improves the product.** Long-term customers provide better insights than one-time buyers.

A 5% increase in retention can increase profits by 25% to 95%, depending on the industry. The math favors keeping customers over constantly replacing them.

## Measuring retention

- **Churn rate:** the percentage of customers lost in a period. Monthly churn of 5% means replacing half the base every year just to stay flat.
- **Retention rate:** the percentage of customers who remain. 95% monthly retention = 5% churn.
- **Repeat purchase rate:** the percentage of customers who buy again. Relevant for transactional businesses.
- **Net revenue retention (NRR):** revenue from a cohort over time, including expansions and churn. NRR above 100% means the business grows from existing customers alone.

## Why customers leave

Customers churn for reasons inside and outside your control:

- **Product does not deliver value.** The core promise is unmet.
- **Poor onboarding.** Customers do not reach their first success quickly enough.
- **Bad support.** Problems are not resolved, or resolution is slow.
- **Price exceeds perceived value.** The customer no longer believes the product is worth it.
- **Life changes.** The customer's situation changes (job, location, business closure).
- **Competitor offers something better.** Price, features, or experience.

Some churn is unavoidable. The goal is to minimize controllable churn.

## Improving retention

1. **Fix onboarding.** The first days determine whether the customer stays. Help them reach value quickly.
2. **Listen to support.** Support tickets reveal where the product confuses or fails customers.
3. **Segment by behavior.** Identify customers at risk (declining usage, support tickets, late payments) and intervene before they leave.
4. **Deliver consistent value.** The product must keep solving the problem, not just at launch.
5. **Build relationships.** Customers stay for people, not just products. Account management and customer success matter.
6. **Make leaving hard (ethically).** Data, workflows, and relationships built in your product create switching costs. This is legitimate when it reflects real value, not lock-in tricks.

## Retention is a company-wide responsibility

Retention is not just a customer success metric. It is affected by product quality (engineering), pricing (finance), expectations (marketing), and support (operations). Every team influences whether customers stay.
