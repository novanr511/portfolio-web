Legal research has three properties that make AI both useful and dangerous: the sources are authoritative and citable, the language is precise, and a wrong answer can affect a real decision. A tool that helps find and summarise law is valuable; a tool that invents a citation is a liability. The discipline is to keep the source visible at every step.

**Direct answer:** Use AI to retrieve relevant authorities, summarise what they say, and check that every citation points to a real source supporting the claim. Anthropic's legal summarisation guide is the primary use-case reference, and the retrieval article covers the search mechanism. The boundary: a model can locate and condense authority, but it cannot replace legal judgement about how a source applies to a specific matter, which the contract-review article explains for adjacent work.

## Three tasks

**Retrieval.** Find statutes, regulations, and case law relevant to a question. This is a search problem, as the retrieval article explains, and the measure is recall — did the search find the controlling authority?

**Summarisation.** Condense a long judgment or statute into the points that matter for the question. The reduce-hallucinations article covers grounding: every claim should trace to the text.

**Citation checking.** Verify that every cited source exists and supports the claim attached to it. This is the hardest and most important task, because a plausible but nonexistent citation is a specific failure mode of language models, as the detection article discusses for generated text generally.

## A worked research workflow

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A lawyer asks a research assistant: "What does the governing statute say about notice periods for this contract type?"

The workflow:

1. The system retrieves three candidate sources from a licensed database.
2. It summarises the relevant sections, quoting the operative language.
3. It produces an answer with a citation to section and paragraph for each claim.

Three checks decide whether the result is safe:

1. **Citation existence.** Does the cited section exist? A check against the source database catches invented references, which the reduce-hallucinations article treats as a core verification.
2. **Citation support.** Does the cited passage actually say what the summary claims? A real citation attached to a wrong claim is a subtler failure, which the evaluation-rubric article explains for groundedness scoring.
3. **Completeness.** Was the controlling authority retrieved at all? A clean summary of the wrong source is worse than no answer, which the reranking article covers for retrieval quality.

Three conclusions follow:

- **The lawyer verifies every citation**, as the human-in-the-loop design requires.
- **The system's value is in finding and condensing**, not in deciding how the law applies, which the governance article addresses for high-stakes domains.
- **Provenance is the product** — a summary without traceable sources is not useful legal work, as the data-governance article argues.

## Building a citation-checking habit

A citation check is only useful if it is routine and specific. Three practices make it reliable:

**Check in two directions.** Verify both that a cited source exists and that it supports the exact claim, as the reduce-hallucinations article distinguishes grounding from mere reference. A real citation attached to the wrong proposition is the subtle failure.

**Keep the source text adjacent.** A summary whose cited passage sits next to it is faster to check than one that requires opening a database. The context-engineering article explains why placing material near the decision improves use.

**Record what was checked.** A note of which citations were verified and when gives the reviewer a trail and makes a later challenge answerable, which the data-governance article requires for relied-upon records.

These habits are slow relative to generating a summary, which is precisely why they must be designed into the workflow rather than left to the reviewer's discretion at the end of a busy day.

## What legal AI cannot do

**It cannot give legal advice.** A summary is a research aid, not an opinion, which the EU AI Act and governance discussions treat as a high-stakes distinction.

**It cannot guarantee the law is current.** A retrieved statute may have been amended, which the monitoring article frames as a change trigger.

**It cannot replace judgement.** Applying authority to a client's facts is the lawyer's work, as the contract-review article explains for the adjacent task of clause evaluation.

The counterargument — that a competent lawyer will catch errors — is true and does not remove the need for tool design that makes errors visible, which the security checklist argues for boundaries that do not depend on vigilance alone.

## A decision test

1. **Retrieve from authoritative, licensed sources** with a defined scope.
2. **Ground every summary claim** in the retrieved text.
3. **Check that citations exist** against the source database.
4. **Check that citations support** the specific claim, not just the topic.
5. **Measure recall** of controlling authority on a labelled set, as the embedding-selection article recommends.
6. **Keep legal judgement with a person**, as the oversight article explains.
7. **Verify currency** of sources with a dated check.
