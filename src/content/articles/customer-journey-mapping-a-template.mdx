Journey maps often fail because they are built in a workshop from assumptions. The resulting poster describes the team's imagination of the customer, not the customer's experience. A useful map is a hypothesis with evidence attached, and every claim on it should be traceable to something a customer actually did or said.

**Direct answer:** A journey map should record stages, customer actions, questions, emotions, evidence, and the business response — with the evidence column distinguishing observed behaviour from interpretation. Demand-generation material explains the decision stages, and AAPOR's standards apply when the evidence comes from interviews or surveys. The boundary: a map is a simplification of a nonlinear process, not a literal path every customer follows.

## Six columns

**Stage** — the coarse phase: awareness, consideration, purchase, onboarding, use, renewal.

**Customer action** — what the person actually does, in observable terms. "Visits the pricing page" is observable; "evaluates options" is an interpretation.

**Question** — what they are trying to answer at that moment. This is usually inferred, so label it as such.

**Emotion** — a hypothesis about how they feel. Useful for designing support, dangerous when treated as measured fact.

**Evidence** — the source for the row: interview quote, support ticket, log data, survey result. This is the column that makes the map honest.

**Business response** — what the company currently does, and where the gap is.

The evidence column is what separates a map from a poster. If a row has no evidence, mark it as an assumption.

## A worked example of one stage

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A SaaS company maps the consideration stage:

| Column | Entry |
| --- | --- |
| Stage | Consideration |
| Action | Visits pricing page, then leaves; returns two days later |
| Question | "What will this cost once we include seats and support?" |
| Emotion | Uncertain about hidden costs (hypothesis) |
| Evidence | 40 interview notes; 3,000 pricing-page sessions with a 60% exit rate |
| Response | Pricing page lists per-seat cost but not support tiers; no calculator |

Three decisions follow:

1. **The evidence supports the action, not the emotion.** The exit rate is measured; the feeling of uncertainty is a hypothesis. The map should label it.
2. **The gap is specific.** Support tiers are missing from the page, which matches the inferred question. Adding them is a bounded change with a testable result.
3. **The metric for the change is defined before it is made.** Return visits to the pricing page and demo requests from that page, measured against the prior month.

Three cautions apply: interview notes may overrepresent customers who agreed to be interviewed; session data does not reveal motive; and the proposed fix should be tested against the stated metric, not judged by a new map.

## Building the map without inventing evidence

**Start from behaviour.** Log data, support tickets, and sales notes describe what people did. Build the observable rows first.

**Add questions from direct contact.** Interviews and support conversations reveal what people were trying to decide. Record quotes with permission and mark the sample size.

**Label inferences.** Anything about emotion or motivation should be tagged as a hypothesis until it is tested. AAPOR's standards on question wording matter here: a leading interview question produces a leading "evidence" entry.

**Review the map with customers where possible.** Showing a draft to five customers and asking where it is wrong is a cheap correction.

## What a journey map cannot do

**It cannot predict individual behaviour.** A map describes a common pattern, not what any one person will do. Treating it as a script for every customer produces generic service.

**It goes stale.** Products, pricing, and competitors change. Tie a review to material changes, not to an annual workshop.

**It does not fix the gaps it identifies.** The map is a diagnostic. The work is in the response column, with owners and metrics.

The counterargument — that a map is just a diagram — is fair when it contains only opinions. With evidence and a response column, it becomes a prioritisation tool: the rows with the strongest evidence and the largest gaps are the ones to fix first.

## A decision test

1. **Build observable rows first** from logs and records.
2. **Add questions from interviews** with sample sizes stated.
3. **Label emotions and motives as hypotheses.**
4. **Fill the evidence column** or mark the row as an assumption.
5. **Write the current business response** and the gap.
6. **Prioritise by evidence strength** and gap size.
7. **Review on change** and test each fix against a defined metric.
