AI models are built, not born.

**Direct answer:** Training an AI model is a pipeline: collect and prepare data, choose a model architecture, train on compute infrastructure, evaluate performance, tune hyperparameters, and deploy. Each stage involves trade-offs between cost, accuracy, speed, and risk. Understanding the pipeline helps businesses set realistic expectations about timeline, cost, and what can go wrong.

## The training pipeline

**1. Data collection and preparation.** Raw data is cleaned, labeled, and split into training, validation, and test sets. Garbage data produces garbage models, no matter how advanced the architecture.

**2. Architecture selection.** Choose a model type—neural network, decision tree, transformer, etc.—based on the problem. More complex does not always mean better.

**3. Training.** The model sees examples, makes predictions, and adjusts internal parameters to reduce error. This requires compute (GPUs/TPUs), time, and monitoring.

**4. Evaluation.** The model is tested on data it has never seen. Metrics depend on the task: accuracy, F1, BLEU, perplexity, etc.

**5. Tuning.** Hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, layers) are adjusted to improve performance without overfitting.

**6. Deployment.** The model is packaged, served via API, and monitored in production. Training is not the end; it is the beginning of operations.

## Data is the differentiator

Two teams using the same architecture and compute can get different results because their data differs. Quality, quantity, relevance, and labeling consistency matter more than marginal model improvements. A simple model on excellent data often beats a complex model on poor data.

## Compute and cost

Training large models is expensive. GPU hours, electricity, and engineer time add up. Smaller models trained efficiently can outperform larger models trained wastefully. The trend is toward smaller, specialized models for specific tasks rather than one giant model for everything.

## Evaluation is not accuracy alone

A model can be accurate on average and harmful in edge cases. Evaluation must consider:

- **Bias:** does the model perform differently across groups?
- **Robustness:** does it fail on slightly different inputs?
- **Explainability:** can you understand why it made a decision?
- **Latency:** is it fast enough for production use?

## Deployment changes everything

A model that works in a notebook may fail in production: data drifts, users behave unexpectedly, infrastructure breaks. Models need monitoring, retraining, and rollback plans. MLOps—machine learning operations—is the discipline of managing this lifecycle.
