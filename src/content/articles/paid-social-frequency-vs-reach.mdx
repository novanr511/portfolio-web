Reach and frequency are two ways to spend the same budget: one buys breadth of exposure, the other buys repetition among people already reached, and a plan chooses which to prioritise before delivery begins. Google's [About audience segments](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2497941?hl=en) documents a weekly target frequency that first serves an impression to as many matching users as possible, then makes those users eligible for later exposures over seven days. On 22 November 2026 a curation pass reduced 1,004 templated articles to a curated, restorable set behind an allowlist ([curation case study](/projects/seo-content-curation)): one rule applied to every URL. The RAG agent's `number_audit` ran 193 checks showing prose matched retrieved files ([build log](/projects/rag-research-agent)), and the BTC study paired 92.5% model accuracy with 94.0% for a zero-parameter sign rule at Holm-adjusted p = 1.0 ([study record](/projects/btc-monthly-ml)). Each is a rule or a comparator fixed before a conclusion was drawn. Every illustrative figure below is labelled ASSUMED or DERIVED.

## What does a reach-versus-frequency decision actually allocate?

A reach-versus-frequency decision allocates one budget between distinct people and repeat exposures. Reach counts people who saw an ad at least once; frequency is the average exposures per reached person. The plan fixes the trade-off in advance, and the report records what that trade-off purchased.

Google's [target frequency documentation](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2497941?hl=en) makes the ordering explicit: a first impression goes to as many matching users as possible, after which those users become eligible for remaining frequencies within a seven-day window. That is an allocation rule, not a finding about how many exposures are optimal. A frequency cap is another rule: a ceiling on repeated delivery to the same person.

The arithmetic linking delivery figures is an identity. With 100,000 impressions ASSUMED and 50,000 reached people ASSUMED, frequency is 2.0 DERIVED, because 100,000 divided by 50,000 = 2. The same 100,000 impressions ASSUMED spread over 25,000 people ASSUMED gives 4.0 DERIVED. Nothing in that division says which allocation is worth buying; it only states how the two levers trade against each other for a fixed impression total.

## Why does a bigger impression count fail as an allocation rule?

A bigger impression count can mean more new people or more repeats among the same people, and the total alone cannot distinguish them. Without a stated distribution, a rising frequency is either planned repetition or an audience served again because it is cheap. Decide the distribution before reading the total.

Platform delivery follows the rule it is given. The target frequency setting puts breadth first and repetition second within its seven-day window, so a plan that leaves frequency uncapped can concentrate impressions wherever delivery is easiest. That is a property of the mechanism, not a measured response curve. The point at which repetition stops helping a particular audience is not measured in any record cited here, and treating a high average as proof of fatigue or of persuasion would invent a result.

A reach-first plan makes the opposite bet: it pays for many first exposures and accepts a lower average. Neither bet is "more" in any useful sense. Both spend the same impressions on different patterns of people. The question a budget review should ask is which pattern the plan set out to buy, and whether the delivery matched it, rather than whether the impression total grew.

## How do credit rules and counting windows change what an allocation looks like?

Credit rules and counting windows decide which exposures appear to have worked. A repeat impression can receive full credit under one rule and share it under another; the same spend can look efficient or wasteful without changing. Record both settings before comparing allocations, because a figure carries the rule that produced it.

Google's [attribution-model definition](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6259715?hl=en) makes the choice visible: last click assigns all credit to the final interaction, while data-driven distributes it using the account's own data. First-click, linear, time-decay and position-based models are no longer supported, and affected conversions were upgraded to data-driven. A comparison run under a rule that has since been retired is a comparison to a different instrument.

The [conversion-window documentation](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/3123169?hl=en) adds a second setting: a window of 1 to 90 days, with a 30-day default for Search and Display and a one-day default for view-through conversions. Changes apply going forward, not retroactively. A frequency-heavy plan may collect more final-touch credit simply because it generates more late touches; that is an expectation from the rule's mechanics, labelled ASSUMED here, not an observed lift. The same page notes that the model-comparison report can filter out campaigns without enough data, so a missing row can be an eligibility artefact.

## What would a pre-set allocation decision look like with labelled numbers?

Fix the exposure you are buying, the budget ceiling, and the comparison that will judge the plan before any report is reviewed. Then choose the allocation whose trade-off you can defend and log the rule with its date. Rewriting it after the report would turn a decision into a story.

A worked structure, with no platform rate or client result attached:

1. **ASSUMED:** a fixed budget buys 100,000 impressions under a stated cost per thousand, invented for illustration.
2. **ASSUMED:** a reach floor of 50,000 distinct people. **DERIVED:** if the floor holds, the frequency ceiling is 2.0, since 100,000 ÷ 50,000 = 2.
3. **ASSUMED:** an alternative plan targets frequency 4.0. **DERIVED:** at the same impression total, implied reach is 25,000, since 100,000 ÷ 4 = 25,000.
4. **DERIVED:** the comparison is 50,000 people at 2.0 versus 25,000 people at 4.0, not "good" against "bad". Choose the pattern the campaign can justify to a reader before the numbers arrive.
5. **DERIVED:** no outcome figure is asserted. The check is whether delivery matched the chosen pattern and whether a later comparison survives the same credit rule and window.

The arithmetic is scaffolding for a decision, not evidence of performance. A team that treats 100,000 impressions as a stable property of the campaign has already made an unstated assumption about how those impressions are distributed. Writing the assumption down is the gain.

## What can the first-hand records tell you about rules and outcomes?

The 22 November 2026 curation applied one allowlist rule to the whole archive, and its case study records structural before-and-after facts while leaving the traffic outcome blank. That is the shape of an allocation decision: a rule written first, its consequences recorded, and no outcome invented to make the record look stronger.

As of that dated snapshot, 1,004 templated articles became a curated, restorable set of about 34 live pieces; distinct tags fell from 810 to roughly ten; 21 colliding category slugs resolved to zero in the curated set; `llms.txt` had been roughly 1,034 lines and `llms-full.txt` about 7 MB generated per request. Those are structural facts about a system, read from the [migration record](/articles/why-i-killed-966-articles) and the [curation case study](/projects/seo-content-curation). No traffic outcome was measured before or after the change.

The other two records sharpen the point. The RAG agent's `number_audit` completed 193 checks on 18 November 2026 and found prose consistent with retrieved files; that is a consistency result, not evidence that the underlying comparison was sound. In the BTC study the model reached 92.5% accuracy at day 25 while a zero-parameter `sign(mtd_ret)` rule reached 94.0%, with Holm-adjusted p-values of 1.0 across model-versus-rule comparisons. A comparator fixed before the numbers were opened is what keeps 92.5% from reading as an unqualified win. Frequency planning needs the same discipline: name the alternative allocation before the delivery report is seen.

## How should you compare two allocations without claiming lift?

Compare allocations under the same credit rule and counting window, report reach and frequency side by side, and label outcome claims as hypotheses until a withheld group supports them. Keep the rule, window and decision in one dated record so a later reader can tell whether a change came from the plan or the measurement.

The instrument for a causal claim is a comparison the dashboard does not contain. Vaver and Koehler's [geo-experiment method](https://research.google/pubs/measuring-ad-effectiveness-using-geo-experiments/) pairs markets on pre-period outcomes, checks power before launch, screens for spillover and fixes a stop rule in advance. It estimates an area-level effect under stated assumptions; it is a method description here, not an experiment run for this article. The design choices are covered in more detail in [How to Get Geo Experiments Right](/articles/how-to-get-geo-experiments-right).

This article is about which exposure pattern a budget buys, a separate question from what a platform metric defines and from how a budget is divided across channels. For the definitions, see [What Paid Social Actually Measures](/articles/what-paid-social-actually-measures); for the budget-level trade-offs, see [Paid Social Trade-Offs](/articles/paid-social-trade-offs). Keeping the three questions apart prevents an allocation choice from being defended with a metric definition, or a metric definition from being sold as evidence of incrementality. [How to Evaluate Incrementality Testing](/articles/how-to-evaluate-incrementality-testing) addresses the comparison itself.

## What decision can you make today with the evidence available?

You can pick an allocation rule, state its cost and exposure target, and bound the decision with a dated recheck. That is a defensible choice under uncertainty, though the platform report cannot certify that repetition caused the result. Keep reversible bets small and record which rule produced every figure you quote.

In practice, write the reach floor or frequency target beside the credit model and counting window in one document, add the date each was set, and schedule one read against the pre-stated pattern. If the plan changes mid-flight, start a new row rather than editing the old one, because a time series changes meaning when the rule behind it changes. When the decision is large and irreversible, the credited number is insufficient on its own; a withheld group or a documented pause comparison should come first, with its limits stated in the same record.

What the available numbers do not establish here is the incremental effect of one pattern over the other. That gap stays visible instead of being filled with a platform benchmark or an invented fatigue curve. A frequency-versus-reach decision made this way is an honest allocation under uncertainty: explicit about the rule, modest about the claim, and checkable by the next person who reads the log.
