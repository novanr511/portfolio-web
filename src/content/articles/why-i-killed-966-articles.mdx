I published 944 articles in three days. Not for a client, not as part of a commissioned content plan — on my own site, novandri.com. By the time the batch finished, the site held **1,004 articles** and **1,287,042 words**. By the time I was done with it, roughly **34** were left.

This is the story of that decision, by the person who made it. It is not a general argument about AI content. I did not measure traffic before or after; I have no ranking chart, no impressions graph, no AI-citation count. What I have is a set of things I observed in the files, a specific tell that made the corpus feel wrong, and a decision I can explain without dressing it up as a result.

## The three-day publish burst

The date range is 16–18 November 2026. Nine hundred and forty-four articles went live across those three days. That fact is visible in the repository with no analytics required.

A site that publishes nearly a thousand pieces in seventy-two hours is emptying a queue, not responding to demand. I had built a generation pipeline, pointed it at a topic map, and let it fill the site. From a distance: a sitemap with a thousand URLs, a tag cloud, an impressive word count. Up close, it was a fast way to turn a topic list into a million words no one had asked for.

**1,287,042 words** is a number designed to impress. It also describes almost nothing about usefulness. I know how many words I published because it is trivial to count; I do not know how many people read them, because I had set up nothing capable of telling me. That asymmetry — total visibility into output, zero into outcome — should have been the warning. It took longer than it should have.

## The tell: "as the earlier article warns"

The moment the site stopped feeling like a portfolio and started feeling like a machine was reading one of its own articles and noticing a sentence I had seen before.

An article would make a claim and support it with a phrase like *"as the earlier article on this topic warns"* — except the "earlier article" was another piece from the same batch, from the same outline, with no more evidence behind it. A generated article citing a sibling as an authority. A closed loop wearing the costume of a literature review.

Once I saw it, I saw it everywhere. A piece would hedge with "experts note that" and restate what the previous piece had asserted. A paragraph would refer to "the research" without naming research. Cross-references pointed sideways into the same corpus, creating the texture of corroboration — a link, a citation-shaped phrase — while the chain of evidence led nowhere.

That is the tell: **self-referential authority**. Not a misspelling or repetition — those are easy to spot and fix. It is the structural imitation of a source: text that has learned what a supported claim *looks* like and produces the appearance without the thing underneath. On a small scale, a quirky error. At a thousand-article scale, the site's entire epistemics.

To be precise: I am not saying every AI-assisted article is bad. I am saying that a corpus assembled this way had no mechanism for distinguishing a claim from a claim-shaped string — and I could not put my name on the result.

## Internal links are not corroboration

The self-referential pattern worked as well as it did because of a misconception I had absorbed from years of technical SEO work: links are signals of structure, and structure is a proxy for quality.

That is true of a search index in a limited, mechanical sense. It is not true as a reasoning principle. If article A says "X is true" and article B says "as article A explains, X is true," you have one claim and one pointer. A link tells a crawler that pages relate; it does not tell a reader the claim survived contact with reality. Two generated pages agreeing because they share an outline are the same observation twice.

This was the mistake I had to unlearn, because it is easy to make in SEO. We are trained to see internal linking as a tool: distribute authority, flatten architecture, guide crawlers. All of that is real, but it measures **relationships between pages**, not **between claims and evidence**. When the pages are empty, distributing authority moves an empty quantity around.

The AI-facing files made it worse. `llms.txt` had grown to about **1,034 lines** — a catalogue listing everything and foregrounding nothing. `llms-full.txt` was about **7 MB**, generated on every request with no `Cache-Control` header. Both are lists of the same corpus. A larger list of pages that cite each other does not create evidence; it scales the illusion.

The taxonomy told the same story. There were **810 tags**, of which **501 belonged to a single article**. More than half the tag system was singletons — labels generated per piece rather than categories a reader would navigate. There were also **21 colliding category slugs**: different categories resolving to the same URL token, which is an ambiguity in the site's own routing. I had optimised the shape of a huge site without ever asking whether the shape described anything a person wanted.

## What the archive actually was

The change was unglamorous: an **allowlist filter**. An article is either on the short list of pieces rewritten against real evidence, or it is not published. The default flipped. Before, anything generated was live unless removed; after, nothing is live unless it earns an entry.

Thirty-three articles were rewritten with first-hand detail and verifiable claims. The rest were archived. The live set is now roughly **34 articles**, with around **10 tags** and no colliding slugs in the curated set. The `llms.txt` is generated from that smaller set, and the 7 MB per-request file is no longer produced from the full archive.

Every one of those numbers is a property of the site, not an outcome. I cannot say the archive improved crawl efficiency or AI visibility, because I did not measure either. I can say the site is smaller, the taxonomy is coherent, and I can read the whole `llms.txt` in a minute. Modest facts, kept in the modest column.

## What I sacrificed

I sacrificed the appearance of authority by volume. A thousand-article site looks substantial; in a portfolio review, a four-digit sitemap does work that thirty-four URLs cannot. I gave that up knowingly.

I also gave up the benefit of the doubt on long-tail coverage. Some removed pages targeted narrow queries that may have had a small, genuine audience; I did not measure which, if any, earned traffic. I will not call them worthless — I have no evidence for that either. But with no way to separate useful pages from noise, keeping everything is not neutral. It lets the generator decide for you.

There was a personal cost too. When the audience expects proof, every remaining article has to carry its weight; there is no hiding in the long tail. The 33 rewritten pieces and case studies have to be inspected, and I have to be comfortable with what an inspector finds. That is more demanding than publishing a thousand pages and hoping.

## What replaced it

Proof did.

The rewritten articles are the ones where I could point at something: a file, a measured number, a test result, a repository. The rest of the load-bearing evidence on this site is in long-form case studies. The [BTC monthly study](/projects/btc-monthly-ml) documents 133 out-of-sample months and, more importantly, the result that the machine lost to a zero-parameter rule `sign(mtd_ret)` — 92.5% versus 94.0% at D25, with a Holm-adjusted *p*-value of 1.0. The [Maison WordPress theme](/projects/maison-wordpress-theme) has a live demo running straight from the repository. The [RAG research agent](/projects/rag-research-agent) is written up as a build log that says plainly what its `number_audit` could and could not establish. The [curation decision itself](/projects/seo-content-curation) is documented as a migration with an explicit "no traffic outcome measured" row.

The thing these have in common is not length or polish. It is that each one separates a design decision from a measured result. A design decision is something I chose — lexical retrieval, an allowlist, a graph instead of a chain. A measured result is something that happened and can be checked — 0 failed checks in the 193-check audit, 55 tests passed and 1 skipped, ~29 MB of theme images converted to about 500 KB of WebP. Conflating the two is how you end up with a self-referential article citing a sibling as an authority. That is the argument of this publication's standard: experience shown in the work, not asserted in a bio. I would rather write less and keep the boundary visible.

## What I still cannot claim

No traffic outcome was measured, before or after. No ranking improvement is claimed. No AI-citation lift is claimed. I did not run a GEO experiment with a control group, and I did not measure whether the shorter `llms.txt` changes anything for answer engines. The [evidence about those file formats](https://novandri.com/articles/llms-txt-robots-txt-and-ietf-drafts-sorting-ai-file-formats-by-evidence) is thinner than most of the marketing around it, and my own site is not a study that settles it.

I also cannot claim the 966 removed articles were wrong about their topics. I can claim they were unsupported in a specific way — that they used the shape of a source without the source — and that I was not willing to attach my name to that shape at scale. That is a judgement about my standards, not a verdict on every sentence in the archive.

## Why I'm writing this down

The easily tellable version is "I grew a site to a thousand articles, then used AI to clean it up" — smoother, and unhelpful. The useful version is awkward: I built a machine that produced a million words, could not tell which mattered, and the most concrete improvement I can demonstrate is that I stopped publishing most of them and wrote down how.

If you are judging my work from this portfolio, that is the signal I want you to have. Not that I have a large content library — I deliberately do not. That I can recognise when a number is impressive and meaningless at the same time, and act on the distinction even when the action costs me the impressive number.

The archive is gone. The evidence is what's left.
