A creator partnership records a creditable event; it does not, by itself, record a cause. A link, a code or a model can assign an order to a creator, and none of those steps tells you the order would have gone elsewhere without the post. Source-reported on 23 November 2026, Semrush counted citations across 18 user-generated-content domains and found that AI assistants sent 0.14% of sessions to them in August 2026 while direct traffic was 76.7% — a reminder that visibility and visits are separate observations. Google's attribution documentation states that changing the model changes how conversions are counted, not the sales themselves. The FTC's 16 CFR §255.5 requires disclosure of unexpected material connections. Two local artifacts hold the same line: the 22 November 2026 curation moved 1,004 templated articles to a curated set behind an allowlist, and the RAG agent's `number_audit` ran 193 checks establishing consistency between a memo and its files. Consistency is not validity, and credit is not cause.

## What can a partnership actually record?

It can record a delivered post, a link click, a redeemable code, a credited order and the terms under which payment follows. Each is a trace of activity under a rule. None is a measure of what the audience would have done without the creator.

A fee buys deliverables: a post on a named platform and date, with agreed disclosure and usage rights. A commission buys a recorded event defined in the terms, usually a purchase through a link or code. The deliverable is observable; the event is bookkeeping. Neither answers a counterfactual question.

The first column of a partnership report should list events with their definitions, not a single return figure. A credited order has a trigger, a window and a tie-break rule when touchpoints compete. The rules, their reversals and the trust consequences are covered in [Affiliate Program Design: Terms, Tracking, and Trust](/articles/affiliate-program-design-terms-tracking-and-trust). This article stays with what the events mean once the rules are settled.

## What does an attribution model change?

Google Ads' attribution documentation states that the model setting changes how conversions are counted and how automated bidding optimises, while the underlying sales stay the same. Last-click gives all credit to the final interaction; data-driven distributes it using patterns from the account's past conversion paths.

Google describes the data-driven model as comparing paths of converters and non-converters, giving more credit to interactions that recur on converting paths. It recommends at least 200 conversions and 2,000 ad interactions within 30 days (Google Ads Help, read 24 November 2026) — platform guidance, not a finding about creators.

A model can redistribute credit without adding information about the world. If a buyer saw a creator video, searched the brand and bought through a paid ad, last-click will credit the ad and a multi-touch model may split the credit. Neither output is a test of the video's effect. The mechanics and limits of credit allocation are set out in [Marketing Attribution: Multi-Touch Models, Incrementality, and Budget Allocation](/articles/marketing-attribution-multi-touch-models-incrementality-and-budget-allocation).

## What signals does the Semrush UGC study add?

Source-reported on 23 November 2026, Semrush tracked citations to 18 user-generated-content domains from January to August 2026: Google AI citations rose 52% while ChatGPT citations fell 44%, and TikTok more than tripled on both. AI assistants sent 0.14% of sessions to those domains in August 2026.

YouTube held about 45% of tracked AI Overview citations and Reddit roughly 52% of tracked ChatGPT citations; 17 of 18 sites gained monthly AI Overview citations, and TikTok's ChatGPT citations rose 313% (source-reported, 23 November 2026). Those figures show which third-party surfaces answers drew on in that sample.

They are context for a creator decision, not a return on it. The shares sit within the tracked group, the data are grouped by domain, and citation and referral analyses cover different products. A post can shape a description without producing a click, which is why the study keeps those fields apart; the counting rules are in [Measuring GEO: What You Can Actually Count](/articles/measuring-geo-what-you-can-actually-count).

## How do you separate credit from cause?

Credit comes from a rule that assigns an order to a touchpoint; cause needs a comparison in which exposure changes while the rest stays as steady as possible. A holdout, a matched group or a geo test supplies that comparison. A tracking link cannot.

Randomising exposure is the clearest design: one comparable group sees the creator, another does not, and the difference in purchases estimates what the partnership added. The cost is real — a holdout costs traffic and needs a decision rule fixed before the result is seen — so it is proportionate for material spend and excessive for a small test. Where exposure cannot be separated, the honest report is attributed activity with no lift claim; [Incrementality Testing Without a Platform](/articles/how-to-evaluate-incrementality-testing) covers the design choices.

A model comparison is not such a test. Asking Google Ads to show last-click beside data-driven changes the allocation of credit in two reports; it does not create a group that went unexposed. Treating the difference between two models as the creator's effect would confuse a change in accounting with a change in behaviour.

## What does a labelled worked example show?

The arithmetic below is hypothetical: every input is ASSUMED and every result DERIVED. It holds a creator fee fixed and changes which orders are counted, showing how the same credited events can support two different conclusions about value. No partnership was run or measured here.

**ASSUMED:** a creator fee of 2,000 units, 100 credited orders in the reporting window, revenue of 60 units per order and a contribution margin of 40%. **DERIVED:** contribution per order is 60 × 0.40 = 24 units; credited contribution is 100 × 24 = 2,400 units; return on the fee is 2,400 − 2,000 = +400 units.

Now add one ASSUMED input: 20 of those 100 buyers would have purchased anyway at full price. **DERIVED:** the counterfactual contribution is 20 × 24 = 480 units; the estimated incremental contribution is 2,400 − 480 = 1,920 units from 80 orders; return on the fee becomes 1,920 − 2,000 = −80 units. The credited orders did not change, and the sign of the decision flipped.

Those figures are arithmetic on invented inputs. The 20 percent is asserted to show the calculation, not observed in any audience, and no holdout was run. Reading the −80 as a measured loss would repeat the category error the example is meant to expose. What it shows is that the choice of counterfactual can matter more than the choice of creator.

## What does the disclosure rule change about measurement?

Section 255.5 requires a clear and conspicuous disclosure of an unexpected material connection, including payment, free products and early access. Section 255.1 requires endorsements to reflect honest experience and obliges advertisers to guide and monitor endorsers. Disclosure makes an interested voice legible; it does not make the post independent evidence.

Section 255.5 describes an influencer given an expensive lathe who should disclose receiving it free, with the advertiser expected to advise and monitor. Section 255.1 describes an influencer claiming a lotion cures eczema beyond personal experience. These are rules about presentation and substantiation, not performance results.

For measurement the effect is a classification. A disclosed sponsored post belongs in the paid column of the ledger; a person who bought and posted without an incentive is a different observation. Mixing the two into one "creator mentions" total hides the incentive structure you would need to interpret the numbers. The measurement designs appropriate to each are discussed in [Influencer Marketing Measurement](/articles/influencer-marketing-measurement).

## What can be measured without a holdout?

Deliverables, timing, credited orders, code redemptions, citation presence and referral sessions can all be logged with dates and denominators. Each is a property of the observed trail. Without a comparison group, label them as attributed activity and leave the lift column empty.

The RAG agent's `number_audit` ran 193 checks showing its memos matched retrieved files — consistency, not validity. The 22 November 2026 curation reduced 1,004 templated articles to a curated set behind an allowlist, a structural decision with no traffic outcome measured. Both have checkable records and no reader-response claim.

A partnership report can match that discipline. Log what each post delivered, when it ran, which events were credited under which rule, and where the source was a citation trace rather than a click. A citation that appears on one check is a weak observation; the stability question is treated in [Why a Single Citation Check Misleads](/articles/why-a-single-citation-check-misleads). The goal is a record another person can audit, not a number that settles the argument.

## What belongs in the final report?

Report the credited event and its rule, the observable signals with dates and denominators, the counterfactual design if one exists, and a separate line for outcomes that were not measured. That format lets a reader see where credit ends and where a causal claim would begin.

The practical question is whether the same decision would survive a different credit rule. If it would not, the budget case rests on the accounting choice, and the next step is a comparison rather than a new dashboard. If it would, the credited event is at least a stable basis for operational tracking, and a holdout can be reserved for the spend large enough to justify it.

A partnership can be worth running for reasons a credited order does not capture, including the description of a product in answers a brand cannot buy directly. Those reasons should be written as hypotheses with their own evidence, not smuggled into a return figure. The narrow, defensible claim is the one the ledger supports: what was delivered, what was credited, and what remains unknown.
