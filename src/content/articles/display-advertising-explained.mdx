Display advertising is often measured by impressions, which describes delivery rather than effect. The display-advertising entry describes graphic ads on digital channels, and Google's measurement documentation covers conversion credit. The question is what an impression tells you.

**Direct answer:** Treat an impression as evidence of delivery, then measure the outcome the campaign exists for with a comparison where possible. The sources describe the channel; the rule is a synthesis.

**Delta:** This article adds a claim-status table and a worked example, separating documented delivery from local judgements about effect.

## The mechanism

The first point is delivery. The display-advertising entry describes ads placed on digital properties, which means an impression records that an ad could be seen. It does not record that a person noticed it, understood it, or changed a decision.

The second point is credit. Google's measurement documentation describes how conversions are attributed, which depends on rules. A conversion after a display ad may have been caused by something else, and the credit rule does not settle the question.

The third point is the outcome. A campaign for awareness and a campaign for direct response need different measures, and a single report cannot serve both. Naming the outcome before launch is the decision that makes measurement possible.

The sequence is: define the outcome, verify delivery, check the credit rule, then use a comparison for effect. The choices are local, so the conclusions are `inferred`; the channel is `source-reported`.

## A worked example

*Illustrative.* A campaign delivers a million impressions and reports a strong click-through rate.

Three checks decide. First, did the campaign reach the intended audience, or only a large one? Second, does the reported conversion represent the outcome the campaign exists for? Third, is there a holdout or another comparison that separates the campaign from background demand?

The outcome is a qualified reading of delivery and effect. The scenario is hypothetical; the method is the contribution.

## Why an impression is not an outcome

An impression records that an ad was served. The business cares whether a person noticed it and acted, which is a different observation, and the gap is where reporting becomes misleading.

A further consideration is viewability. An ad can be technically delivered in a position where nobody could have seen it, and a report that counts impressions without context overstates reach. Checking the conditions of delivery is part of evaluating the channel.

The campaign also has a frequency dimension. The same person may see an ad many times, and a count of impressions can look large while the reached audience is small. Reporting reach and frequency together is the correction.

The third limitation is that credit rules favour some channels. A platform that reports conversions within its own system will attribute more to itself, which is a reason to use a comparison rather than relying on a single report. The discipline is the same as for any measured channel.

## A decision test

1. Define the outcome.
2. Verify reach and frequency.
3. Check viewability conditions.
4. Read the credit rule.
5. Use a comparison for effect.
6. Record the result and recheck date.

## A counterargument

A reasonable critic can push back that the article's central prescription - use a comparison to separate effect from delivery - is a synthesis its sources do not supply. The display-advertising entry describes the channel, and Google's attribution documentation explains credit rules inside a platform; neither establishes that a holdout is available or necessary. For awareness campaigns, where effects accrue slowly and a short holdout may show nothing, the insistence on a comparison quietly privileges direct-response measurement. The objection is not that impressions are being conflated with outcomes; it is that the article offers one instrument, the comparison, without the conditions in which it works. Where a holdout is infeasible or too noisy, the framework leaves the practitioner with delivery data and a warning, which is less than the decision rule it promises.
