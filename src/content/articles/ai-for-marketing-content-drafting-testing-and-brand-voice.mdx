<Lead>
Marketing teams adopted AI drafting faster than most functions, and the results are mixed. The tool is good at producing volume and variation, which is useful when a campaign needs many versions of a message. It is weak at knowing what the brand should sound like, because <Mark>voice is a set of decisions a model can only imitate from examples</Mark>.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Use AI to draft first versions, generate variants for testing, and check consistency against a defined voice guide — with a person editing and a measured test deciding what ships. Anthropic's use-case guides cover content patterns, and brand-management principles treat voice as part of a maintained identity.

<KeyPoint>
The boundary: a model can mimic a voice, but it cannot decide what the brand stands for, which the value-proposition article explains as a strategic choice.
</KeyPoint>

## Where it helps

**First drafts.** Produce a starting version quickly so a writer edits rather than faces a blank page. <Mark>The draft is a proposal, not final copy</Mark>, which the human-in-the-loop design recommends.

**Variants for testing.** Generate several versions of a headline or message for an A/B test. The value is volume, and the test decides which wins, as the A/B testing article explains — not the model's confidence.

**Repurposing.** Adapt one piece across channels while keeping the underlying claim consistent, as the content-repurposing article describes.

**Consistency checks.** Compare a draft against a voice guide and flag deviations, which a structured rubric makes measurable, as the evaluation-rubric article argues.

## Where it fails

**Invented specifics.** A model can add a plausible number or claim that is not true, which the reduce-hallucinations article treats as a core failure to guard against.

**Voice drift.**

<Trap>
Without explicit rules, generated copy averages toward generic marketing language, which dilutes the identity the brand-guidelines article describes.
</Trap>

**Causal claims.** A draft that says a feature "caused" a result the data only correlates with is a problem the correlation-vs-causation article covers.

## A worked workflow

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A team drafts a product announcement. The workflow:

1. Provide the model with the brief, the value proposition, and three examples of approved copy.
2. Generate five opening variants with explicit constraints: <Mark>no unverified numbers, no causal claims</Mark>, a stated length range.
3. Screen each against a voice rubric — four dimensions scored 0–2, as the evaluation-rubric article recommends.
4. Have a writer edit the top two, then test them in a controlled send.

Three checks decide whether it works:

1. **Fact audit** — does every claim trace to the brief? A generated number without a source is rejected, as the reduce-hallucinations article explains.
2. **Voice consistency** — does the copy match the rubric better than a human-only baseline? If not, the examples or rules need work, which the brand-guidelines article covers.
3. **Test outcome** — does the winner of the controlled send actually perform better, as the subject-line article argues for downstream metrics rather than opens.

Three conclusions follow:

- **The model generates options; people and tests decide.**
- **Voice must be specified**, not assumed, which the brand-voice article explains.
- **Fact-checking is a separate step** that does not disappear because the copy reads well.

## Giving the model a voice to imitate

Voice imitation depends on what you show the model. Three inputs matter:

**Approved examples, with context.** Provide copy that was actually published and explain why it was approved, not just the text. The brand-voice article argues that guidelines need concrete examples, which is equally true for AI prompts.

**Explicit anti-patterns.** State what the brand does not do — no superlatives, no invented urgency — as the brand-guidelines article recommends. A model can only avoid what it has been told to avoid.

**The value proposition, not adjectives.** Give the model the specific promise and audience from the value-proposition article. "Innovative solutions" gives it nothing to say; a concrete outcome gives it material to work with.

These inputs are a one-time investment that improves every draft. A team that skips them will spend more time editing generic copy than it saved generating it.

## What AI content tools cannot do

**They cannot define the brand.** Positioning is a strategic decision, as the brand-positioning article argues.

**They cannot guarantee performance.** No structure guarantees conversion, which the CRO article makes explicit.

**They cannot replace editorial judgement.** A piece can be fluent, on-voice, and still say nothing useful, which the content-marketing article treats as the central quality question.

The counterargument — that readers cannot tell AI-generated copy — misses the point.

<PullQuote>
The risk is not detection; it is publishing a plausible claim that the brand cannot support, as the content-detection article explains for the limits of provenance tools.
</PullQuote>

## A decision test

1. **Brief with a sourced value proposition**, not adjectives.
2. **Provide approved examples** of the voice.
3. **Constrain generation** — no unverified numbers or causal claims.
4. **Score against a voice rubric** before human editing.
5. **Fact-check every claim** against the brief.
6. **Test variants** with a controlled send and a downstream metric.
7. **Keep editorial accountability** with a person, as the governance checklist requires.
