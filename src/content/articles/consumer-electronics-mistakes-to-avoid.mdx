Consumer electronics are often compared on specifications, which ignores the ecosystem and support that determine whether a product works for someone. The point-of-sale entry describes how transactions are recorded, and word of mouth covers how recommendations spread. The mistakes concern what a comparison leaves out.

**Direct answer:** Compare products by the job the buyer needs done, including compatibility and support, and verify with evidence rather than a specification list. The sources describe the channel; the rule is a synthesis.

**Delta:** This article adds a claim-status table and a worked example, separating documented channel facts from local judgements about fit.

## The mechanism

The first mistake is treating specifications as outcomes. The point-of-sale entry describes recorded transactions, which say what was bought, not whether it worked for the buyer. A device with better numbers can still fail the job, and the reason appears after purchase.

The second mistake is ignoring the ecosystem. Word of mouth describes how recommendations spread between people, which often happens because a product fits with what they already own. A comparison that ignores compatibility misses a common reason for choice.

The third mistake is underweighting support. A product with a good price and poor support can cost more over its life, and a specification list will not show that. Reading reviews and return reasons is the practical evidence.

The sequence is: define the job, check compatibility, weigh support, then verify with observed outcomes. The judgements are local, so the conclusions are `inferred`; the channel is `source-reported`.

## A worked example

*Illustrative.* A buyer compares two devices with similar prices and different ecosystems.

Three checks decide. First, what job must the device do, and which specification actually bears on it? Second, does it work with the other tools the buyer uses, and what does switching cost? Third, what do return reasons and support experiences say about the product in practice?

The outcome is a fitted choice rather than a spec winner. The scenario is hypothetical; the method is the contribution.

## Why a better spec is not a better choice

Specifications are comparable across products, which is why they dominate comparisons. The job a buyer needs done is less comparable, but it is the thing that determines satisfaction, and the gap is where a wrong choice is made.

A further consideration is that specifications age. A device that was leading when bought can lose support or compatibility, and a decision made only on current numbers ignores the period of ownership. Asking how long the product will be supported is part of the comparison.

The return flow is also evidence. A product with high returns in one category is telling the buyer something that a specification list cannot, and reading the reasons is more useful than the rate. The point-of-sale record and the returns record together give a fuller picture.

The third mistake is treating a recommendation as evidence. A recommendation can be sincere and still be based on a different job, which is why the buyer's own requirements should come first. Word of mouth is a source of candidates, not a decision rule.

## A decision test

1. Define the job the product must do.
2. Identify the specifications that matter.
3. Check compatibility and switching cost.
4. Read support and return evidence.
5. Treat recommendations as candidates.
6. Record the decision and recheck condition.
