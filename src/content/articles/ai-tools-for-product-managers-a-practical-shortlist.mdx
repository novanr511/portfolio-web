<Lead>
An AI tool list that names products ages quickly and answers the wrong question. The useful question is which part of product work has a shape that AI can help with — synthesis, drafting, analysis, or exploration — and what evidence would show that the tool improved it.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Product work breaks into tasks that AI assists differently: synthesising feedback, drafting specifications, exploring alternatives, analysing product data, and preparing communications. Anthropic's use-case guides cover concrete patterns, and O*NET's occupation descriptions help define what the role actually does. The boundary: a tool is <Mark>a capability, not a workflow</Mark> — value comes from embedding it in a decision process with a baseline and a review step.

## Five task areas

**Feedback synthesis.** Grouping customer feedback by theme, extracting verbatim quotes, and counting mentions. The extraction-and-judgement pattern keeps the evidence separate from the interpretation.

**Specification drafting.** Producing a first version of a requirements document or user story from notes. A person still decides what the product should do.

**Alternative exploration.** Generating options for a design decision with their trade-offs. The value is breadth, not a recommendation — the PM owns the choice.

**Product data analysis.** Natural-language questions over a defined dataset, such as activation by cohort. The analysis must be grounded in the actual numbers, as the reporting-automation article explains.

**Communication.** Drafting updates, release notes, and stakeholder summaries. Tone and accuracy both need review.

## A worked example of tool evaluation

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A PM wants to use an AI tool for feedback synthesis. The team currently spends six hours a month reading support tickets and writing a themes summary.

**Step 1 — Baseline.** Record the current time, the number of themes identified, and a sample of summaries for comparison.

**Step 2 — Trial.** Run one month of tickets through the tool with a fixed prompt: extract distinct complaints verbatim, group by underlying cause, count each group, and cite example tickets.

**Step 3 — Check.** Have a human review 50 tickets and compare their themes with the tool's. If the tool misses a theme that appears in more than 5% of tickets, the prompt or the input coverage needs work.

**Step 4 — Decide.** If the tool reduces time to two hours a month and the theme agreement is above the team's threshold, adopt it. Otherwise, keep the manual process or change the approach.

Three lessons follow:

- **The tool is judged on a task metric**, not on how impressive its demo looked.
- **Theme agreement is measurable**, which makes the decision defensible.
- **Six hours saved is the entire benefit** unless the synthesis changes a decision — which is a separate question the trial should also ask.

## Building the habit

<KeyPoint>
A tool becomes useful when it is attached to a recurring decision rather than tried once.
</KeyPoint>

Three habits support that:

**Keep a prompt library with outcomes.** Save the prompts that worked, along with the task and the measured result.

<Trap>
A prompt without its outcome is hard to improve, and PMs often redo work that a colleague already refined.
</Trap>

**Review failures, not just successes.** When a generated summary misstates the data, record why. The failure category — <Mark>wrong source, ambiguous instruction, unsupported cause</Mark> — tells you whether to change the prompt, the data, or the review step.

**Share the standard, not the tool.** A team benefits more from an agreed threshold for accepting AI-assisted work than from everyone using the same product. The threshold is a decision about quality, which is the PM's responsibility.

These habits turn a shortlist into a practice.

<PullQuote>
Without them, tool adoption is a series of demos that never quite become part of how the product is built.
</PullQuote>

## What the shortlist should not contain

**A ranking of vendors by popularity.** Popularity reflects marketing and distribution, not fit for your task.

**Claims about productivity gains without a method.** A vendor's case study describes their customers' conditions. Treat it as a claim, not evidence.

**Tools that require a data foundation you do not have.** An analytics assistant cannot answer questions about data that is not recorded consistently. The data-strategy article covers the prerequisite.

The counterargument — that PMs should experiment freely with tools — is reasonable. The response is to make the experiment cheap: <Mark>one task, one month, one baseline</Mark>, as in the worked example.

## A decision test

1. **Name the task** before looking at tools.
2. **Record a baseline** — time, quality, and current output.
3. **Run a bounded trial** on one month of real work.
4. **Check the output against human judgement** on a sample.
5. **Decide on a task metric**, not a demo.
6. **Verify the data foundation** the tool depends on.
7. **Review the decision after a model update**, since tool behaviour changes.
