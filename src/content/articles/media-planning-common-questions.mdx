## The mechanism

Three points matter:

<Figure src="/images/articles/inline/media-planning-common-questions-1.png" alt="A five-step flow: define the audience, name the outcome, choose channels with a reason, reserve a comparison, then measure against the outcome." caption="Conceptual: a media plan is built from audience, outcome and measurement before channel spend." />

- **Audience before channel.** The media-planning entry describes selecting channels. A channel with broad reach may not contain the people the plan needs.
- **Outcome before metric.** The demand-generation entry describes stages. Awareness and conversion require different channels and different measures.
- **Measurement before spend.** A plan without a comparison cannot tell whether the campaign worked. The causal-inference distinction applies here.

<KeyPoint>
The sequence is: define the audience, name the outcome, choose channels, then design the measurement.
</KeyPoint>

The choices are local judgement, so they are `inferred`; the channel description is `source-reported`.

## A worked plan

*Illustrative.* A product launch wants qualified visits, not just impressions.

Three checks decide. First, which channels contain the intended audience? Second, what outcome defines success, and how will it be separated from background demand? Third, what share of the budget is reserved for a comparison rather than spent on reach?

The outcome is an allocation with a measurement rule. The scenario is hypothetical; the method is the contribution.

## Why the audience definition drives the plan

<Trap>
A plan that begins with channels will buy whatever is cheapest per impression, which is often an audience that does not contain buyers.
</Trap>

Defining the audience first reverses that logic. It also makes the channel choice explainable: a channel is selected because it reaches the people who matter, not because it has a large number attached to it.

## What comparison means in a plan

A plan that spends its entire budget on exposure has no way to tell whether the exposure changed anything. Reserving a portion for a comparison — <Mark>a holdout, a different period, or a matched group</Mark> — is the cost of learning. It reduces reach in the short term and improves the next plan, which is the trade the measurement budget buys.

## Why reach is not effect

Reach describes how many people were exposed. Effect describes <Mark>what changed because of the exposure</Mark>. A plan that reports only reach cannot answer the second question, which is the one the budget was meant to buy.

A final consideration is that channel performance is not stable. A channel that works at a small budget can saturate as spend grows, and the marginal return falls. A plan based on early results can therefore overcommit to a channel that looked excellent only at low volume. Testing at the intended scale, or at least modelling saturation, is part of a defensible allocation.

The plan also needs a review condition. Audience behaviour, platform rules, and prices change, and a plan that is never revisited becomes a habit. Recording when the allocation was decided and what would trigger a review keeps the plan connected to its purpose.

Another constraint is that attribution windows differ across platforms, which makes their reports hard to compare. A sale credited within a day on one platform and within a week on another will produce different totals for the same campaign. Aligning windows before comparing is a small technical step that prevents a large interpretive error.

The plan should also name who owns its revision. A document with no owner will be reused after its assumptions have expired, which is how a sound plan becomes an obsolete one. Assigning responsibility is part of planning, not an afterthought once the budget is spent.

A plan that is never revised is a record of an old decision rather than a guide to current spending.

## What it cannot do

It cannot guarantee that a channel will perform. It does not remove the need for creative quality. And it cannot prove causation from a credit rule.

<PullQuote>
The counterargument that maximum reach is safest is fair for broad awareness and incomplete for a specific outcome.
</PullQuote>

## A decision test

1. Define the audience.
2. Name the outcome.
3. Choose channels with a reason.
4. Reserve budget for comparison.
5. Measure against the outcome.
6. Record the plan and recheck date.

For the measurement, see [Campaign Measurement](/articles/campaign-measurement-at-scale).
