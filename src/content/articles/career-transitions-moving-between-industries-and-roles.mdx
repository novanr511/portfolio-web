Changing careers is not starting over. It is translating what you know into a new context.

**Direct answer:** A career transition is a move to a different industry, role, or function. It succeeds when you translate existing experience into relevant value, build bridges through networks and learning, and manage the transition as a project—not a leap of faith. Transitions are common and viable at any stage, but they require preparation, patience, and realistic expectations.

## Why transitions happen

People transition for many reasons:

- **Industry decline.** Their field is shrinking or automating.
- **Interest change.** They want different problems, people, or impact.
- **Life circumstances.** Location, family, health, or values shift.
- **Opportunity.** A compelling role or network connection appears.
- **Burnout.** The current role no longer fits.

None of these require starting from zero. Experience transfers, even when the context changes.

## What transfers

Experience transfers in three forms:

- **Skills.** Analysis, communication, project management, writing, coding, selling—these apply across industries.
- **Knowledge.** Domain expertise (finance, healthcare, education, logistics) provides context even in a new role.
- **Network.** Relationships built over years provide introductions, advice, and references.

The challenge is not lacking experience. It is framing experience so the new audience sees its relevance.

## How to translate experience

1. **Identify the target.** What role, industry, or function? What problems do people in that role solve?
2. **Map your experience.** What have you done that solves similar problems, even in a different context?
3. **Reframe the language.** Use the target industry's terminology. "Managed a retail store" becomes "led operations, P&L, and team of 15."
4. **Show outcomes.** Numbers, outcomes, and impact matter more than job titles.
5. **Fill the gap.** Learn the specific tools, regulations, or concepts the target role requires.

## Building bridges

Transitions are easier with connections:

- **Informational interviews.** Talk to people in the target role. Learn what they do, what they value, and how they got there.
- **Side projects.** Do the work before you have the title. Build, write, or volunteer in the target domain.
- **Referrals.** A recommendation from someone inside the target industry carries more weight than a cold application.
- **Education.** Courses, certifications, or degrees signal commitment and provide structure.

## Managing expectations

Transitions take time. You may start at a lower level or salary than your previous role. You may need to prove yourself again. This is not failure; it is the cost of entry into a new domain.

Financial preparation matters. Savings reduce pressure and allow time for the transition to work.

## The narrative matters

How you tell your story determines how others perceive it. A transition framed as "running away from a bad job" raises concerns. A transition framed as "moving toward a specific goal with relevant experience" builds confidence.

Your narrative should answer: why this, why now, and why you?
