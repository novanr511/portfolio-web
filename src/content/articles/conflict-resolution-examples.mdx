A conflict that looks like a personality clash is often a disagreement about interests that nobody has stated. Two teams want the same engineer, and the argument is about priority; behind the positions are different obligations with different deadlines. **Direct answer:** Resolve conflict by separating positions from interests, defining the decision, and testing options against shared criteria rather than splitting a position. ## The method in the examples

**Separate position from interest.** "I need the engineer full-time" is a position; "my release depends on their work" is an interest, which the negotiation article treats as the distinction that opens options, as the decision-memo article requires a specific question.

**Name the decision and the owner.** A conflict that reopens weekly is often an unclear decision, which the decision-memo article treats as a failure to record the owner, as the small-team decision article advises. One person should be accountable, as the governance checklist requires.

**Use a shared criterion.** Options should be judged against a criterion both sides accept — customer impact, deadline, risk — as the evaluation-rubric article requires for concrete criteria, as the prioritisation article advises for ranking.

**Record the outcome.** The agreement and its reason should be written where the team can find it, which the documentation article advises, as the internal-communications article treats decisions others must act on, as the decision-memo article requires.

A fair process needs a real criterion, as the rubric article requires, as the fairness discussion in the responsible-AI article treats as a design property. And the outcome should be reviewed after a material change, as the monitoring article advises.

## Worked examples

The following are hypothetical and illustrative.

**Two teams, one engineer.** Team A says the engineer must join its release; Team B says the same. A split would halve both efforts. ## A decision test

1. State each side's position and the interest behind it, as the negotiation article advises.
2. Name the decision and one accountable owner, as the decision-memo article requires.
3. Choose a criterion both sides accept, as the rubric article requires.
4. Generate options before judging them, as the negotiation article recommends.
5. Check whether the conflict is actually a formal HR or legal matter, as those articles require.
6. Record the outcome and its reason, as the documentation article advises.
7. Review after a material change, as the monitoring article recommends.
