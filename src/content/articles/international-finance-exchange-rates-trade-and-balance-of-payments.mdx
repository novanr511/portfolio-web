Money does not stop at borders. Neither do financial risks.

**Direct answer:** International finance studies how money, capital, and financial assets move between countries. Exchange rates determine the price of one currency in terms of another; trade balances record exports and imports; the balance of payments tracks all cross-border transactions. These forces affect exporters, importers, investors, and multinational companies through currency risk, financing costs, and competitive positioning.

## Exchange rates: the price of money

An exchange rate is how much one currency buys of another. Rates move based on:

- **Interest rate differentials:** higher rates attract foreign capital, strengthening the currency.
- **Inflation differentials:** higher inflation erodes currency value over time.
- **Trade balances:** strong export demand increases demand for the currency.
- **Capital flows:** foreign investment inflows strengthen the currency; outflows weaken it.
- **Speculation and expectations:** traders bet on future rate movements.

## Fixed versus floating

- **Floating rates:** determined by market supply and demand (most major currencies).
- **Fixed or pegged rates:** the government or central bank maintains a set value, usually against another currency, using reserves and intervention.

Fixed rates provide stability but require large reserves and can break under pressure (currency crises).

## Trade balance: exports minus imports

The trade balance is the difference between a country's exports and imports of goods and services.

- **Trade surplus:** exports exceed imports. Foreign buyers need the domestic currency, supporting its value.
- **Trade deficit:** imports exceed exports. The country finances the gap through borrowing or selling assets to foreigners.

A deficit is not inherently bad; it can reflect strong domestic demand and investment. A surplus is not inherently good; it can reflect weak domestic consumption.

## Balance of payments: the full record

The balance of payments records all economic transactions between residents and the rest of the world:

- **Current account:** trade in goods and services, primary income (investment income), and secondary income (transfers).
- **Capital account:** capital transfers and acquisition/disposal of non-produced assets.
- **Financial account:** investment flows (direct investment, portfolio investment, other investment, and reserve assets).

The current account and financial account must balance overall: a current account deficit is financed by a financial account surplus (foreign investment inflows).

## Implications for business

- **Exporters:** a weaker domestic currency makes their goods cheaper abroad, boosting competitiveness.
- **Importers:** a stronger domestic currency makes foreign inputs cheaper.
- **Multinationals:** earnings in foreign currencies must be translated, creating translation risk.
- **Borrowers:** foreign-currency debt becomes more expensive when the domestic currency weakens.

## Hedging currency risk

Businesses can manage exchange-rate risk through:

- **Forward contracts:** locking in an exchange rate for a future date.
- **Options:** the right, but not obligation, to exchange at a set rate.
- **Natural hedging:** matching currency revenues with currency costs (e.g., producing where you sell).
