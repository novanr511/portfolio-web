Inventory is money you cannot spend until it sells.

**Direct answer:** Inventory management is the practice of ordering, storing, and using stock so that customer demand is met without tying up excessive capital. It balances the cost of holding inventory against the cost of running out. Good inventory management improves cash flow, reduces waste, and increases customer satisfaction. Poor inventory management leads to stockouts, dead stock, and liquidity problems.

## What inventory represents

Inventory is an asset on the balance sheet, but it behaves like frozen cash. Money spent on inventory cannot be used for payroll, marketing, or growth until the inventory is sold and collected. The more inventory a business holds, the more capital is locked up—and the more risk it carries (damage, obsolescence, theft).

## The core trade-off

Inventory management balances two opposing costs:

- **Holding cost:** storage, insurance, handling, spoilage, obsolescence, and the opportunity cost of capital. More inventory means higher holding cost.
- **Stockout cost:** lost sales, dissatisfied customers, emergency orders, and damaged reputation. Too little inventory means lost revenue.

The optimal inventory level minimizes the total of these two costs, not either one individually.

## Key metrics

- **Inventory turnover:** how many times inventory is sold and replaced per period. Higher turnover generally means efficient management.
- **Days of inventory:** how many days of sales are represented by current inventory.
- **Stockout rate:** how often demanded items are unavailable.
- **Carrying cost:** the annual cost of holding inventory as a percentage of inventory value.
- **Fill rate:** the percentage of customer demand met from stock on hand.

## Common inventory models

**Just-in-time (JIT).** Order inventory to arrive just as it is needed. Minimizes holding cost but requires reliable suppliers and predictable demand. Vulnerable to supply chain disruptions.

**Just-in-case.** Hold extra inventory as buffer. Increases holding cost but protects against stockouts and supply uncertainty.

**Economic Order Quantity (EOQ).** A formula that calculates the optimal order size to minimize ordering and holding costs. Useful for stable, predictable demand.

**ABC analysis.** Classify inventory by value: A items (high value, low volume), B items (moderate), C items (low value, high volume). Manage A items tightly, C items loosely.

## Demand forecasting

Inventory decisions depend on demand forecasts. Methods include:

- **Historical averages:** past sales predict future sales.
- **Seasonal adjustments:** account for holidays, weather, and cycles.
- **Market intelligence:** promotions, competitor actions, economic trends.
- **Machine learning:** models that incorporate multiple demand signals.

Forecasts are always wrong to some degree. Inventory policy should account for forecast error, not assume perfect prediction.

## The supply chain connection

Inventory is not just a warehouse problem. It is connected to suppliers, lead times, shipping, and production. Long lead times require more safety stock. Unreliable suppliers require more buffer. Local suppliers reduce lead time but may cost more.

For retailers, inventory placement matters: stock must be where customers are, not just where it is cheapest to store.

## Cash flow implications

Inventory purchases are cash outflows before they are inflows. A business can be profitable on paper but insolvent because cash is tied up in unsold inventory. Inventory management is therefore a cash flow decision, not just an operations decision.
