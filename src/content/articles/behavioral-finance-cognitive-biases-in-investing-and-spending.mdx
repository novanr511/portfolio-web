Money decisions are not made by spreadsheets. They are made by people.

**Direct answer:** Behavioral finance combines psychology with economics to explain why investors and consumers make systematic errors. Cognitive biases—such as overconfidence, loss aversion, anchoring, and herd behavior—distort financial decisions, leading to overtrading, under-saving, and poor investment outcomes. Recognizing these biases is the first step toward better financial decisions.

## Common biases

**Overconfidence.** People overestimate their knowledge, predictive ability, and control. Overconfident investors trade too frequently, underdiversify, and take excessive risk.

**Loss aversion.** Losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good. Investors hold losing investments too long (to avoid realizing a loss) and sell winners too early (to lock in gains).

**Anchoring.** People rely too heavily on the first piece of information they see. An investor anchored to a stock's purchase price may refuse to sell below it, regardless of new information.

**Herd behavior.** People follow the crowd, especially under uncertainty. Bubbles and panics are amplified when everyone buys at the top or sells at the bottom.

**Confirmation bias.** People seek information that confirms existing beliefs and ignore contradictory evidence. Investors may hold a failing stock because they only read bullish analysis.

**Mental accounting.** People treat money differently based on its source or intended use (e.g., treating a tax refund as "free money" while carrying credit-card debt).

## How biases distort decisions

- **Overtrading:** frequent buying and selling increases transaction costs and taxes, reducing net returns.
- **Under-diversification:** overconfidence leads to concentrated portfolios in familiar stocks or sectors.
- **Panic selling:** loss aversion and herd behavior amplify market downturns.
- **Under-saving:** present bias (preferring immediate gratification) undermines retirement and emergency savings.
- **Impulse purchases:** anchoring on a "sale" price leads to buying unneeded items.

## Mitigating bias

1. **Rules-based investing.** Set predefined criteria for buying, selling, and rebalancing. Remove emotion from individual decisions.
2. **Automation.** Automate savings and investments to reduce present bias and decision fatigue.
3. **Pre-commitment.** Write an investment policy statement that defines your strategy before markets get volatile.
4. **Seek disconfirming evidence.** Actively look for arguments against your favorite investment.
5. **Slow down.** Major financial decisions benefit from a cooling-off period.

## Limits of behavioral finance

Behavioral finance explains patterns, not individual outcomes. Not every investor exhibits every bias, and bias does not guarantee failure. Use behavioral insights to improve process, not to time the market.
