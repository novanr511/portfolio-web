**Direct answer:**

<KeyPoint>
A B2B landing page should state the problem and the outcome, show evidence a buyer can verify, address the objections that block a decision, and offer a next step proportionate to intent.
</KeyPoint>

Marketing-communications principles govern consistency across channels, while demand generation explains the stage the page serves. The boundary: a page is one touchpoint in a long decision; it cannot be judged on immediate conversions alone.

## Four parts of the page

**Problem and outcome.** The first screen should tell a reader with the relevant problem what changes if they choose you. A vague headline about innovation gives them nothing to evaluate.

**Verifiable evidence.** Specific numbers with their context — a measured result, a named customer with permission, a documented method. A claim without a denominator invites scepticism from a buyer who will be asked to defend the choice.

**Objection handling.** B2B buyers worry about implementation effort, security, integration, and cost. A page that answers these questions reduces the work a champion must do internally. A comparison table or a short FAQ can carry this.

**A next step matched to intent.** "Book a demo" is appropriate for a late-stage visitor; "read the evaluation guide" suits an early-stage one.

<Trap>
Offering only the highest-intent action loses readers who are not ready.
</Trap>

## A worked example of a page audit

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A vendor sells compliance software. Its landing page gets 5,000 visitors a month, 100 demo requests, and 20 qualified opportunities.

An audit asks three questions:

1. **Is the outcome specific?** The headline currently says "Simplify compliance". A buyer cannot tell what improves. Rewriting it to state the measurable result — for example, the time to prepare an audit — would make the page easier to evaluate. The number must come from a real customer with permission, not an estimate.
2. **Are the objections answered?** The page has no content on data residency or integration. If those are the reasons deals stall, the page should address them, or link to a page that does. The information a champion needs internally is part of the product's marketing surface.
3. **Is the next step matched?** Every visitor sees "Book a demo". Adding a downloadable evaluation checklist gives early-stage readers somewhere to go. <Mark>Track which path produces qualified opportunities, not just which produces more form fills</Mark>.

Three decisions follow:

- **Rewrite the headline around an outcome** with evidence.
- **Move the top two objections onto the page** or into a clearly linked resource.
- **Add a lower-intent path** and measure opportunity quality by path.

Three cautions apply: the new headline needs a real number, the objection content must be accurate, and a test must change one element at a time, as the A/B testing article explains.

## What a page cannot do

**It cannot replace a sales process.** A complex purchase involves conversations, security review, and procurement. The page supports that process by giving the champion material to circulate.

**It cannot compensate for a weak product.** A page that overpromises produces more demos and fewer closes, which is worse than fewer qualified visitors. Read the conversion metric alongside opportunity quality.

**It cannot be all things to all segments.** <Mark>A page aimed at every industry usually speaks to none.</Mark> Separate pages with distinct evidence are better than one broad page with generic claims.

The counterargument — that a landing page should be short — is true when the reader's decision is simple.

<PullQuote>
For a B2B purchase with a committee, length is not the problem; irrelevance is.
</PullQuote>

A long page that answers real objections outperforms a short one that does not.

## A decision test

1. **Name the buying committee** and the reader's role.
2. **State the outcome** in the first screen, with evidence.
3. **Answer the top two objections** with verifiable information.
4. **Offer a next step** matched to intent — demo and evaluation content.
5. **Keep evidence consistent** across the ad, the page, and the follow-up.
6. **Measure qualified opportunities by path**, not form fills.
7. **Test one element at a time** with a defined primary metric.
