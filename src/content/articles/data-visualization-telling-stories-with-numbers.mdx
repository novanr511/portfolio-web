Numbers do not speak for themselves. They need to be shown.

**Direct answer:** Data visualization is the practice of representing data graphically to communicate insight, not just to display numbers. The right chart makes patterns obvious; the wrong chart hides or distorts them. Effective visualization starts with the question, chooses the appropriate chart type, avoids deception, and leads the audience to a conclusion.

## Why visualization matters

Raw data in tables is hard to interpret at scale. A chart can show a trend, an outlier, or a relationship in seconds. Visualization is how data becomes decision-relevant. But it is also how data can mislead: the same numbers can look like growth or decline depending on the axis, the color, or the chart type.

## Choosing the right chart

| Chart type | Best for | Avoid when |
|---|---|---|
| Bar chart | Comparing categories | Showing trends over many time points |
| Line chart | Trends over time | Comparing many unrelated categories |
| Scatter plot | Relationships between two variables | Categorical data with no order |
| Pie chart | Parts of a whole (few categories) | More than 3–4 categories; precise comparison |
| Histogram | Distribution of a single variable | Comparing two variables |
| Heatmap | Patterns in a matrix | Exact value lookup |

The rule: choose the chart that answers the question, not the chart that looks impressive.

## Common deceptions

- **Truncated y-axis.** Starting the axis at 90 instead of 0 makes small differences look massive.
- **Dual axes.** Two y-axes can imply correlation where none exists.
- **3D effects.** Depth distorts perception and adds no information.
- **Cherry-picked time ranges.** Showing only a period that supports the narrative.
- **Unlabeled axes.** Without units and scale, the chart is meaningless.

Good visualization discloses its choices: axis starts, sample sizes, definitions, and limitations.

## Design for the audience

Executives need the conclusion first, with evidence behind it. Analysts need detail and the ability to drill down. The same data may need different visualizations for different audiences. The goal is clarity, not decoration.

## Tell a story

A chart should answer: what changed, why it matters, and what to do. A line chart showing rising support tickets is not insight until paired with context: a new release, a seasonal spike, or a broken feature. The visualization starts the conversation; the narrative finishes it.
