A great product with no launch plan is a product nobody buys.

**Direct answer:** Go-to-market (GTM) strategy is the plan for delivering a product to the right customers, through the right channels, at the right price, at the right time. It answers who the customer is, what problem the product solves, how to reach them, and how to win against alternatives. GTM is not a marketing campaign; it is a cross-functional decision that shapes product, pricing, distribution, and sales.

## What GTM covers

A GTM strategy answers five questions:

1. **Who is the customer?** Define the target segment precisely: role, company size, industry, context, and pain point.
2. **What is the value proposition?** Why should this customer buy this product now, from you?
3. **How do you reach them?** Which channels—sales, marketing, partnerships, product-led—will acquire customers profitably?
4. **What is the price?** How does pricing reflect value, position against alternatives, and support the business model?
5. **How do you win?** What is the differentiated advantage that makes customers choose you over alternatives?

## Product-market fit comes first

GTM does not create demand for a product nobody wants. Before launching, validate that customers have the problem and will pay for the solution. A GTM strategy amplifies product-market fit; it does not create it.

Launching before product-market fit is like pouring water into a leaky bucket: you can keep filling it, but you will never keep it full.

## Choosing the right channel

GTM channels include:

- **Sales-led:** a sales team finds, qualifies, and closes customers. Suitable for high-ACV, complex B2B products.
- **Marketing-led:** content, ads, and events generate inbound demand. Suitable for products with clear search or social intent.
- **Product-led:** the product itself drives adoption (free trial, freemium). Suitable for low-friction, self-serve products.
- **Channel-led:** partners, resellers, or marketplaces sell on your behalf. Suitable when you lack direct access to customers.

The channel depends on the product, the customer, and the economics. A $50/month tool cannot support a field sales team. A $100,000 enterprise contract cannot be sold through a self-serve checkout.

## Pricing and positioning in GTM

Pricing and positioning are GTM decisions, not afterthoughts. They determine who buys, how much revenue per customer, and how you compete. A premium product with premium pricing needs premium positioning and sales. A low-cost product needs high volume and efficient acquisition.

## The launch sequence

A launch is not a single day. It is a sequence:

- **Pre-launch:** build the product, define the segment, prepare materials, align the team.
- **Launch:** make the product available, announce to the target segment, begin acquisition.
- **Post-launch:** measure, collect feedback, fix issues, iterate.

The goal of launch is not maximum noise; it is validated learning. Did the right customers respond? Did they buy? Did they stay?

## Common GTM mistakes

- **Launching to everyone.** A product for everyone is a product for no one. Start with a specific segment.
- **Copying a competitor's GTM.** Their economics, team, and product are different. What works for them may fail for you.
- **Underestimating acquisition cost.** Customer acquisition is often more expensive and slower than founders expect.
- **Ignoring retention.** Acquiring customers who churn is a leaky bucket.

## GTM is a living strategy

Markets change. Competitors respond. Customers evolve. A GTM strategy is not a document written once; it is a framework that is revisited as evidence accumulates.
