Launch plans are usually measured by activity — press coverage, email sends, social posts, an event — and a launch can generate all of those and still fail. The reason is that a launch has one real job: to get the product in front of the people for whom it is obviously the answer, and to leave behind evidence about whether that was true.

**Direct answer:** A go-to-market strategy connects a position to a specific first customer set and a repeatable route to them. The launch is the first measurement of whether the connection holds. The work therefore starts before any announcement: define the situation in which the product wins, identify who is in that situation, choose the route by which they will encounter it, and instrument what happens. Porter's trade-off logic applies — "a strategic position is not sustainable unless there are trade-offs with other positions" — so a launch that tries to appeal to everyone has already lost the positioning decision.

## The first customer set is a strategic choice

The most important launch decision is not the channel; it is who the first customers are. A useful first set has three properties: the problem is acute, the product already solves it convincingly, and the users are reachable through a route you can repeat.

That last property is where many launches fail. A founder can personally recruit the first ten customers through relationships, and the company concludes the product has found a market. It has found a market of that founder's acquaintances, which is different. The test is whether the same approach works when it is handed to someone else.

Choosing a narrow first set is also a trade-off in Porter's sense. It means refusing other segments, including some that look attractive, and the refusal is what makes the launch measurable — a small, coherent group produces interpretable evidence, while a broad one produces a mixture of signals you cannot attribute.

## The route has to match the decision

The question "where do we launch?" is really "where does this buyer make this decision?" For a developer tool, that may be documentation and a package registry; for a consumer product, a marketplace; for an enterprise system, a partner or a procurement process.

Distribution is part of the answer, and its importance is often under-rated relative to product quality. "Trade-offs occur when activities are incompatible", and a route choice determines which other routes you cannot take simultaneously: a partner-led launch cannot also be a fully direct one without creating the channel conflict that kills both.

This is why a launch plan must name the route explicitly, including the thing that has to exist for it to work — a listing, a relationship, a piece of documentation, a marketplace approval — and who owns it.

## The message comes after the position

By launch time, the positioning work should be finished: the one-sentence description, the refusal list, and the proof points. If those are still being debated, the launch will produce a compromise message that nobody remembers.

Amazon's published company material is useful as a worked example of customer-led framing expressed in operating decisions rather than slogans. The lesson is not to imitate the language; it is that the language is downstream of something real. A launch message that has no corresponding activity behind it is a promise the product will struggle to keep, and the first customers will be the ones who notice.

## Measurement is part of the launch

An event model determines what can be learned. Google Analytics defines an event as a measurable "interaction or occurrence" — a page load, a purchase, a link clicked — and the same principle applies to a launch: if the behaviour you need to evaluate was not recorded, the launch produced attention but no evidence.

Three measures matter at launch:

- **Activation** — how many of the people who encountered the product reached the first moment of value. This is the test of the product and the message together.
- **Time to activation** — how long it took. A long delay often means the product is asking the user to do the work the launch should have done.
- **Quality of the first cohort** — whether these are the customers you wanted, not merely the ones who were easiest to reach.

For decisions about spend, attribution is insufficient. Google Ads describes attribution models as a way to "choose how much credit each ad interaction gets for your conversions" — a rule, not a counterfactual. Where a launch operates at market level, Google's published geo-experiment work provides the alternative: "one approach that Google has successfully employed to measure advertising effectiveness is geo experiments", with a systematic design process.

## A launch sequence that holds up

1. **Write the position and the refusal list.** If the trade-offs are not decided, the message will be generic.
2. **Define the first customer set** by acuteness of need, product fit, and repeatable reach.
3. **Choose the route** and name the thing that has to exist for it to work.
4. **Prepare the evidence** — the proof points, documentation, and answers to the questions that buyer will ask.
5. **Instrument activation and time to activation** before the announcement.
6. **Launch to the narrow set first**, and fix what the first cohort reveals.
7. **Expand only when the route is repeatable** by someone other than the founder.
