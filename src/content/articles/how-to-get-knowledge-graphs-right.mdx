## The mechanism

The knowledge graph entry describes a knowledge base that uses a graph-structured representation, with entities as nodes and relations as edges. The format is not the value; it is what allows a system to resolve identity and follow connections between things. The practical consequence is that the hard work is deciding what counts as the same entity and which relations are worth storing. A graph built on ambiguous identities produces <Mark>confident-looking answers that cannot be trusted</Mark>.

<Figure src="/images/articles/inline/how-to-get-knowledge-graphs-right-1.png" alt="A small graph with an author, an article, a topic, and a source connected by typed relations." caption="Conceptual: nodes are entities; labelled edges are the relationships worth modelling." />

The search engine marketing entry describes the practice of increasing a site's visibility in search engine results, through on-page and off-page work and through paid and organic activity. The practical consequence is that visibility is the outcome, while structured, consistent information about entities is one input among many. A graph can make an organisation's facts easier to interpret, but the source does not frame it as a ranking guarantee, and treating it as one confuses a supporting asset with the result.

The bridge between the two is disambiguation. When a system can tell which entity a mention refers to and how that entity relates to others, it can answer questions and keep facts consistent across surfaces. The practical consequence is that investment should go to canonical identifiers, clear schemas, and provenance for each edge, because those are the parts that determine whether the graph resolves identity correctly. A graph that stores relations without recording where they came from <Mark>cannot be audited when a fact is challenged</Mark>.

The mechanism ends with maintenance. Entities change, relations expire, and sources disagree, so a graph is a living artefact rather than a one-time build. The practical consequence is that a decision test must include an update path and an owner for correctness. Without them, the structure decays quietly, and the longer it is used the more confidently it propagates stale facts into answers and content.

## A worked example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative. A company with a large product catalogue wants a knowledge graph to support both internal answers and consistent public descriptions. Three checks shape the build. First, entity identity is tested: each product must have a stable identifier that survives name changes and similar model numbers. Second, provenance is recorded for each relation, so a specification can be traced to the source that supplied it. Third, graph attributes are compared with the public-facing descriptions to see whether they tell the same story. The outcome: the graph improves the consistency of answers and catches conflicting descriptions, while the team makes no claim about search ranking. The build is judged by the decision it supports, not by visibility.

## What it cannot do

A knowledge graph does not create truth; it encodes what was put into it. If the underlying records are inconsistent or wrong, the graph preserves those errors and makes them easier to query. The practical consequence is that the structure can <Mark>increase the speed and reach of a mistake</Mark>, which is worse than a slow mistake in a system nobody trusts. A team that treats the graph as an authority has reversed the relationship between the representation and the source.

The hidden cost is the work around the graph. Ontology design, entity resolution, deduplication, and ongoing curation consume skilled time that could go to the underlying data. For a modest or static dataset, a well-maintained table often answers the same questions at a fraction of the effort. The practical consequence is that the decision to build a graph should be justified by a query or integration need that simpler structures cannot meet, rather than by the appeal of the format.

<Trap>
The common failure modes are duplicate entities, ambiguous names, and relations with no provenance.
</Trap>

Two records that refer to the same thing under different names fragment the graph, while a relation added without a source cannot be checked when it is questioned. Circular sourcing is a quieter version, where a fact is copied from a system that originally took it from the graph. The practical consequence is that confidence in an answer depends on the chain behind it, not on the graph interface, and a clean visualisation hides a broken chain as readily as a sound one.

There are boundaries to its reach. A graph is not a search ranking guarantee, and the search marketing source does not offer one. It is also not a replacement for good content: if the public-facing pages are vague or contradictory, a well-structured internal graph cannot make them persuasive. The honest boundary is that a graph supports identity, consistency, and connected questions. Used as a promise of visibility or as a substitute for content quality, it will disappoint for reasons that have little to do with the technology.

Finally, the graph needs review because its meaning drifts. Schemas change, sources update, and the questions asked of the system evolve. The practical requirement is to name an owner, to record the provenance of relations, and to revisit the entity model on a fixed cadence. Without that discipline, the graph becomes a reflection of past decisions, and users cannot tell whether an answer is current. A maintained graph is useful; an abandoned one is a source of quietly wrong answers.

<PullQuote>
The counterargument that a graph is over-engineering for most teams is fair for small, static datasets and incomplete where entities must be reconciled across many sources and queried in connected ways.
</PullQuote>

## A decision test

1. State the decision or question the graph must support.
2. Check whether a simpler table could answer it first.
3. Define canonical identifiers for the core entities.
4. Record provenance for each relation before storing it.
5. Compare graph facts with the public-facing descriptions they support.
6. Name an owner and an update cadence for the structure.
7. Revisit the entity model when sources or questions change.

These steps keep the build tied to a real question. The test is passed when a reader can trace an answer to its entities and sources, and failed when the graph is presented as a ranking asset with no defined use. Revisit the decision when the source landscape or the question set changes.
