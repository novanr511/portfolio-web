## The metrics

**Comprehension.** Does the audience repeat the intended point in their own words, which the brand-awareness article treats as an unaided signal, as AAPOR's standards require for neutral wording if a survey is used, as the survey-design article advises?

**Belief and trust.** Does the story change what the audience believes about the product or cause, which the value-proposition article treats as the buyer's evaluation, as the content-marketing article argues for trust before the ask, as the credibility article treats as a base?

**Action.** Does the audience take the next step, which the funnel article treats as a stage measure, as the analytics article recommends for observed behaviour, as the NPS article warns about stated intentions?

**Reach with context.** Views matter only against the right audience, which the market-definition article advises, as [Marketing Dashboards without the Hype](/articles/marketing-dashboards-without-the-hype) argues for metrics tied to decisions, as the evaluation-rubric article requires for concrete criteria.

Three consequences follow. The audience should be defined, as the market-definition article advises, as the personas article treats segmentation, as the data-governance article requires for a dated definition. The measure should be tied to a decision, as [Marketing Dashboards without the Hype](/articles/marketing-dashboards-without-the-hype) argues, as the evaluation-rubric article requires. And any survey should be neutrally worded, as AAPOR's standards require, as the survey-design article advises.

## A worked example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative. A brand publishes a founder story and reports 100,000 views.

Three checks decide whether the story worked. First, what did viewers understand, as the brand-awareness and survey-design articles advise, with unaided language, as AAPOR's standards require? Second, did trust or belief change, as the value-proposition and content-marketing articles treat, with a measurement tied to the decision, as the rubric article requires? Third, did viewers take the intended next step, as the funnel and analytics articles advise, rather than stating an intention, as the NPS article warns?

Three conclusions follow. Comprehension and action need their own measures, as the rubric article requires. And the result should be recorded with the limits, as the decision-memo article requires, as the monitoring article advises for review.

## Why a story needs a baseline

A story changes understanding only relative to where the audience started, which the evaluation-rubric article treats as a baseline, as the geo-experiment guidance recommends for a comparison, as the decision-memo article requires for a recorded judgement.

They cannot prove a story caused a change, which the causal-inference article treats as a different question, as the correlation article warns about association, as the geo-experiment guidance recommends for comparisons. ## A decision test

1. Define the audience, as the market-definition article advises.
2. State the intended point and next step, as the rubric article requires.
3. Measure comprehension with unaided language, as the brand-awareness article advises.
4. Word any survey neutrally, as AAPOR's standards require.
5. Measure action, not intention, as the analytics and NPS articles advise.
6. Use a comparison for causal claims, as the experiment guidance recommends.
7. Record the result, as the decision-memo article requires.
