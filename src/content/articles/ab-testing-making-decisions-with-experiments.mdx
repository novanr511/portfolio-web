Guessing is expensive. Testing is cheap.

**Direct answer:** A/B testing is an experiment that compares two versions (A and B) of a webpage, feature, message, or process to determine which performs better on a defined metric. It replaces opinion with evidence by randomly splitting traffic and measuring outcomes. A/B testing works best when the hypothesis is clear, the sample is large enough, and the result is acted on—regardless of which version wins.

## What A/B testing is

In an A/B test:

- **Version A** is the control (current experience).
- **Version B** is the variant (proposed change).
- Traffic is randomly split between them.
- A metric (conversion rate, click-through, sign-ups, revenue) is measured for each.
- Statistical analysis determines whether the difference is real or noise.

The change can be anything: a headline, button color, price, layout, email subject, or feature flag.

## Why experiments beat opinions

The highest-paid person's opinion (HiPPO) is often wrong. Customers behave unpredictably; what seems like an improvement can hurt performance. A/B testing lets the customer vote with their behavior, not their words.

Examples:
- A "free trial" button outperforms a "contact sales" button.
- A shorter checkout form increases completion by 15%.
- A lower price increases volume but reduces revenue per customer.

Without testing, these are guesses. With testing, they are decisions.

## The experiment process

1. **Identify a problem.** Where are users dropping off? What metric needs improvement?
2. **Form a hypothesis.** "Changing X will increase Y because Z."
3. **Design the test.** Control vs variant, randomization, metric, duration.
4. **Run the test.** Split traffic, collect data, avoid peeking.
5. **Analyze.** Is the difference statistically significant? Is it practically meaningful?
6. **Decide.** Implement the winner, or learn and test again.

## Statistical significance matters

A result from 50 visitors is not reliable. Tests need enough observations in both control and variant to detect a real difference with confidence. Running a test too long wastes time; stopping too early produces false positives.

Also, testing many variants at once increases false-positive risk. Test one change at a time, or correct for multiple comparisons.

## What A/B testing cannot do

A/B testing answers "which performs better" but not "why." It cannot:
- Tell you what to test next (that comes from research and insight).
- Fix a bad product or offer.
- Measure long-term brand effects from short-term tests.
- Replace strategic judgment.

A test can show that variant B converts better, but not whether B builds long-term trust.

## Common mistakes

- **Testing without a hypothesis.** Random changes produce random results.
- **Peeking.** Checking results daily and stopping when they look good inflates false positives.
- **Ignoring segment differences.** A change that helps new users may hurt returning users.
- **Optimizing the wrong metric.** Increasing clicks at the expense of quality leads is not progress.

## When to test and when to decide

A/B testing is for optimization, not strategy. It cannot tell you whether to enter a new market or build a new product. It can tell you how to price, position, and present what you have already decided to do.
