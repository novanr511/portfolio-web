Open source and proprietary software are not enemies. They are different business and engineering models.

**Direct answer:** Open source software makes source code publicly available for anyone to view, modify, and distribute. Proprietary software keeps source code closed and restricts use through licenses. Neither is universally better; the right choice depends on cost, control, support, and strategic goals. Open source wins on flexibility and community; proprietary wins on accountability and commercial support.

## What open source means

Open source software is defined by the Open Source Definition: free redistribution, access to source code, derived works, no discrimination against persons or fields of endeavor, and technology neutrality. Examples include Linux, Apache, MySQL, Python, and thousands of libraries used by virtually every software company.

Open source does not mean free of cost. It means free of restrictions on use and modification. Companies often pay for open source through support contracts, hosting, or internal engineering time.

## What proprietary software means

Proprietary software is owned by a company or individual. Users get a license to use it, usually with restrictions: no reverse engineering, no redistribution, no modification. Examples include Microsoft Office, Adobe Creative Cloud, and most commercial enterprise software.

Proprietary software comes with a vendor: someone to call when it breaks, someone accountable for security patches, someone who owns the roadmap.

## The trade-offs

| Factor | Open Source | Proprietary |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Usually no license fee; cost in engineering time | License or subscription fee |
| Control | You can modify and fork | Vendor controls the roadmap |
| Support | Community or paid third-party | Vendor support contract |
| Security | Public code can be audited by anyone | Vendor is responsible for patches |
| Lock-in | Lower; you own the code you modify | Higher; vendor can change terms or prices |

## Who really wins?

Neither side wins universally. The winner depends on context:

- **Open source wins** when you need flexibility, want to avoid vendor lock-in, have engineering capacity to maintain the software, or want to benefit from community innovation.
- **Proprietary wins** when you need a single throat to choke, lack internal engineering resources, need guaranteed SLAs, or are buying software that is not your core competency.

Most modern businesses use both. They run Linux (open source) on servers and buy Salesforce (proprietary) for CRM. The question is not which model is better, but which model fits each part of the business.

## The hybrid reality

The line between open source and proprietary is blurring. Open core models offer a free open source version with paid proprietary features. Some companies open source their software but sell hosted versions. Others release proprietary software with source available for inspection but not modification.

The strategy is what matters: know what you own, what you rent, and where your leverage sits.
