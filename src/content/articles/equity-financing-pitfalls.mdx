<Lead>
Equity financing is often described as the opposite of debt: no repayment, no interest, just dilution. That framing misses the cost. Selling ownership changes control, sets expectations for the next round, and creates a valuation that future financings will be measured against. The cash is free; the ownership is not.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Raise equity when the capital funds a specific learning goal and the terms preserve enough ownership and control to reach the next stage. The equity-finance overview provides the instrument's context, and investor education treats ownership as a claim with risk. The boundary: a round cannot create product-market fit, which the startup-validation article treats as the prior question, and the valuation is conditional, as the startup-valuation article explains for estimates.

## The pitfalls

**Dilution without a plan.** A round that funds activity without a milestone leaves the company smaller in ownership and no closer to the next round, as [Cap Table Mechanics: Equity, Options, and Dilution](/articles/cap-table-mechanics-equity-options-and-dilution) explains for cumulative dilution, as the burn-rate article treats runway as a deadline.

**Terms beyond price.** Liquidation preferences, board rights, and protective provisions <Mark>change control</Mark>, which the term-sheet article argues for reading as the substance of a deal, as the governance checklist treats authority as a real distribution.

**A valuation that becomes an anchor.** A high round sets an expectation for the next one, which the startup-valuation article treats as a conditional estimate, and a down round can be costly, as the anti-dilution discussion in the term-sheet article explains.

**Investor fit.** Capital comes with expectations and often with help, which the venture-debt and founder-led-sales articles treat as part of the operating environment, as the negotiation article advises for aligned parties.

Three consequences follow. The use of funds should be explicit, which the 13-week cash-flow article requires for an operating view. The ownership effect should be modelled, as [Cap Table Mechanics: Equity, Options, and Dilution](/articles/cap-table-mechanics-equity-options-and-dilution) advises. And the decision should be recorded with the assumptions, as the decision-memo article requires.

## The cost after the round

<KeyPoint>
The dilution on a term sheet is only the first cost of equity.
</KeyPoint>

New investors often bring reporting expectations, board seats, and a view on how capital should be deployed, which the term-sheet article treats as control terms with real consequences, as the governance checklist requires an accountable owner for any ongoing obligation.

<Trap>
A founder who plans only the cash and not the governance is likely to find the post-round company unfamiliar, which the management article analogises to culture built by decisions.
</Trap>

The next round also prices this one. A valuation that was generous at the time becomes an anchor, and a flat or lower next round can trigger anti-dilution provisions or simply damage morale, as [Term Sheet Basics: What Founders Should Read Twice](/articles/term-sheet-basics-what-founders-should-read-twice) explains for down-round mechanics, as the startup-valuation article treats each round as a conditional estimate. This is why <Mark>the milestone matters more than the headline number</Mark>, which the burn-rate article frames as time to learn, not comfort.

Finally, equity cannot be reused casually. Each round <Mark>narrows the founders' ownership</Mark> and changes the cap table for everyone who joins later, as the cap-table article models for cumulative dilution, as the technical-debt article warns about complexity that accumulates without decision. The decision should therefore be recorded with the milestone and the terms, as the decision-memo article requires for consequential choices.

## What equity financing cannot do

It cannot fix a weak business, which the value-proposition article treats as the buyer's real evaluation. It cannot remove risk, which investor education states explicitly. And it is not free of obligations — governance and reporting expectations come with the capital, as [Term Sheet Basics: What Founders Should Read Twice](/articles/term-sheet-basics-what-founders-should-read-twice) treats control terms as real, as the governance checklist requires an accountable owner.
