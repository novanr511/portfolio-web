<Lead>
A team can have a passing test suite and still take weeks to ship a small change. The reason is that pipeline health and delivery performance are different properties. The DORA research programme identified a small set of measures that describe how software actually reaches users, which is more useful than counting builds.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Track deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, and time to restore service, as the DORA framework does. GitHub Actions documentation covers the pipeline mechanics. The boundary: these are system-level measures, not targets to game — optimising one in isolation can harm the others, which the observability article warns about for metrics that do not reflect outcomes.

## The four measures

**Deployment frequency** — how often changes reach production. A low frequency <Mark>usually means large batches</Mark>, which the DevOps article explains for the risk of infrequent integration.

**Lead time for changes** — the time from a change being ready to its deployment. A long lead time <Mark>hides queueing</Mark>, which the batching-economics article treats as a cost of waiting that no amount of local speed removes.

**Change failure rate** — the share of deployments that cause a problem requiring a fix or rollback. A low rate with a very low frequency may mean the team rarely ships, which the risk article treats as exposure rather than safety.

**Time to restore** — how long it takes to recover from a failure. A fast restore can be more valuable than a low failure rate, as [Site Reliability Engineering: SLOs, Error Budgets, and Incident Response](/articles/site-reliability-engineering-slos-error-budgets-and-incident-response) argues for containment and recovery.

Three consequences follow. The measures should be read together, which the evaluation-rubric article argues for multi-dimensional scoring. They should be <Mark>measured from the system, not self-reported</Mark>, as the observability article recommends for reliable signals. And they describe the whole path to production, not one stage, which the data-pipeline article treats as the integration work behind a result.

## A worked example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

<Trap>
A team reports that its CI pipeline runs in eight minutes, which it treats as a success.
</Trap>

DORA-style measurement shows a lead time of fourteen days from commit to production.

Three checks explain the gap. First, where does the time sit, which the batching-economics article treats as queueing versus processing? The eight-minute pipeline may wait three days for a review and ten for a release window. Second, what is the batch size, as the DevOps article asks for integration frequency? A two-week batch makes each deployment riskier, which the change-failure measure would show. Third, can the team restore quickly, as [Site Reliability Engineering: SLOs, Error Budgets, and Incident Response](/articles/site-reliability-engineering-slos-error-budgets-and-incident-response) requires? A fast pipeline does not help if rollback takes hours.

Three conclusions follow.

<KeyPoint>
The local metric was real and irrelevant to the outcome, as the evaluation-rubric article warns about proxy measures.
</KeyPoint>

The four-system view reveals the constraint, which the observability article treats as the point of measurement. And the decision to change the release process should be recorded, as the decision-memo article requires.

## What pipeline metrics cannot do

They cannot tell you whether you are building the right product, which the value-proposition article treats as the prior question. They do not guarantee reliability, which the site-reliability article frames as a separate discipline of SLOs and error budgets. And they can be gamed by redefining what counts as a deployment, which the data-governance article treats as a definition problem.

The counterargument that these metrics are too abstract for a small team is fair about tooling and wrong about the logic.

<PullQuote>
A four-number view of delivery is easier to reason about than a dashboard of build times, which the observability article argues for useful alerts over raw data.
</PullQuote>

## A decision test

1. Measure the four DORA metrics from the system, not by report.
2. Break lead time into queueing and processing, as the batching article advises.
3. Check batch size and integration frequency, as the DevOps article recommends.
4. Read frequency and failure rate together, as the evaluation-rubric article requires.
5. Measure time to restore with the incident-response method.
6. Treat the measures as a system, not independent targets, as the observability article warns.
7. Review after a change to the pipeline or team, as the monitoring article advises.
