## The mechanism

Three points matter:

- **Direction matters.** Underforecasting a high-margin item costs a lost sale; overforecasting a perishable one costs waste. A symmetric error metric treats them alike.
- **The average hides the tail.** A forecast can be accurate on most days and badly wrong on a few, which are the days that cause stockouts or spoilage.
- **A baseline is required.** A complex model that cannot beat a seasonal average has not earned its cost.

The sequence is: define the decision, choose a metric that reflects its cost, report the distribution, then compare with a baseline. The choice is local judgement, so it is `inferred`; the context is `source-reported`.

## A worked example

*Illustrative.* A retailer forecasts weekly demand for a perishable item.

Three checks decide. First, which error is more expensive — too much stock or too little? Second, how large are the worst errors, not just the mean? Third, does the model beat a simple average of the last four weeks?

The outcome is a metric choice with a recorded rationale. The numbers are hypothetical; the method is the contribution.

## Why the baseline is part of the metric

A forecast that beats a simple average is doing useful work; one that does not is adding complexity without value. The baseline should be chosen before the model, because selecting it after seeing the results invites a comparison that flatters the new approach. A seasonal average or a naive carry-forward is usually enough to start.

The second reason for a baseline is communication. Stating that a model reduces error by a fraction of a percent is abstract; stating that it <Mark>reduces the worst stockouts by a third</Mark> is concrete. Both describe the same system, but only one connects to the decision a planner makes.

## Why direction belongs in the report

An error metric that treats overforecasting and underforecasting as identical hides the asymmetry of real costs. For a perishable item, too much stock becomes waste; for a high-margin item, too little becomes a lost sale. Reporting errors by direction lets the reader see which failure the model tends to make, which is often more important than the size of the average.

## Why the distribution matters

<Trap>
An average error is easy to compute and easy to misread.
</Trap>

<KeyPoint>
Two forecasts with the same mean can have very different tails, and the tail is where service failures occur.
</KeyPoint>

Reporting percentiles or the count of large errors gives a clearer picture of the risk a planner will actually experience.

A final consideration is how the forecast is used. A metric that is good for evaluating a model in isolation may not be good for the person who must order stock. A planner needs to know the range of plausible demand and the consequences of each decision, not a point estimate presented as certainty. <Mark>Publishing the distribution alongside the headline number</Mark> makes the uncertainty visible and lets the decision reflect the cost of being wrong.

The data behind a forecast also has a date. A model trained on an older period can be reliable until a structural change makes the past a poor guide. Recording when the training data ended, and stating what change would invalidate the model, turns the forecast into <Mark>a maintained artefact rather than a one-time result</Mark>.

The chosen metric should be written down with the decision it serves.

## What it cannot do

It cannot remove uncertainty, which belongs to the conditions the forecast describes. It does not guarantee that a better metric improves decisions, which still requires someone to act on it. And it cannot replace a baseline comparison.

<PullQuote>
The counterargument that a single metric is simpler is fair for reporting and incomplete for a decision with asymmetric costs.
</PullQuote>

## A decision test

1. Define the decision.
2. State which error costs more.
3. Report the distribution, not only the mean.
4. Compare with a simple baseline.
5. Use dated data.
6. Record the metric rationale and recheck date.
