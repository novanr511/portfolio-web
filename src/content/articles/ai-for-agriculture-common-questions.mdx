**Direct answer:** Use AI in agriculture for bounded tasks with measurable outcomes — detection, estimation, scheduling — with local validation and a human decision. ## Common questions

**What can AI actually do on a farm?** Detect disease or pests from images, estimate yield, and support irrigation or input scheduling, which the precision-agriculture entry describes for sensing and variable action, as the FAO material covers digital tools, as the evaluation-rubric article requires for concrete measures.

**Does a model transfer between regions?** Not necessarily. <Mark>Conditions, crops, and practices differ</Mark>, which the benchmarks article warns about applying results beyond their conditions, as the embedding-selection article advises for local evaluation, as the data-governance article requires for dated data.

**Who makes the decision?** The grower, which the human-in-the-loop article treats as meaningful review, as the AI-safety article treats the system as a support, as the decision-memo article requires for a recorded judgement.

<KeyPoint>
A model output is not a recommendation by itself, as the legal and safety articles treat high-impact decisions as formal.
</KeyPoint>

**What data is needed?** Local, dated observations with a source, which the data-governance article requires, as the data-quality article warns about bad inputs, as the FRED-style discipline of dated series applies to external data, as the monitoring article advises for review on change.

Three consequences follow. The task should be narrow and measurable, as the rubric article requires. Local validation should precede use, as the benchmarks article advises. And the decision should remain with a person, as the oversight article requires, as the decision-memo article requires for a record.

## A worked example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative. A tool claims to detect a crop disease from leaf photos with high accuracy in a trial.

Three checks decide whether to use it. First, was the trial conducted under <Mark>conditions comparable to the local farm</Mark>, as the benchmarks article asks, with a dated source, as the data-governance article requires? Second, what is the cost of a false positive or a false negative, as the risk-assessment article requires, with a measure tied to the decision, as the evaluation-rubric article requires? Third, does a grower review the result before acting, as the oversight article advises, with a record of the decision, as the decision-memo article requires?

Three conclusions follow.

Trial accuracy does not transfer automatically, which the benchmark check reveals.
</Trap>

The cost of an error matters more than the headline score, as the risk article requires. And the decision stays with a person, as the oversight article advises, as the FAO material treats digital tools as support.

## A decision test

1. Define one bounded task with a measure, as the rubric article requires.
2. Check that evidence matches local conditions, as the benchmarks article advises.
3. Source and date all data, as the data-governance article requires.
4. Weigh the cost of errors, as the risk-assessment article requires.
5. Keep the decision with a grower, as the oversight article advises.
6. Check current regional rules, as the legal article requires.
7. Record the decision and review on change, as the decision-memo article requires.
