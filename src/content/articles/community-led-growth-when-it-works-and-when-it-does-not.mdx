<Lead>
Community-led growth is often prescribed as a universal strategy: build a community, and growth follows. In practice, community works in a narrow set of conditions and fails when it is treated as a marketing tactic. A forum with no genuine peer interaction is a support burden with a friendly name.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Community leads to growth when members get value from each other around a problem or identity the product serves, and when the product benefits from their interaction. Demand-generation principles explain the preference-building stage community can support, and AAPOR's standards apply if you survey members about their experience. The boundary: a community cannot be manufactured by invitation alone, and it should not be measured by member count, which the email-metrics article argues for engagement over vanity figures.

## When it works

**A shared problem or identity.** Members need a reason to interact that is not the product's feature list — a craft, an industry, a common challenge, as the category-design article explains for shared frames.

**Peer value.**

<KeyPoint>
A member's question should be answered by another member, not always by the company.
</KeyPoint>

<Mark>The value comes from the network</Mark>, which the network-effects article covers as a structural advantage.

**A product connection.** The community should make the product more useful — through templates, examples, or support — rather than sitting beside it as an unrelated social space, as the content-marketing article argues for value before asks.

## When it fails

**The company does all the talking.**

<Trap>
A broadcast channel with a comment section is not a community, which the social-media-marketing article distinguishes from genuine interaction.
</Trap>

**No critical mass.** A forum with few active members feels empty and repels the next member, which the waitlist article treats as an engagement problem rather than a signup problem.

**It is used to deflect support.** Sending customers to a community to reduce support cost, without anyone answering, damages trust, which the retention article identifies as a leading cause of churn.

## A worked example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A B2B software company considers a community. Three checks:

1. **Do members have a reason to talk?** The product serves operations managers who face similar reporting problems — a plausible shared problem, as the customer-segmentation article recommends for defining a real segment.
2. **Will peers answer?** The team identifies 50 power users who already answer questions in other channels. Seeding with them gives the community a chance of peer value, which the advocacy article covers as a source of credible voices.
3. **Does the product benefit?** The community would host templates and workflow examples that make the product more useful, which the content-repurposing article treats as a value asset.

Measurement after launch should focus on three things: the share of questions answered by members rather than staff, repeat participation by members, and whether community members retain better than comparable non-members — with the attribution article's caution about selection, which the loyalty article also confronts.

Three conclusions follow:

- **The preconditions were checked before the platform was chosen**, which the AI-integration blueprint recommends for any new initiative.
- **The metrics are interaction and retention**, not member count, which the email-metrics article argues for vanity avoidance.
- **The company's role is to enable**, not to dominate, which the brand-voice article treats as a matter of restraint.

## What community cannot do

**It cannot fix a weak product.** <Mark>Members will not build a community around something that does not solve a real problem</Mark>, which the value-proposition article argues.

**It cannot be outsourced to a platform.** The tool does not create the peer value, just as an email platform does not create a good newsletter, which the newsletter article explains.

**It cannot be measured by size.** <Mark>A large quiet community is worth less than a small active one</Mark>, which the brand-awareness article distinguishes from meaningful engagement.

The counterargument — that every product needs a community — is a fashion, not a finding. Many successful products have none, and the decision should follow the three preconditions, not a wish to be seen as community-led.

## A decision test

1. **Name the shared problem or identity** that would bring members together.
2. **Check for peer value** — will members answer each other, as the advocacy article describes?
3. **Check the product connection** — does interaction make the product more useful?
4. **Seed with credible members** before launching, as the advocacy article recommends.
5. **Measure peer answers and repeat participation**, not member count.
6. **Compare retention with a comparable non-member group**, accounting for selection, as the attribution article warns.
7. **Keep the company in an enabling role**, which the brand-voice article advises.
