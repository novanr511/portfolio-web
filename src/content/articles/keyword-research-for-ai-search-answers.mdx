<Lead>
Keyword research for traditional search relies on volume, difficulty, and a SERP you can inspect. In AI answer engines, the output is a generated answer with a handful of citations, and <Mark>there is no reliable public volume data</Mark> for the questions people ask. The honest approach is to work with what can be observed and to label the rest as a hypothesis.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:**

<KeyPoint>
Use three evidence sources — questions your own customers ask, queries visible in search console or server logs, and the citation patterns you can observe in answer engines — then structure content so a specific claim is easy to extract and verify.
</KeyPoint>

AAPOR's standards apply to any survey-based question research, and demand-generation principles still govern which decision a piece serves. The boundary: no tactic guarantees an AI citation, and no tool can currently give you a trustworthy volume number for answer-engine queries.

## What can and cannot be observed

**Observable:** the questions that arrive in your support inbox, the queries that bring visitors to your site, the sources an answer engine cites when you ask a question, and changes in referral traffic from answer platforms.

**Not observable with confidence:** how many people asked a question inside an answer engine, which answers they saw, or whether a citation changed their behaviour. Any tool claiming precise numbers here <Mark>deserves scrutiny</Mark>. The measuring-GEO article covers what can actually be counted.

Three consequences follow:

- **Use observed questions as the primary input.** They are real, denominated by your own traffic, and tied to decisions your business cares about.
- **Treat answer-engine citations as directional.** A citation today does not imply one tomorrow, and the selection mechanism is not fully public.
- **Do not invent volume figures.** A keyword list with made-up numbers is worse than one with none.

## A worked example of topic selection

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A company sells billing software. It gathers 300 questions from support tickets and 200 query strings from search console over a month. After removing duplicates, three candidate topics emerge:

- "How do I reconcile invoices with payments?" — 40 support tickets, 900 search queries.
- "Best billing software for small teams" — 5 support tickets, 2,000 search queries.
- "What is a credit note versus a refund?" — 60 support tickets, 300 search queries.

Three decisions follow:

1. **Weight by decision proximity, not volume alone.** The comparison query has the highest volume and the lowest support frequency. It serves buyers already in a decision, which may be valuable — but it is also the most competitive and the least differentiated.
2. **Weight by evidence you can provide.** The reconciliation question is one the company can answer with real product data and a worked example. That makes a defensible, extractable answer more likely.
3. **Check the answer-engine surface.** Ask the question in the engines you care about and note which sources are cited. If authoritative accounting sites dominate, a vendor answer needs to be exceptionally specific to be chosen.

Three conclusions follow:

- **Pick the reconciliation topic first.** It has real demand, a decision context, and evidence you uniquely possess.
- **Structure the answer for extraction** — a direct answer, a worked example with numbers, and a clear statement of scope.
- **Record the method**, so the choice can be reviewed when the data changes.

## Structuring for extraction

An answer engine is more likely to use a passage that is self-contained and specific. Four habits help:

**Answer the question in the first sentence.** A direct statement is easier to extract than a paragraph that builds to one.

**Include sourced numbers with their context.** A figure with a denominator, date, and unit is more defensible than a round claim.

**State the boundary.** "This applies to X, not Y" gives the engine a precise scope and gives a reader a reason to trust the answer.

**Use clear headings that match the question.** A heading phrased as the query helps both the engine and the reader locate the relevant passage.

None of these guarantees inclusion. They make the content easier to use if a system chooses to cite it, which is <Mark>the honest claim</Mark>.

## What the method cannot promise

**No citation guarantee.** Answer-engine selection is not fully documented, and a tool that promises inclusion is promising something it cannot control.

**No stable volume.**

<Trap>
Query counts from your own logs are real; volume estimates for answer engines are not.
</Trap>

Keep the distinction explicit.

**No lasting advantage.** A format that works today may not work after the engine changes. Re-check the observable signals periodically.

The counterargument — that if it cannot be measured it should not be done — is too strong.

<PullQuote>
Content that answers real customer questions has value independent of AI citations.
</PullQuote>

The method simply prevents that value from being overstated.

## A decision test

1. **Gather questions from your own channels** with a stated time window.
2. **Weight by decision proximity and evidence availability**, not volume alone.
3. **Inspect the answer-engine surface** for the source types it cites.
4. **Answer directly** in the first sentence of the relevant section.
5. **Include sourced numbers** with denominators and dates.
6. **State the scope** of the answer explicitly.
7. **Re-check the observable signals** when the engine or your data changes.
