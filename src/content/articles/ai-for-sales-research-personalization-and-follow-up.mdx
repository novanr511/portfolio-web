The promise of AI in sales is often framed as automated persuasion, which is both unappealing and a weak description of what the technology does well. The real value is in preparation — researching an account, assembling relevant evidence, and keeping follow-up consistent. Those are tasks where a seller's time is scarce and the output can be checked.

**Direct answer:** Use AI to summarise account research, personalise outreach with verifiable facts, and maintain follow-up cadence — not to invent claims or pressure prospects. Anthropic's use-case guides cover research and content patterns, and the O*NET task descriptions identify what sales work involves. The boundary: a personalised message with a wrong fact damages credibility, so every generated claim needs a source, as [How to Reduce Hallucinations in LLM Applications](/articles/how-to-reduce-hallucinations-in-llm-applications) explains.

## Three uses

**Account research.** Summarise public information — filings, news, job postings — into a short brief with sources. The value is time saved, not a claim the model could not find elsewhere, as the financial-statement article explains for primary sources.

**Personalisation.** Draft an opening that references a verifiable fact about the recipient's situation. A fact with a source is useful; an invented detail is a credibility failure, which the reduce-hallucinations article covers.

**Follow-up.** Keep a consistent cadence across a pipeline and draft reminders tied to the last interaction. This is a workflow-automation task, as the workflow article describes, not a creative one.

## A worked workflow

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A seller prepares for 20 account meetings a week. The AI workflow:

1. Gathers public sources for each account and produces a one-page brief with citations.
2. Drafts three personalisation points, each linked to a source, for the seller to verify.
3. Schedules follow-up drafts tied to the meeting outcome.

Three checks decide whether it works:

1. **Citation accuracy** — does the cited source actually support the generated point? A wrong citation is worse than no personalisation, as [How to Reduce Hallucinations in LLM Applications](/articles/how-to-reduce-hallucinations-in-llm-applications) argues.
2. **Time saved** — measure preparation time before and after, net of verification. A brief that takes ten minutes to check may not save much, which the email-metrics article explains for intermediate metrics.
3. **Reply quality** — do personalised messages get more substantive replies, or merely more opens? The attribution article warns against treating a proxy as a result.

Three conclusions follow:

- **The seller verifies every fact**, as the human-in-the-loop design requires.
- **Consistency of follow-up** is a reliable gain, while personalisation quality is empirical, which the demand-generation article distinguishes from capture mechanics.
- **The tool is a preparation assistant**, not an autonomous sender, which the security checklist recommends for actions with consequences.

## Keeping a source of truth

Personalisation is only as good as the facts behind it. Three practices keep the process defensible:

**One research record per account.** Store the brief, the sources, and the date in a single place so a seller can see what is current and what has been superseded, as the data-governance article recommends for any relied-upon record.

**Expire stale facts.** A job posting or funding round mentioned in outreach loses relevance quickly. A date on each fact and a rule for when it is too old prevents a personalised message from referencing news that is a year old, which the monitoring article covers as drift.

**Separate verified from inferred.** A fact with a source is different from an inference about a prospect's priorities. Label them separately so the seller knows which claims can be stated directly and which are hypotheses to test in conversation, as the evaluation-rubric article argues for distinguishing evidence from interpretation.

These habits turn personalisation from a copywriting trick into a research discipline that a seller can stand behind.

## What sales AI cannot do

**It cannot create trust.** A message that references a fact accurately is still a cold outreach; the relationship develops through conversation, which the content-marketing article explains as preference building.

**It cannot replace qualification.** An account that fits a research profile may not have a need or budget, which the funnel article covers as a stage distinction.

**It cannot invent credibility.** Generated claims without sources damage the seller's reputation, which the reduce-hallucinations article treats as a core failure mode.

The counterargument — that buyers can tell when outreach is AI-generated — is an argument for using AI to prepare better material, not for avoiding it. A well-sourced brief and a thoughtful message are indistinguishable in quality from one written by hand, which is the point.

## A decision test

1. **Use AI for research and preparation**, not automated persuasion.
2. **Cite a source for every personalisation point.**
3. **Have the seller verify facts** before sending.
4. **Automate follow-up cadence** with drafts tied to prior interactions.
5. **Measure preparation time net of verification.**
6. **Score reply quality**, not opens or sends.
7. **Keep the seller accountable** for every message, as the governance checklist requires.
