## The practices

**Define the exposure.** Is the organisation a buyer or a seller of the physical commodity, and what does a price move do to it, which the risk-assessment article requires, as [Hedging Best Practices](/articles/hedging-best-practices) advises, as the value-at-risk article treats aggregation?

**Use dated supply-and-demand data.** Inventories, production, and consumption move prices, which the commodity-market entry explains, as FRED provides dated series, as the data-governance article requires for provenance, as the time-series article warns about non-stationarity.

**Distinguish hedge from bet.** A hedge reduces an existing exposure, which the hedging article treats as the purpose, as the derivatives article explains for offsetting positions, as the options article warns about obligations, as the risk-assessment article requires residual risk.

**Read the contract.** Standardization helps comparison, but the contract specifies quantity, quality, and delivery, which the futures entry explains, as the term-sheet article treats formal terms as the substance, as the basis-risk discussion in the hedging article warns about imperfect matches.

Three consequences follow. The exposure should be stated before the instrument, as [Hedging Best Practices](/articles/hedging-best-practices) advises. The data should be dated, as data governance requires. And the outcome should be recorded with residual risk, as the decision-memo article requires, as the risk article requires.

## Why the physical position comes first

The instrument should follow the exposure, not the other way around. A processor that buys a commodity has a different risk from a producer that sells it, which the risk-assessment article treats as the starting point, as [Hedging Best Practices](/articles/hedging-best-practices) advises, as the value-at-risk discussion treats aggregation.
