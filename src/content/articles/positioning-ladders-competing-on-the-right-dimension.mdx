Positioning is often described as finding a place in the market's mind, which is vague until you ask what the market is ranking. A positioning ladder is the sequence of alternatives a buyer considers for a given problem. Competing on the wrong dimension puts you low on a ladder nobody cares about; choosing the right one changes the comparison itself.

**Direct answer:** A positioning ladder is the ordered set of options a customer considers for a decision. Porter's strategy argument and the blue-ocean framing explain the two moves — compete within the ladder on a dimension you can defend, or change the ladder by framing the problem differently. The boundary: a ladder is a hypothesis about buyer behaviour that must be tested with evidence, as the positioning-workshop article requires, and it cannot substitute for a real difference, as the strategy article argues.

## Two moves

**Climb the existing ladder.** Buyers already compare options on a dimension — price, speed, reliability. Choose the one where you can win and that buyers value, then support it with activities, as Porter's fit argument requires.

**Change the ladder.** Reframe the problem so the old criteria matter less, which the category-design article describes as creating new market space. This is harder and requires educating buyers, as the waitlist article notes about building demand before a product is understood.

Three consequences follow:

- **The dimension must be valued by buyers**, not chosen for internal convenience, as the value-proposition article argues.
- **The claim must be defensible**, which the positioning workshop tests with customers.
- **The ladder can shift**, which the monitoring article treats as a change trigger.

## A worked example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A project-management tool competes on a ladder where incumbents are ranked by feature breadth. It cannot win there. Two options:

1. **Climb with a different criterion.** Choose setup time — the tool is usable in an afternoon — and test whether buyers rank on that. If yes, build messaging and product around it, as the landing-page article recommends for B2B clarity.
2. **Change the ladder.** Frame the problem as "coordination without meetings" rather than "project management software", which the category-design article describes. This requires evidence that buyers experience the problem that way, as the positioning workshop requires.

Three checks decide:

- **Buyer evidence.** Do customers actually rank on the chosen dimension? Survey with AAPOR-style wording and a defined population, as the survey standards require.
- **Defensibility.** Can competitors copy the claim without changing anything, as the positioning workshop asks? Setup time backed by architecture is more defensible than a tagline.
- **Outcome.** Does the new frame change qualified conversion and retention, as the funnel article measures stages?

Three conclusions follow:

- **The dimension is tested**, not declared, as the workshop article recommends.
- **Climbing is cheaper than changing the ladder**, which the waitlist article treats as an education cost.
- **Both require a real difference**, as the strategy article argues for activities that support a position.

## Reading the market's ladder

You cannot choose a ladder you cannot see. Three sources reveal it:

**Buyer language.** The words customers use to describe alternatives, which the voice-of-customer article recommends gathering with AAPOR-style wording, reveal the categories they already hold.

**Lost-deal notes.** Why buyers chose a competitor tells you which dimension decided it, which the competitive-intelligence article treats as primary evidence for positioning.

**Switching behaviour.** What made customers leave a competitor shows the criterion that mattered at the moment of choice, as the retention article analyses causes of churn.

These sources are evidence, not opinion, which the evaluation-rubric article requires for any claim about what the market ranks on.

The practical value of a ladder is that it turns a vague ambition into a specific choice. When a team says it wants to be "the best solution", it has made no decision about what buyers compare. Naming the ladder forces a question about which dimension actually decides the purchase and whether the business can defend a position on it. That discipline is useful even when the conclusion is to compete on familiar ground, because it makes the reason explicit and gives the team something to test.

## What positioning ladders cannot do

**They cannot create a benefit.** A frame highlights a real difference; it does not manufacture one, which the value-proposition article argues.

**They are not permanent.** Competitors and buyer needs change, which the monitoring article covers as a change trigger.

**They do not replace execution.** A position on a ladder is a claim that the product must keep, as the reputation article warns about claims that experience contradicts.

The counterargument — that all positioning is perception — misses the strategic point. Perception is shaped by what the business actually does, which the strategy article treats as the substance behind a position.

## A decision test

1. **Name the ladder** buyers currently use, as the positioning workshop recommends.
2. **Identify the dimensions** they rank on, with AAPOR-style evidence.
3. **Choose where you can win** and that buyers value, as the value-proposition article argues.
4. **Test changing the ladder** only with evidence of a different frame, as the category-design article requires.
5. **Support the claim with activities**, as Porter's fit argument requires.
6. **Measure qualified outcomes**, as the funnel article recommends.
7. **Review on market change**, as the monitoring article advises.
