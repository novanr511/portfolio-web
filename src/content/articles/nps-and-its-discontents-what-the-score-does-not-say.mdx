The Net Promoter Score reduces customer sentiment to one number between -100 and 100. That simplicity is why it appears on board decks everywhere, and why it is frequently over-interpreted. The score records an answer to one question; it does not explain why, and it is not a forecast of growth.

**Direct answer:** NPS measures the balance of respondents who would recommend a company to a friend or colleague, on an 0–10 scale, grouped into promoters, passives, and detractors. AAPOR's standards govern the survey behind it — population, wording, and sampling decide whether the number means anything. The boundary: the score does not measure actual referral behaviour, is sensitive to sample composition, and cannot tell you what to fix, which the voice-of-customer article argues for turning feedback into decisions.

## What the score captures

**A stated intention.** Asking "how likely are you to recommend?" records a reported attitude, not an observed action, which the AAPOR standards require you to state as such.

**A distribution summarised.** Promoters minus detractors collapses a richer set of answers into one figure, which the email-metrics article warns about for aggregate proxies.

**A snapshot of respondents.** Who was asked and when determines the result, which the survey standards make central.

## A worked interpretation

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A company reports an NPS of 40, up from 30 a year ago. Three checks decide what it means:

1. **Sample composition.** Was the survey sent to all customers or to a segment? If only engaged customers responded, the score is biased, which AAPOR's standards require you to disclose. Suppose the response rate was 12% — the result describes respondents, not the base.
2. **What changed.** Did the score rise because detractors decreased or promoters increased? The same net figure can hide opposite shifts, which the email-metrics article treats as a reason to report distributions.
3. **Behavioural check.** Did referrals actually increase, as the referral article measures with rewards and holdouts? If stated intention rose but behaviour did not, the score is a weak proxy, as the attribution article warns about credit rules.

Three conclusions follow:

- **The number alone is not a result**, which the evaluation-rubric article argues for concrete evidence.
- **The distribution and the sample matter more than the net**, as the survey standards require.
- **The follow-up question — why? — is where value lies, which the voice-of-customer article treats as turning feedback into decisions.

## What to use alongside it

**The open-ended reason.** Ask why a respondent gave that score and group the answers by theme, as the voice-of-customer article recommends.

**Behavioural metrics.** Retention, repeat purchase, and referral conversion are observed, which the retention and referral articles cover as outcomes rather than intentions.

**Segment cuts.** A single average hides a problem in one group, which the customer-segmentation article argues for real differences.

The counterargument — that NPS is useless — is too strong. It is a simple, consistent signal for tracking direction over time within one organisation, provided its limits are stated, as the brand-awareness article argues for signals with a defined scope.

## Keeping the survey honest

The value of a trend depends on the survey staying comparable. Three practices protect it:

**Fix the wording.** Changing the question, even slightly, breaks the series, which AAPOR's standards require for valid comparisons over time.

**Fix the population.** Sending the survey to a different mix of customers changes the score without changing the experience, which the survey standards treat as a sampling issue.

**Report the sample with the score.** A number without a response rate and a population is hard to interpret, which the email-metrics article argues for denominators.

These practices are unglamorous and determine whether the score is a signal or noise, which the evaluation-rubric article treats as the difference between a metric and a measurement.

## What NPS cannot do

**It cannot forecast growth.** Stated intention and actual behaviour differ, which the referral article treats as a central measurement problem.

**It cannot tell you what to fix.** That requires the reason and the behavioural data, as the voice-of-customer article explains.

**It cannot compare across cultures without care.** Response styles differ by region, which the survey standards treat as a wording and context issue.

## A decision test

1. **State the population and response rate** with every score, as AAPOR's standards require.
2. **Report the distribution**, not just the net, as the email-metrics article advises.
3. **Ask why** and group answers by theme, as the voice-of-customer article recommends.
4. **Compare with behaviour** — retention and referrals, as the retention and referral articles cover.
5. **Cut by segment** to find hidden problems, as the customer-segmentation article argues.
6. **Keep wording stable** over time, which the survey standards require for trend validity.
7. **Treat the score as a signal**, not a verdict, as the evaluation-rubric article advises.
