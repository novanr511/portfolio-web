A REIT is a company that owns income-producing property and distributes much of its income to shareholders, which makes it look like a cross between a stock and a bond. That hybrid nature creates questions that ordinary equity analysis does not answer: what is the property worth, how is the debt structured, and is the distribution covered, as the Nareit overview of the sector explains.

**Direct answer:** Analyse a REIT by asking what properties it owns, what they earn, how the debt is arranged, and whether the distribution is covered by recurring cash flow. Nareit is the primary industry source for the structure, and investor education covers the risks of income investments. The boundary: this is educational orientation, not personalised advice — the figures are jurisdiction-specific and change, as [Real Estate Markets: REITs, Property Cycles, and Investment Analysis](/articles/real-estate-markets-reits-property-cycles-and-investment-analysis) treats property cycles as a source of variation, as the tax article requires a dated check.

## The questions

**What does it own?** The property type, geography, and quality determine the income stream, which the real-estate article explains for how property cycles affect values, as the market-definition article advises for defining the market.

**What do the properties earn?** Occupancy, rent, and lease terms drive the cash flow, which the financial-statement article explains for how income is reported, as the free-cash-flow article requires for the cash actually generated.

**How is it financed?** Leverage, maturity dates, and interest costs decide what happens when rates rise or properties fall in value, which the debt-restructuring article treats as a covenant and refinancing question, as the fixed-income article explains for how rate changes transmit.

**Is the distribution covered?** A high yield can reflect a falling share price rather than a healthy payout, which the dividend-policy article treats as a signal that should be interpreted carefully, as the P/E article explains for per-share measures.

Three consequences follow. The metrics need a consistent period and definition, which the data-governance article requires for relied-upon figures. The yield should be read against the payout and the cash flow, as [Dividend Policy and Share Buybacks](/articles/dividend-policy-and-share-buybacks) advises. And the result should be recorded with the assumptions, as the decision-memo article requires.

Three checks decide whether the yield is attractive. Second, leverage and maturity: if a large share of debt refinances within two years at higher rates, interest cost will rise, which the debt-restructuring article explains for covenants and maturity. Third, property trend: if occupancy is falling in the REIT's main market, the income base is weakening, which the real-estate article treats as a cycle signal.
