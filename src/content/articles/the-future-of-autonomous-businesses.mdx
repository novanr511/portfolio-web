<Lead>
An autonomous business sounds like a company that runs itself. The realistic version is narrower and more useful: specific business processes in which software observes a state, decides an action, and executes it with limited human involvement. The difficulty is not making a single step autonomous — it is making a chain of steps reliable enough that the whole process can be trusted.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** Autonomous processes are built from agents that interleave reasoning and tool use. Yao et al.'s ReAct work describes a "reason–act–observe" pattern in which a model reasons about a task, calls a tool, and uses the observation to continue. The boundary is mathematical: if each step has a 95% success rate, a ten-step chain succeeds roughly 60% of the time.

<KeyPoint>
Autonomy is therefore a reliability budget, not a switch.
</KeyPoint>

## How an agent loop works

Three components form the loop:

**Reason.** The model interprets the goal and the current state, and decides what to do next. This is where ambiguity is resolved — or misinterpreted.

**Act.** The model calls a tool: a database query, an API request, a calculator, a file write. The tool is the model's only way to affect the world.

**Observe.** The result is fed back into the context, and the loop repeats until the goal is met or a stop condition fires.

ReAct's significance is that it <Mark>makes the loop explicit and grounded in observations</Mark> rather than pure generation. Later agent frameworks add state, branching, checkpoints, and human approval gates, but the basic structure remains.

For a business process, the loop maps onto a workflow: read the customer request, check the system of record, decide an action, execute it, verify the result. The interesting questions are what happens when a step fails and who is accountable for the outcome.

## A worked example of error propagation

The following numbers are hypothetical and illustrative.

Suppose an autonomous invoicing process has five steps: extract the invoice data, match it to a purchase order, check the budget, create the payment record, and notify the vendor. Each step is 95% reliable when considered independently.

- Probability the whole chain succeeds: 0.95^5 ≈ **77%**.
- With ten steps: 0.95^10 ≈ **60%**.
- To reach 95% end-to-end over ten steps, each step needs about 99.5% reliability: 0.995^10 ≈ 95%.

Three design responses follow:

1. **Shorten the chain.** Combine steps that do not need separate decisions, or remove confirmations that add a failure point without adding information.
2. **Add verification.** A step that checks the output of the previous step can convert a silent error into a detected one. Detection is not correction, but it enables a safe fallback.
3. **Introduce human gates at irreversible points.** Sending a payment is irreversible; drafting a message is not. <Mark>Put approval before the irreversible action, not before every step</Mark>.

The arithmetic also explains why demonstrations are misleading.

<Trap>
A demo that succeeds once tells you very little about a ten-step process that must run a thousand times a day.
</Trap>

The relevant measurement is <Mark>the end-to-end success rate and the distribution of failure modes</Mark>, not the capability shown in a single trace.

## What adoption data tells us — and does not

Bick, Blandin, and Deming's NBER paper reports that 23% of employed respondents had used generative AI for work at least once in the previous week as of late 2024, and 9% used it every workday. That is evidence of diffusion into work, not evidence of autonomous business processes.

Three distinctions matter:

**Assistance is not autonomy.** A person using a tool to draft an email is assisted. A process that sends the email without review is autonomous. The adoption figures largely describe the former.

**Capability is not deployment.** A model that can perform a task in a controlled demonstration may not do so reliably in a messy business environment with incomplete data and changing rules.

**Efficiency is not the whole case.** A process can be autonomous and still be a bad idea if errors are expensive and accountability is unclear.

The OECD AI policy work exists because these questions are not purely technical: trustworthy deployment involves standards, governance, and human oversight. Forecasts about autonomous businesses should be labelled as scenarios with stated assumptions, not treated as predictions of a timeline.

## Where autonomy is reasonable

Three conditions make a process a good candidate:

**The action is reversible.** Sending a reminder can be undone; sending a payment cannot. Start with reversible processes.

**The cost of an error is bounded and measurable.** If a mistake costs a known, small amount, the reliability arithmetic can be turned into a business decision.

**The system can detect its own failure.** A process that cannot tell whether it succeeded needs human monitoring, which defeats the purpose.

The counterargument — that humans make errors too — is fair and often used too loosely. Human operators also have an advantage: they can notice a novel situation and escalate. An autonomous process must be designed with that capability explicitly, or it will fail confidently in situations its training did not cover.

## The economics of an autonomous step

Autonomy has a cost that is easy to overlook: the infrastructure, monitoring, and exception handling around the model. A worked comparison makes it visible.

Suppose a human costs 40 per hour and processes 20 invoices per hour — 2 per invoice. An autonomous system costs 5,000 to build, 300 a month to run, and handles 90% of invoices without intervention. The remaining 10% still require a human at 2 each. At 5,000 invoices a month:

- Human-only cost: 5,000 × 2 = 10,000 per month.
- Autonomous cost: 300 + (500 × 2) = 1,300 per month, plus the 5,000 build cost.
- Payback: roughly 0.6 months of the 8,700 monthly saving.

The result looks overwhelming, and that is exactly why the arithmetic needs stress-testing:

1. **Error cost is missing.** If 2% of automated invoices contain an error that costs 50 to repair, that is 5,000 × 0.02 × 50 = 5,000 a month — erasing more than half the gain.
2. **Monitoring is not free.** Someone must review the exception queue and the failure statistics. If that takes 20 hours a month, add 800.
3. **Volume changes the answer.** At 500 invoices a month, human cost is 1,000 and the autonomous system costs 300 + 100 + monitoring — the fixed costs dominate and the case weakens.

<PullQuote>
The point is that autonomy is a capital investment with ongoing operating costs, not a deletion of labour cost.
</PullQuote>

The reliability arithmetic and the cost arithmetic must be done together.

## What the future framing cannot support

Three limits on any claim about autonomous businesses:

**Reliability does not compose.** The worked example showed that high per-step reliability still produces a mediocre end-to-end result. There is no shortcut around this.

**Accountability cannot be automated.** A decision that harms a customer still needs an accountable party. "The system did it" is not an answer that satisfies regulators or affected people.

**The environment changes.** A process that works today may face new inputs, new rules, or an adversary studying it. Autonomy requires monitoring and a defined stop condition, which is ongoing work rather than a one-time implementation.

## A decision test

1. **Map the process as a chain of steps** and count them.
2. **Compute end-to-end reliability** from per-step rates, as in the worked example.
3. **Place human gates at irreversible actions**, not arbitrarily.
4. **Require failure detection** at each step that can fail silently.
5. **Start with reversible, bounded-error processes.**
6. **Measure the full chain** in production, not single traces in a demo.
7. **Name the accountable owner** and the stop condition before deployment.
