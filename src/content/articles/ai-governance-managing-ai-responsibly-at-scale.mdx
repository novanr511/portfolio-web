AI governance is not about stopping AI. It is about steering it.

**Direct answer:** AI governance is the framework of policies, roles, and processes that ensures AI systems are developed and deployed responsibly. It defines who decides what AI can do, how risks are managed, how compliance is demonstrated, and who is accountable when things go wrong. Governance becomes essential as AI scales from experiments to core business operations.

## What AI governance covers

AI governance answers:

- **Approval:** which AI use cases are allowed, and which require review?
- **Risk:** what could go wrong, and how is it monitored?
- **Accountability:** who owns each AI system, from development to retirement?
- **Compliance:** how are regulations, standards, and internal policies met?
- **Data:** what data can be used, and under what conditions?
- **Transparency:** how are AI decisions explained to users and regulators?

## Why governance scales with AI

A single chatbot experiment needs little governance. A company using AI for hiring, credit scoring, fraud detection, and customer support needs significant governance. The more AI touches customers, money, and people, the more governance matters.

Without governance, scaling AI means scaling risk: biased decisions, privacy violations, security failures, and regulatory exposure all grow with usage.

## The governance structure

Effective AI governance includes:

- **AI owners:** accountable for specific models or systems, responsible for performance and risk.
- **AI review boards:** cross-functional committees (legal, privacy, security, business) that review high-risk use cases.
- **Risk assessments:** documented evaluation of bias, safety, security, and compliance before deployment.
- **Monitoring:** ongoing tracking of model performance, drift, and real-world impact.
- **Audit trails:** records of what models were deployed, when, by whom, and with what data.

## Risk-based governance

Not all AI needs the same level of governance:

- **Low risk:** internal productivity tools, marketing copy generation. Lightweight review.
- **Medium risk:** customer-facing recommendations, fraud detection. Regular monitoring and periodic review.
- **High risk:** hiring decisions, credit scoring, medical diagnosis, legal analysis. Strict review, human oversight, and regulatory compliance.

The governance effort should match the potential harm.

## Data and model accountability

Governance extends to the inputs and outputs:

- **Data provenance.** Where did training data come from? Was consent obtained?
- **Model versioning.** Which version is in production? What changed from the last version?
- **Performance baselines.** What is acceptable accuracy, and how is it measured?
- **Rollback plans.** How is a model removed if it causes harm?

## Compliance and regulation

AI regulation is evolving worldwide. Governance helps organizations prepare:

- **Transparency requirements.** Informing users when they interact with AI.
- **Bias testing.** Demonstrating that systems do not discriminate.
- **Human oversight.** Ensuring humans remain in the loop for high-stakes decisions.
- **Record keeping.** Being able to show regulators how decisions were made.

Compliance is the minimum. Ethical governance goes beyond the law.

## A practical starting point

1. **Inventory AI systems.** Know what models are in use, where, and by whom.
2. **Classify risk.** Categorize each system by potential harm.
3. **Assign owners.** Name the person accountable for each system.
4. **Set review criteria.** Define which use cases need board-level review.
5. **Monitor and report.** Track performance, incidents, and compliance metrics.

Governance starts with visibility. You cannot govern what you do not know exists.
