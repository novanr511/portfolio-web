Marketing teams have more data than ever and often make the same decisions as before, because the data arrives in a form that cannot change the choice. The dashboard reports what happened; the decision needs to know what would have happened otherwise. The gap between those two is where most marketing analytics fails.

**Direct answer:** Data changes a marketing decision when it is timely, tied to a counterfactual, and attached to a specific choice with an owner. The three instruments that matter are experiments for interventions that can be randomised, geo experiments for those that cannot, and attribution models that are understood as models rather than measurements. Google's own documentation is unusually honest about this: "Attribution models let you choose how much credit each ad interaction gets for your conversions" — a choice, not an observed fact.

## From reporting to deciding

The default form of marketing analytics is a report: spend by channel, conversions by channel, cost per acquisition, trend lines. It is descriptive and it is useful for detecting that something changed, but it cannot answer "should we do more of this?" because it has no counterfactual.

The shift to a decision instrument happens with one addition: a statement of what would have happened otherwise. For an intervention that can be assigned at random, that is an experiment. For one that cannot, the alternative is a design that constructs a comparison at the level where the intervention operates.

Google's published work on geo experiments states that "one approach that Google has successfully employed to measure advertising effectiveness is geo experiments", and describes them as having "a systematic and effective design process" with results that are "easy to interpret". The value of the approach is not the geography as such; it is that it creates a counterfactual for a decision — a market-wide campaign — that user-level randomisation cannot.

## Attribution is a choice, and the choice has consequences

When teams talk about "data-driven marketing", they often mean a multi-touch attribution model. Google Ads describes the mechanics plainly: "Attribution models let you choose how much credit each ad interaction gets for your conversions", and notes that actions using deprecated models have been "upgraded to use data-driven attribution", with the last-click model still supported.

That language matters. A model that gives 30% of the credit to the first touch and 70% to the last is not describing a fact about the world; it is applying a rule. Two teams using different models can look at the same journey and reach opposite conclusions about which channel deserves the next pound, and both will say "the data shows".

The way out is not to find the true model. It is to stop asking attribution to answer the incremental question and to use experiments for that. Attribution is useful for description and for allocating credit inside a reporting framework; it is not a substitute for knowing what would have happened if the spend had not occurred.

## The data has to arrive in time

A second failure mode is temporal. A report that tells you a campaign was ineffective after the quarter has closed is not a decision input; it is a post-mortem.

The design question is the delay between the event being measurable and the decision it should inform. Long-cycle purchases make this hard, and the honest response is to shorten the measurement loop where you can — leading indicators that are cheap to measure, tested against the eventual outcome — rather than to wait and hope.

Survey-based market research has a parallel discipline worth borrowing. The American Association for Public Opinion Research publishes standards and ethics for research, and the principle that transfers is that a claim should record who was asked, how they were selected, and what the sample can support. Marketing data deserves the same disclosure, and it rarely gets it.

## Instrument the decision, not the channel

What should a marketing team actually record? The answer follows from the decisions it makes repeatedly:

- **Which offer was shown to which person in which context.** Without this, no experiment can be reconstructed.
- **What the outcome was, including downstream value** — the kept order, the retained customer, the margin — not only the immediate conversion.
- **The pre-experiment behaviour** needed for variance reduction. CUPED "utilizes data from the pre-experiment period to reduce metric variability and hence achieve better sensitivity", with a reported variance reduction of about 50% on Bing. If you cannot tie pre-period behaviour to the same user, you cannot use the method.
- **The decision that was made on the basis of the data**, so you can later check whether the recommendation was followed and what happened.

The last item is the one almost nobody keeps, and it is the only way to learn whether your analytics function changes outcomes or merely produces documents.

## A checklist for the next decision

1. **Name the decision and its owner** before you request the analysis.
2. **Ask what the counterfactual is.** If nobody can say, the report cannot answer the question.
3. **Choose the instrument**: randomisation for user-level interventions, geo experiments for market-level ones, attribution for description only.
4. **State the model's assumptions out loud.** "We used last-click" is a choice that another team should be able to challenge.
5. **Check the timing.** Will the answer arrive before the decision?
6. **Record the outcome and the decision**, including when they disagreed.
7. **Review the archive quarterly.** A list of decisions and their actual results is the only marketing dataset that improves over time.
