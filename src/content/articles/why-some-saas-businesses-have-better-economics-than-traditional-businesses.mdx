SaaS is not just software on the internet. It is a different economic engine.

**Direct answer:** SaaS businesses have better economics than many traditional businesses because of recurring revenue, low marginal costs per customer, and scalability. Once software is built, serving an additional customer costs little. But SaaS economics are not automatically profitable: high customer acquisition cost, churn, and service obligations can destroy value. The model is attractive because of its unit economics potential, not because of the label.

## The SaaS economic engine

**Recurring revenue.** Subscriptions create predictable cash flow. Customers pay monthly or annually, and revenue compounds as the base grows. This predictability makes planning, hiring, and fundraising easier than in transactional businesses.

**Low marginal cost.** The cost to serve one more customer is mostly server and support time—not manufacturing, shipping, or inventory. As the customer base grows, fixed development costs spread across more users.

**Scalability.** SaaS can add thousands of customers without proportional cost increases. The same codebase serves all customers. This is impossible for businesses that deliver physical products or bespoke services.

**Higher LTV.** A customer paying $100/month for three years is worth $3,600, compared to a one-time $500 sale. Higher lifetime value justifies higher acquisition spending.

## Where SaaS economics break down

**CAC can exceed LTV.** If it costs $2,000 to acquire a customer who pays $50/month and stays 12 months ($600 LTV), the business loses money on every customer. Many SaaS companies discover this too late.

**Churn kills.** A 5% monthly churn rate means replacing half the customer base every year just to stay flat. Growth becomes a treadmill, not an engine.

**Service obligations never end.** SaaS customers expect uptime, updates, and support indefinitely. The cost of serving does not go to zero after the sale.

**Sales cycles can be long.** Enterprise SaaS sales can take 6–18 months, with no revenue during the cycle.

## The metrics that determine survival

- **LTV:CAC ratio.** How much value a customer creates versus what it costs to acquire them. Below 1:1 destroys value; 3:1 is commonly cited as healthy but context-dependent.
- **Monthly churn.** The percentage of customers who cancel. This determines how fast you must replace lost revenue.
- **Payback period.** How many months of margin it takes to recover acquisition cost. Longer payback requires more capital.
- **Net revenue retention.** Whether existing customers expand (upgrade, buy more) faster than they churn. Above 100% means the business grows without new customers.

## When SaaS fits and when it does not

SaaS fits when:

- The product delivers ongoing value.
- Customers have a recurring need.
- The marginal cost of serving is low.
- The market is large enough to support acquisition costs.

SaaS does not fit when:

- The problem is solved once (a tool used once is not a subscription).
- Customers resist recurring payments.
- Service costs scale linearly with users.
- The market is too small for the CAC.
