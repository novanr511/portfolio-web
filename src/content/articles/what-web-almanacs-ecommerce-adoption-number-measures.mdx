The Web Almanac’s 2025 e-commerce chapter says it detected e-commerce on 19.9% of analyzed desktop sites and 19.2% of analyzed mobile sites. Those figures are easy to paraphrase as “one in five websites sells online.” That is broader than what the study measured. The chapter identifies sites through platform and commerce signals in a specific web dataset; it is not a census of businesses, retailers, transactions, or online sales.

**Direct answer:** The 19.9% desktop and 19.2% mobile figures are HTTP Archive’s detection rates for e-commerce sites within its 2025 analyzed site set. The chapter says detection depends on Wappalyzer identifying an e-commerce platform or another strong signal. The underlying site set comes from Chrome field-data coverage and is therefore shaped by which sites appear in that ecosystem. Use the percentages to describe this dataset and method—not the share of all businesses or retail sales that are online. [The Web Almanac chapter](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/ecommerce) documents both the findings and limitations.

## The unit is detected sites, not sales

The chapter’s headline is about the percentage of sites identified as e-commerce sites. It is not a measure of gross merchandise value, order volume, business revenue, or consumer adoption. One large store and one small store each count as a site in an unweighted site-level prevalence statistic, even though their commercial scale may be very different.

That unit also differs from the U.S. Census Bureau’s quarterly retail e-commerce estimate. The Census measure estimates dollars of retail sales meeting its e-commerce definition as a share of total U.S. retail sales. The Web Almanac estimates the prevalence of detected e-commerce sites in a web sample. The two percentages answer different questions and should not be compared as if they shared a numerator, denominator, geography, or method. For the sales measure, see [What E-commerce’s Share of Retail Sales Measures](/articles/what-ecommerce-share-of-retail-sales-measures).

## Detection rules shape the result

HTTP Archive says it can identify an e-commerce site when Wappalyzer detects an e-commerce platform or a strong e-commerce signal. Detecting only a payment processor is not sufficient, because non-store websites may also accept payments.

This is a classification rule, and classification can miss cases. A bespoke store might not expose a known platform signature; conversely, a detected signal is evidence used by the classifier, not a direct audit of the organization’s commercial activity. The chapter states the rule so readers can understand that the result is a detected prevalence estimate, not a verified business registry.

The chapter also notes that the dataset is drawn from Chrome’s field-data ecosystem and thus reflects Chrome users’ visited sites. The authors additionally say that the site set is biased toward sites visited by Chrome users. The implication is not that the result is unusable; it is that its scope must remain attached when interpreting or citing it.

## Why rank segments tell a different story

The chapter reports that detected e-commerce adoption varies by site popularity. Its figure indicates roughly 1% among the top 1,000 sites and around 22% desktop / 21% mobile in the top 10 million. These are rates within different rank-based groups, not a statement that less popular sites collectively produce a greater volume of online sales.

The segment pattern can reflect the composition of each group as well as adoption. It does not show that a site becomes more likely to add e-commerce merely by becoming less popular; the analysis is descriptive, not a causal experiment. Nor should an overall figure be averaged from the segment rates without knowing each group’s size and the chapter’s weighting procedure.

When using rank comparisons, keep the rank boundary and device type in the sentence. “About one in five sites in the top-10-million group was detected as e-commerce” is meaningfully different from “one in five websites is an online store.”

## How to cite the finding responsibly

Before quoting the chapter, note:

1. **Edition:** Web Almanac 2025, published January 2026.
2. **Population:** sites in HTTP Archive’s analyzed set, drawn from Chrome field-data coverage.
3. **Detection:** Wappalyzer platform or strong e-commerce signals; payment processor alone is insufficient.
4. **Unit:** percentage of sites classified, not share of sales, orders, or businesses.
5. **Segment:** desktop/mobile and any popularity tier named in the claim.
6. **Limit:** detection and sample coverage affect which sites are counted.

The chapter is useful for studying web implementation patterns: platform adoption, performance measurements, and differences across site segments. Its estimates should not be used to answer a national retail-sales question. Conversely, a government sales survey cannot explain what e-commerce platforms or technologies websites deploy.
