Customer Lifetime Value answers: how much is one customer worth?

**Direct answer:** Customer Lifetime Value (LTV) is the estimated net profit a business expects to earn from a single customer over the entire relationship. It guides how much can be spent to acquire a customer (CAC) while remaining profitable. LTV is an estimate built on assumptions—average revenue, gross margin, retention, and lifespan—not a guarantee. Used with CAC, it determines whether a business model is sustainable.

## What LTV measures

LTV answers: if I acquire one customer today, how much profit will they generate before they stop buying?

A simple calculation:

LTV = Average Revenue per Customer × Gross Margin × Expected Customer Lifespan

Expected lifespan is derived from churn: if monthly churn is 5%, expected lifespan is roughly 1 ÷ 0.05 = 20 months.

Example: A subscription business charges $50/month with 80% gross margin and 5% monthly churn:

LTV = $50 × 0.80 × 20 = $800

This means the average customer is expected to generate $800 in gross profit before churning.

## Why LTV matters

LTV sets the ceiling on acquisition spending. If LTV is $800 and CAC is $200, the business can afford to spend up to $800 to acquire a customer and still break even. The LTV:CAC ratio (commonly cited around 3:1 as healthy, though context-dependent) shows whether acquisition is efficient.

Without LTV, acquisition spending is guesswork. A company might spend $500 to acquire a customer worth $400—and not know it until cash runs out.

## What LTV is not

LTV is not:

- **Revenue.** It is profit, after costs.
- **Certain.** It depends on assumptions about future behavior.
- **Static.** It changes as pricing, product, and retention change.
- **The same for all customers.** Enterprise customers and self-serve customers have different LTVs.

Cohort-based LTV (calculated by customer segment or acquisition period) is more accurate than a single blended number.

## The assumptions that matter

LTV is sensitive to inputs:

- **Churn.** A small change in churn dramatically changes lifespan and LTV.
- **Margin.** Higher margin means more profit per dollar of revenue.
- **Revenue per customer.** Upsells and cross-sells increase LTV.
- **Time horizon.** A 3-year LTV differs from a 5-year LTV.

Overestimating lifespan or understating churn makes LTV look better than reality—a common source of bad acquisition decisions.

## LTV and CAC together

LTV alone is incomplete. It must be compared with CAC:

- **LTV:CAC < 1:** each customer destroys value. The business model does not work.
- **LTV:CAC = 1:** each customer breaks even. No profit from acquisition.
- **LTV:CAC > 1:** each customer creates value. The business can scale acquisition.

The ratio is a starting point, not a rule. Businesses with high fixed costs may need higher ratios; businesses with network effects can operate with lower ones.

## Payback period

LTV tells the total value. Payback period tells how quickly it arrives: how many months of margin it takes to recover CAC. A shorter payback reduces capital needs and risk.
