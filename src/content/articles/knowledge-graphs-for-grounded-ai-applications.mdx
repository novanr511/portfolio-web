Vector retrieval finds passages that look similar to a query. It does not know that a particular person reports to a particular manager, or that one drug contraindicates another. When an application needs relationships rather than similarity, a knowledge graph can supply structure that embeddings cannot.

**Direct answer:** A knowledge graph stores entities and the relations between them as triples, allowing queries that traverse connections rather than rank passages. The knowledge-graph overview provides the structural reference, and the RAG article covers the retrieval pattern it can complement. The boundary: a graph is only as good as its data — building and maintaining the entities and relations is the real cost, and many applications need both a graph and vector retrieval.

## Structure versus similarity

Three differences matter:

**Explicit relations.** A graph states that A works for B. A vector index can find documents mentioning both, but it does not assert the relationship.

**Traversal.** You can ask "which projects report to this director through two levels of management?" — a question that is awkward to answer by similarity search.

**Provenance.** Each edge can carry a source and a date, which makes the answer traceable. That matters in regulated domains, as the governance article explains.

The complement is important: a graph is poor at open-ended similarity, and vectors are poor at exact relationships. Many systems use both.

## A worked example of a graph query

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A company stores an organisational graph: people, teams, projects, and the relations "reports to", "member of", and "owns". A user asks: "Which projects are owned by teams whose lead reports to the CTO?"

A vector system retrieves documents mentioning the CTO and projects, then asks the model to infer the chain. It may return a plausible but incomplete list.

A graph system traverses: find people whose "reports to" is the CTO; find teams they lead; find projects those teams own. The result is exact given the graph, and each edge can be cited.

Three checks decide whether the result is trustworthy:

1. **Completeness.** Are all relevant reporting relationships in the graph? A missing edge produces a missing project with no warning.
2. **Freshness.** A reorganisation last week may not be reflected. Edges need dates and an update process, as the data-governance article explains.
3. **Entity resolution.** Two records for the same person produce two partial answers. Deduplication is a graph problem before it is an AI problem.

Three decisions follow:

- **Use the graph for exact relationships**, not for general document search.
- **Invest in entity resolution and provenance** before adding clever query logic.
- **Combine with vector retrieval** for questions that mix structure and text.

## When a graph pays

**Multi-hop questions** that traverse several relationships.

**Regulated domains** where the source of each claim must be shown.

**Stable schemas** where the entity types and relations are known in advance.

## When it does not

**Unstructured exploration** where the reader does not know what to ask.

**Rapidly changing domains** where edges go stale faster than they can be maintained.

**Small corpora** where a passage search is sufficient and a graph is overhead.

The counterargument — that a model can infer relationships from text — is true for simple cases and unreliable for multi-hop ones. A model that has to hold several relations in working memory can make mistakes that a traversal would not.

## What a graph cannot do

**It cannot be complete by accident.** Every entity and edge must be sourced and maintained, which is why graph projects often fail on data work rather than query logic.

**It cannot answer without a schema.** Someone must decide what an entity and a relation are, which is a modelling choice that can be wrong.

**It cannot replace text.** The graph tells you a project is owned by a team; the documents tell you why. Both are usually needed, as the RAG article explains.

## A decision test

1. **Identify the questions that need relationships**, not similarity.
2. **List the entity types and relations** required to answer them.
3. **Check the data exists** and can be kept current, with an owner.
4. **Resolve entities** before adding edges.
5. **Store provenance** on each edge.
6. **Combine with vector retrieval** for mixed questions.
7. **Measure answer correctness** on real queries before expanding the schema.
