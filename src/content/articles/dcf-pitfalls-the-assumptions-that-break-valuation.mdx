<Lead>
A discounted cash flow model converts a set of assumptions into a single number, <Mark>which makes it look more authoritative than it is</Mark>. The arithmetic is mechanical; the assumptions are judgements about an uncertain future. Most DCF failures are not errors in the formulas but weaknesses in the inputs and in the story they encode.
</Lead>

**Direct answer:** The main pitfalls are inconsistent cash flows and discount rates, an unrealistic forecast horizon, a terminal value that dominates the result, and a growth assumption that exceeds the economy in the long run. Damodaran's valuation materials treat these as explicit choices, and the cost-of-capital article explains the discount rate. The boundary: a DCF is <Mark>a conditional estimate, not a prediction</Mark>, and the result should be reported as a range with the drivers stated, as the sensitivity article recommends.

## Four failure points

**Inconsistent basis.** Nominal cash flows must be discounted at a nominal rate; real cash flows at a real rate, as the real-interest-rates article explains.

<Trap>
Mixing them produces an answer that looks precise and is structurally wrong, which the financial-modeling article treats as a definitional error.
</Trap>

**Forecast horizon.** A five-year forecast is an assumption about how long the company has a distinguishable future. Too short and the terminal value carries everything; too long and the explicit forecast becomes fiction, as the valuation article argues for a horizon tied to the business's competitive position.

**Terminal value dominance.** If most of the value comes from the terminal calculation, the model is really a statement about perpetual growth, which the sensitivity article recommends testing explicitly. A small change in the terminal growth rate then moves the entire result.

**Impossible growth.** A company cannot grow faster than the economy forever, which the macroeconomics article treats as an arithmetic constraint.

<KeyPoint>
A terminal growth rate above the long-run nominal growth rate is a signal to revisit the model, not a detail to defend.
</KeyPoint>

## A worked example

The following is hypothetical and illustrative. A DCF produces a value of 200 million using ten years of forecast cash flows and a terminal growth rate of 4%. The discount rate is 9%.

Three checks decide whether to trust it. First, what share of value comes from the terminal calculation? If it is 75%, the model depends mostly on the 4% assumption, which the sensitivity article recommends ranking as dominant. Second, is 4% sustainable in perpetuity? If long-run nominal growth is around 3%, the assumption implies the company eventually becomes the economy, which the macroeconomics article treats as impossible. Third, is the discount rate consistent with the cash flows, as [Cost of Capital for Private Companies: A Practical Estimate](/articles/cost-of-capital-private-companies) requires? A nominal 9% against real cash flows is a structural error, as the real-interest-rates article explains.

Three conclusions follow. The terminal growth rate should fall to a sustainable level, which reduces the value materially. The result should be reported as a range, as the valuation article recommends. And the assumptions should be recorded with sources, as the financial-modeling article requires for any relied-upon model.

## What a DCF cannot do

It cannot value a company with no predictable cash flows, which the startup-valuation article treats as a reason to use other methods at an early stage. It cannot remove uncertainty about the future, which investor education states explicitly. And it cannot decide strategy: a high value does not make an acquisition right if it does not fit the business, which the strategy article argues for positioning before analysis.

<PullQuote>
The counterargument that DCF is too uncertain to use is too strong.
</PullQuote>

It is uncertain, which is why <Mark>the assumptions should be explicit and the result a range</Mark>, as the sensitivity article recommends.

## A decision test

1. Match the cash-flow and discount-rate basis, as the real-interest-rates article requires.
2. Set a horizon tied to the competitive position, as the valuation article advises.
3. Check the share of value from the terminal calculation.
4. Test terminal growth against long-run nominal growth, as the macroeconomics article explains.
5. Verify the discount rate with the cost-of-capital framework.
6. Report a range with the dominant drivers, as the sensitivity article recommends.
7. Record the assumptions and sources, as the financial-modeling article requires.

For the discount rate, see [Cost of Capital for Private Companies](/articles/cost-of-capital-private-companies); for the method, see [How to Value a Public Company](/articles/how-to-value-a-public-company-three-methods).
