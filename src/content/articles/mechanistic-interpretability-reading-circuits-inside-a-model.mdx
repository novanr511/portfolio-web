A model card can tell you where a system was evaluated and what it is intended for. It cannot tell you *why* the model produced a particular answer. Mechanistic interpretability aims to open the black box and describe the computations inside — not just the outputs — with the ambition of predicting and controlling behaviour from the mechanism.

<KeyPoint>
Mechanistic interpretability studies the internal components of a neural network — individual neurons, attention heads, and the circuits they form — to explain how a computation is performed.

## What a circuit is

A circuit is a set of components and connections that implements a particular function. The analogy to electronic circuits is deliberate: the goal is to identify a pathway — these heads detect a pattern, these neurons carry it forward, this component writes the result — that can be described independently of the rest of the network.

Three properties make the goal difficult:

**Superposition.** The toy-model paper shows that a network can store more features than it has dimensions by representing them in overlapping directions. A single neuron may respond to several unrelated features, so reading one unit does not reveal one concept.

**Polysemanticity.** For the same reason, a component's apparent meaning depends on context.

<Trap>
A neat one-neuron-one-idea story is usually wrong.
</Trap>

**Scale.** A small model may be tractable to analyse; a frontier model has billions of parameters and many layers of interaction.

## What the field has found

**Features can be decomposed.** Sparse autoencoder work has identified directions in activation space that correspond to more interpretable features than individual neurons. This is a method for finding a useful basis, not proof that the model uses those features explicitly.

**Circuits can be partially reverse-engineered.** Specific behaviours — such as copying a token or completing a pattern — have been traced through small models, showing how attention heads and feed-forward components cooperate.

**Interventions can test hypotheses.** If a proposed circuit is correct, ablating it should change the behaviour in the predicted way. This is <Mark>the causal test that separates an explanation from a story</Mark>.

## A worked example of an interpretability claim

The following is hypothetical and illustrative, based on the method rather than a measured result.

A researcher proposes that a particular attention head in a small model copies the previous token. Three tests decide whether the claim holds:

1. **Activation pattern.** When the input contains repeated tokens, does the head's attention concentrate on the previous position? This is correlational.
2. **Ablation.** If the head is disabled, does the model lose the ability to copy previous tokens while other behaviours remain intact? This is a causal test.
3. **Generalisability.** Does the pattern hold across different prompts and token types, or only in the example used to find it? A single example is a hypothesis, not an explanation.

Three outcomes are possible:

- All three support the claim, which becomes a credible description of that head's role in that model.
- Ablation has no effect, which means the head was correlated but not necessary — other components compensate.
- The pattern appears only in one context, which means the proposed circuit is incomplete.

Three lessons follow:

- **Correlation is not explanation.** Activation patterns alone are suggestive, not causal.
- **Redundancy is common.** Removing one component may do nothing because others take over, which complicates ablation studies.
- **Scope matters.** An explanation of a small model's behaviour does not automatically transfer to a larger one.

## What interpretability cannot promise

**It is not the same as model cards.** Documentation reports intended use and evaluation conditions; interpretability examines internal computation. Both are useful and they answer different questions, as the model-card paper explains.

**It does not establish safety.** Understanding a mechanism does not guarantee that behaviour is desirable, just as understanding a car engine does not make a driver safe.

**It does not yet scale.** A convincing analysis of a small model is not a method for a frontier system. The field is honest about this limitation, which is why it is often framed as research rather than a production control.

The counterargument — that black-box evaluation is enough — is fair for many applications.

<PullQuote>
Interpretability becomes valuable when you need to predict behaviour in novel situations or when a high-stakes decision demands more than a statistical test.
</PullQuote>

## A decision test

1. **Name the specific behaviour** you want to explain.
2. **Form a mechanistic hypothesis** about which components are involved.
3. **Test with activation analysis**, then with causal intervention.
4. **Check generalisation** across prompts and contexts.
5. **Account for redundancy** — null effects may mean compensation, not irrelevance.
6. **State the model and scale** the claim applies to.
7. **Treat the result as explanation**, not a safety guarantee.

For the documentation standard, see [Model Cards for Model Reporting](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.03993); for the safety frame, see [AI Safety](/articles/ai-safety-alignment-robustness-and-governance).
