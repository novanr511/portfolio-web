## The mechanism

The first function is recording. The POS entry describes transactions at the point of sale. A record that is wrong — a wrong quantity, a missed discount — propagates into every report that uses it, which is why <Mark>accuracy matters more than a fast interface</Mark>.

<Figure src="/images/articles/inline/pos-systems-for-beginners-1.png" alt="A flow from defining the decisions, to checking what the system records, verifying inventory updates, then testing the reports." caption="Conceptual: a POS earns its place through accurate records and usable reports." />

The second function is inventory. The inventory entry treats stock as cash. A sale that does not update stock leaves the system describing a reality that no longer exists, and the error grows with each transaction.

The third function is data for decisions. A system can record a sale without making it easy to answer which products sold or when. The reports a business actually needs should be part of the evaluation, not <Mark>an afterthought once the system is installed</Mark>.

The sequence is: define the decisions, check what the system records, verify inventory updates, then test the reports. Each step is local work, so the conclusions are `inferred`; the functions are `source-reported`.

## A worked example

*Illustrative.* A shop considers a new system that speeds up checkout.

Three checks decide. First, does it record the fields the business needs, including returns and discounts? Second, does it update inventory at the moment of sale, with a clear rule for returns? Third, can the owner answer a simple question — which products sold last week — from the system without exporting data?

The outcome is a decision about the system's data, not just its speed. The scenario is hypothetical; the method is the contribution.

## Why speed is not the whole result

A faster checkout improves the customer's wait, but a system that records poorly creates costs across inventory, accounting, and decisions.

<Trap>
The two are different outcomes, and a choice based only on the first will be judged later on the second.
</Trap>

A system cannot fix undisciplined processes. If staff routinely override prices or skip scanning, the data it records will be wrong regardless of the software, and the business will make decisions from a record that does not describe reality. Training and checks are part of the system in practice.

The beginner should also recognise that a system chosen for a single store may not scale to several. Multi-location inventory, consistent pricing, and consolidated reporting introduce requirements that a small installation does not test. Asking about the next stage before buying avoids a replacement sooner than expected.

The data should also be reviewed on a schedule. A field that was unused at launch can become important later, and a report that nobody reads at the start may become the one the business depends on. Revisiting the configuration prevents the system from quietly falling behind the work.

The data a system records is a long-term asset, and a decision based only on checkout speed undervalues it.

The inventory record should be checked against a physical count, since a system that agrees with itself can still be wrong.

<PullQuote>
A system should be chosen for the data it will still need in a year, not only for today's checkout.
</PullQuote>

## What it cannot do

It cannot fix a weak product range. It does not guarantee accurate stock without disciplined use. And it cannot replace a decision about what to measure.

The counterargument that checkout speed is what customers notice is fair for experience and incomplete for the business data that follows.

## A decision test

1. Define the decisions the data supports.
2. Check the recorded fields.
3. Verify inventory updates.
4. Test the reports.
5. Check returns handling.
6. Record the decision and recheck date.
