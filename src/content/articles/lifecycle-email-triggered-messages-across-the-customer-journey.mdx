Lifecycle email is often described as a series of automated messages — welcome, onboarding, renewal, win-back. That description misses the design work: each message is triggered by an event and should stop when its purpose is achieved. A programme that keeps sending because the sequence says so damages the relationship it is meant to build.

**Direct answer:** Design lifecycle email as triggered messages tied to customer events with explicit entry conditions, exit conditions, and a metric per message. Gmail's sender guidelines govern deliverability as volume rises, and demand-generation principles explain which decision each message serves. The boundary: automation removes manual work, not judgement — a message that fires on the wrong event or continues after the outcome is achieved is a cost, as the marketing-automation article explains.

## The messages and their triggers

**Welcome** — triggered by signup, with the job of delivering the promised value and setting expectations.

**Onboarding** — triggered by a milestone or its absence, helping the customer reach the first meaningful outcome, as the customer-success article describes for activation.

**Re-engagement** — triggered by inactivity, asking once whether the subscriber wants to continue and then stopping.

**Renewal or replenishment** — triggered by a known date or usage pattern, which makes it the highest-value lifecycle message for many businesses.

**Win-back** — triggered by lapse, with specific evidence of improvement rather than a generic discount, as the retention article argues.

Each needs three definitions: the event that starts it, the event that stops it, and the outcome it is meant to change.

## A worked sequence

The following is hypothetical and illustrative.

A SaaS product designs an onboarding sequence for new signups:

- **Day 0:** deliver the promised resource and state what the next emails will cover.
- **Day 3:** a worked example if the user has not completed the first project.
- **Day 7:** a single question asking what the user is trying to achieve.

Three exit conditions: the user completes a project, unsubscribes, or replies. Three checks decide whether the sequence works:

1. **Cohort comparison** — new signups in the month after launch versus before, with the same acquisition sources, as the newsletter article recommends for net growth.
2. **Guardrails** — complaint and unsubscribe rates, which the sender guidelines make central to deliverability.
3. **Engagement by step** — are later messages opening, or are they noise that the sequence sends because the schedule says so, as the email-metrics article explains.

Three conclusions follow:

- **The sequence stops on success**, which prevents over-messaging, as the marketing-automation article describes.
- **The metric is the meaningful action**, not opens, which the email-metrics article argues.
- **The reply at day seven is research**, feeding the voice-of-customer work, as the customer-journey article recommends for evidence.

## Auditing a trigger

A trigger can be correct in definition and wrong in implementation. Three checks catch the common problems:

**Test the boundary cases.** What happens when a user signs up and completes the first action in the same session? A sequence that sends "you haven't started" the next day looks careless, which the marketing-automation article warns about as a failure of exit conditions.

**Check the stop logic against real events.** An exit condition that depends on an event the tracking system sometimes misses will leave people in a sequence after they have succeeded, which the monitoring article covers as instrumentation drift.

**Read a sample of sent messages.** A monthly sample of what actually went out catches content that has gone stale — a renamed feature, an outdated policy — which the brand-consistency article treats as a governance issue.

These checks are unglamorous and more valuable than adding more messages to the sequence, which the email-metrics article argues for measuring outcomes over volume.

## What lifecycle email cannot do

**It cannot fix a weak product.** A sequence that pushes users toward an outcome the product cannot deliver increases churn, which the retention article covers.

**It cannot substitute for consent.** Sending because an event fired is not the same as having permission, which the data-privacy article explains.

**It is not set-and-forget.** Triggers and content need review as the product and audience change, which the monitoring article covers as a change trigger.

The counterargument — that more messages mean more revenue — ignores the cost of fatigue, which the deliverability article treats as a leading indicator of list decay.

## A decision test

1. **Define the trigger event** for each message.
2. **Define the exit condition** — success, unsubscribe, or reply.
3. **State the outcome metric** with a time window, as the email-metrics article recommends.
4. **Track complaints and unsubscribes** as guardrails.
5. **Compare cohorts**, not raw opens, as the newsletter article explains.
6. **Review triggers after product changes**, which the monitoring article covers.
7. **Assign an owner** for the programme, as the governance checklist requires.
