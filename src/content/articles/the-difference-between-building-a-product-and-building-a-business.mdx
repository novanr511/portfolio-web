Teams often treat product quality and business viability as the same goal viewed from different angles. They are not. A product can be delightful, technically excellent, and used regularly while the organisation around it runs out of money. The reason is that the two activities answer different questions: whether something is worth using, and whether the company can survive providing it.

**Direct answer:** Building a product asks whether a solution works for a user and solves a real problem. Building a business asks whether value can be created, delivered, and captured repeatedly at a sustainable cost. Porter's *What Is Strategy?* is the right frame for the second: "operational effectiveness is not strategy", and a distinctive position must be supported by a fit of activities. The boundary: product work remains necessary — a business with no product has nothing to sell — but it does not answer the business question.

## Two different scorecards

A product scorecard includes usability, reliability, retention of use, and whether users accomplish the task. A business scorecard includes acquisition cost, conversion, revenue, gross margin, retention of *paying* customers, and cash runway.

The metrics overlap in places but diverge in important ones:

- **A user is not a customer.** Free usage can be evidence of value and still be far from revenue.
- **Retention of use is not retention of payment.** A user who opens a tool daily may churn the moment a charge begins.
- **Quality is not willingness to pay.** A product can be better than alternatives and still serve a market too small or too price-sensitive to sustain a company.

This is why a team can report strong engagement and a worsening financial position without anyone lying. Both measurements are correct; they describe different systems.

## A worked example of the gap

The following numbers are hypothetical and illustrative.

A software product has 10,000 active users, 40% weekly retention, and a net promoter score that the team is pleased with. Only 500 are paying customers at 20 per month. Monthly gross revenue is 10,000.

- Monthly acquisition spend: 30,000, yielding 1,000 new signups, of which 5% convert to paid — 50 new customers.
- CAC per paying customer: 30,000 / 50 = 600.
- Gross margin after infrastructure and support: 80%, so contribution per paying customer is 16 per month.
- Payback period: 600 / 16 = 37.5 months.

A payback period of more than three years is a business problem irrespective of product quality. Three changes affect it:

1. **Improve conversion from signup to paid** from 5% to 10%. CAC halves to 300, payback to 18.75 months — the same product, the same spend, a different business.
2. **Raise price from 20 to 30.** If conversion holds, contribution becomes 26 per month and payback falls to about 23 months.
3. **Reduce acquisition spend** by shifting to a channel with lower cost but fewer signups. This may improve payback while slowing growth — a different trade-off, not a free win.

Notice that none of these is a product feature. The team could ship a better product and leave the payback problem untouched. Conversely, improving conversion without improving the product may boost a metric while making the offering less useful — which is why the two scorecards must be read together.

Two cautions: gross margin here excludes fixed costs, and a 37.5-month payback may be acceptable in some enterprise models with contractual commitments. The number is a signal to investigate, not a universal verdict.

## Where product thinking helps the business

The distinction is not a reason to ignore product quality. Three ways product work directly improves business metrics:

**Activation.** A user who reaches the product's core value in the first session is more likely to become paying. This is a product-design problem with a revenue consequence.

**Retention.** Paying customers leave when the product stops solving the problem. Retention is the product's report card in the business scorecard.

**Reduced support cost.** A clearer interface lowers the cost to serve, which improves gross margin. Product quality is a cost structure decision as well as a user-experience one.

The productive framing is that product work creates the conditions in which business mechanisms can work — but it does not choose the mechanisms.

## Three ways of capturing value

The mechanism a business chooses shapes nearly every other decision, and the product alone does not determine it.

**Subscription** charges for continued access. It rewards retention and makes lifetime value the central metric. It also creates a continuous obligation to deliver value, and it exposes the company to churn at every renewal.

**Transaction fees** charge when a deal occurs. They align the company's revenue with the customer's success, which is attractive in marketplaces, but they require enough volume and a reliable way to observe the transaction.

**Advertising** charges a third party for attention. It can make a product free for users, but it rewards engagement rather than user outcomes, and the incentives can diverge sharply from the product's stated purpose.

A fourth category — one-time licensing — front-loads revenue and shifts the challenge to repeat purchases.

The product can be identical under two mechanisms, while the business is entirely different. A tool sold by subscription and the same tool funded by advertising will make different choices about notifications, data use, and which user behaviours to encourage. Porter's fit argument applies: the capture mechanism must fit the positioning, not merely be attached to it.

## Where the business question is ignored

Three patterns signal that a team is building a product without a business:

**Growth is described without unit economics.** "Users are growing 20% a month" is a fact about a number, not evidence of a viable model.

**The price was set once and never revisited.** Pricing is a hypothesis about value and a mechanism for capture. It should be tested as deliberately as a feature.

**The acquisition channel is an assumption.** A product that depends on a channel nobody has measured has a plan for the product but not for the business.

Porter's point about fit is the corrective. The channel, the price, and the product must be designed together: a self-serve product needs a channel that brings users to a self-serve moment, and a high-touch enterprise product needs a sales motion that can support its price.

## What success stories leave out

Survivorship and hindsight apply with force here. The companies celebrated for "just building a great product" are those for whom the business model worked. The many excellent products that failed to find a viable model are not written up. Amazon's company news is useful primary material for what that company says about its own choices; it is not evidence that the same choices would work elsewhere.

The counterargument that a remarkable product will find its business model is sometimes true and unfalsifiable in advance. A more useful stance is to treat the business model as a set of testable assumptions alongside the product assumptions, and to track both scorecards from the beginning.

## A decision test

1. **Keep two scorecards** — product value and business viability — and review them together.
2. **Distinguish users from paying customers** in every metric.
3. **Compute CAC and payback** with full costs and the same time period as revenue.
4. **Test pricing** as a hypothesis, not a one-time decision.
5. **Check the fit** between product, channel, and monetisation.
6. **Treat acquisition as a measured system**, not an assumption.
7. **Read success stories as hypotheses**, with the failed cases remembered.
