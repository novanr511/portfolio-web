Every recommender works by learning from past behaviour, and every system meets a moment when there is no past behaviour to learn from. A new user has no history; a new item has no interactions; a new platform has neither. The cold start is not an edge case in a catalogue that grows — it is the permanent condition at the boundary of the system.

**Direct answer:** There are three distinct cold starts — user, item, and system — and they require different responses. User cold start needs information about the person beyond their behaviour; item cold start needs information about the item beyond its interactions; system cold start needs a strategy that does not depend on personalisation at all. Content-based signals, side information, and a deliberate onboarding moment are the standard instruments, and each trades precision for coverage.

## The three cases

**New user.** Collaborative filtering cannot place a user in the interaction structure because there are no interactions. The typical response is to ask for something — explicit preferences, a few selections, or an implicit signal from the first session — and to use demographic or contextual information where it is available and acceptable.

**New item.** A product with no interactions cannot appear in a collaborative recommendation, no matter how good it is. This is the quieter and often more damaging case, because it means a catalogue's newest additions are systematically invisible, and the system becomes biased toward its own past.

**New system.** A brand-new platform has neither users nor interactions, and no amount of clever modelling creates them. The answer is editorial curation, popularity, and human-designed defaults until enough behaviour exists.

## Content is the bridge

The most general answer to both user and item cold start is side information — anything known about the entity that does not depend on interactions with it.

For items, that means the product description, category, price, image, text, or the structured attributes a catalogue already carries. A content-based model can recommend items similar to what the user liked even if nobody else has interacted with them, which is exactly what a collaborative model cannot do.

For users, it means the preferences they state, the context they arrive in, or a small set of choices they make at onboarding. The key is that the signal is collected *before* the system has to make a decision, which is why onboarding is a product decision rather than a formality.

The trade-off is that content-based methods are limited by the richness of the metadata. A sparse catalogue with generic descriptions produces generic recommendations, and they suffer from the "more of the same" problem — showing a user only variations of what they already consumed. Hybrid systems combine content and collaborative signals, and DeepFM's work on feature interactions is relevant to the ranking side: it is built to model "both low- and high-order feature interactions" from user behaviour and item attributes, which is one way of letting content signal into a ranking model.

## Embeddings help when content is unstructured

When the side information is text or images, pretrained embeddings turn it into a representation a model can use. Sentence-BERT produces sentence embeddings that "can be compared using cosine-similarity", which is the basis for recommending items by the semantic similarity of their descriptions. Word2vec-style embeddings do the same for shorter text and for sequences of behaviour.

This is not a cure. A pretrained embedding brings the model's own training distribution and biases into your catalogue, and a domain with specialised vocabulary will be represented poorly. The embeddings are a bridge over the cold start, not a source of truth about the item.

## Why the problem never goes away

A catalogue is a stream. New items arrive continuously, new users arrive continuously, and the system's cold-start population is therefore a steady-state property, not a phase it passes through.

Two consequences follow. First, cold-start handling has to be a permanent part of the architecture rather than a launch-time workaround. Second, the system needs a way to move entities *out* of the cold start — an exposure policy that gives new items a controlled chance to accumulate interactions, and a measurement of whether that exposure produced useful signal.

A catalogue that never exposes new items will converge on its past favourites, and the recommendation quality will appear excellent while the catalogue quietly becomes a museum. This is a distribution problem as much as a modelling one: without a deliberate route to exposure, new items have no chance to become recommendable.

## A strategy for each case

1. **User cold start:** collect a minimal, explicit signal at onboarding; use content-based recommendations until behaviour exists; measure how quickly a new user reaches useful recommendations.
2. **Item cold start:** ensure the catalogue carries descriptive metadata; use content-based similarity; reserve a small, measured share of exposure for new items.
3. **System cold start:** use editorial curation and popularity, and instrument the behaviour that will later feed the model.
4. **All three:** build a hybrid ranker that can combine content and collaborative signals, and validate it on the segments where one signal is absent.
5. **Measure the transition:** track how long it takes a new user or item to acquire enough signal to be served by the main model. That time is the real measure of your cold-start handling.
